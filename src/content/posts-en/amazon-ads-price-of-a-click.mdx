A merchant buying advertising on Amazon bids the amount they are willing to pay when a shopper clicks on their product. Amazon selects the ad and calculates the charge under its own pricing rules. Competing bids shape the outcome alongside parameters set by the platform.

On November 5, 2026, the US Federal Trade Commission drew renewed attention to allegations detailed in a less heavily redacted version of its complaint against Amazon. The FTC and 22 states accuse the company of raising certain advertising prices through undisclosed reserves while giving advertisers a misleading account of its auctions. Amazon disputes that interpretation. The company publicly describes its use of reserves and defends their role in pricing more relevant ads. The case remained pending in the sources reviewed as of November 8. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01">S01</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s02">S02</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03">S03</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05">S05</a></sup>

Understanding the case starts with the mechanics of a click. The ceiling an advertiser accepts can leave considerable room for the system that calculates the bill. That price then meets a more familiar constraint: the margin available on the product being sold.

## One placement, two decisions

Sponsored Products lets advertisers promote individual products and pay when someone clicks, a [cost-per-click](/en/glossary/cout-par-clic/) (CPC) model. The advertiser sets a bid and campaign parameters; Amazon determines which ad appears and how much a click costs. The platform’s transparency guide describes a ranking system that considers both the bid and the customer’s expected interest. A higher bid can therefore lose to an ad judged more relevant. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s06">S06</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s17">S17</a></sup>

The first decision awards a placement. The second determines the price of a click on the winning ad. The two decisions may use some of the same inputs, but they answer different questions. Ranking determines visibility; pricing divides the economic value of that visibility between the seller and the platform.

Keeping them separate avoids a common shortcut. Knowing that an ad won because of its relevance tells us something about selection. Understanding its bill also requires the payment rule. A system can favor an ad suited to the shopper and charge the merchant the full amount of its effective bid. Both outcomes can occur together.

In a theoretical auction for a single placement using a pure second-price rule, the winner pays the second-highest bid. This benchmark helps explain why some advertisers distinguish what they are willing to pay from what they expect to pay. Real advertising auctions add placements, quality criteria and reserves. Research on *generalized second-price* auctions shows that the properties of the elementary case do not automatically carry over to these more complex markets. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s14">S14</a></sup>

The public documents examined here allow us to explain these layers. They do not disclose a complete formula for Amazon’s ranking system, with its coefficients, competing bids and reserves for each transaction. The following model uses entirely fictional numbers to isolate a pricing lever described in the documents.

## A reserve that still lets the bid win

A hard reserve sets an entry threshold. A [soft reserve](/en/glossary/reserve-souple/) can influence the price while still allowing a bid below that reserve to win. Amazon distinguishes these functions in its public response: when the winning bid falls below the soft reserve but clears the hard reserve, the ad can receive the placement and pay its own bid. The complaint also describes a price capped at the winning bid. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05">S05</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01">S01, §63</a></sup>

Consider one placement and three equally relevant ads. Their bids, after any adjustments, are $2, $1.20 and $0.80. We set the entry threshold at $0.50. Ad A wins with its $2 bid. Under our pure second-price benchmark, its click costs $1.20, the amount of the second-highest bid.

Now introduce a soft reserve of $1.60. A still wins; our model raises its payment to $1.60. Raise the reserve again, to $2.20: A keeps the placement in this example and pays its own bid, or $2. The competing bids, relevance and winner have stayed the same. Changing the pricing rule alone shifts an additional $0.40, then $0.80 per click to the platform, relative to the $1.20 benchmark. The curve illustrates a pricing mechanism. It holds the price at the second-highest bid, raises it with the reserve, then stops it at the winning bid. This function belongs to the l0g model. Its purpose is to isolate an economic relationship; it does not claim to reproduce Amazon’s software or measure a real seller’s bills.

The ceiling therefore deserves close attention. Agreeing to pay up to $2 can be entirely consistent with expecting a price set under particular competitive rules. What that agreement means also depends on the information provided about the calculation. The legal dispute concerns, among other things, how the mechanism was presented and whether the alleged surcharges occurred. The existence of a cap is part of the evidence, but does not by itself settle those questions. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01">S01, §§299–314</a></sup>

## Two Decembers in the court record

The complaint makes the issue concrete through historical episodes. According to paragraph 228, a change implemented on December 10, 2021 allegedly produced a 20% increase in the average cost per click on Sponsored Products over the following five days. Some advertisers allegedly experienced increases exceeding 200%. The complaint attributes the change to a relaxation of the limits governing surcharges. That causal claim is the plaintiffs’. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01">S01, §228, p. 76</a></sup>

The time window matters: five days after December 10. Recasting the number as an annual increase, or an average for the entire holiday season, would change its meaning. It concerns an episode presented by the FTC using internal documents, for which we do not have the full underlying dataset.

The following year, the complaint says, the teams raised reserves more gradually. A memo cited in paragraphs 249 and 250 attributes more than $87 million in additional worldwide revenue during December 2022 to the operation. The figure is an alleged revenue increase. It has a global scope; it is neither advertising profit nor an amount awarded by a court. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01">S01, §§249–250, p. 82</a></sup>

The contrast between the two episodes supports the FTC’s theory: how prices were moved allegedly mattered as much as their level, with a gradual increase intended to provoke less resistance. Testing that interpretation requires putting the cited exchanges in context and reconstructing the auctions concerned. The complaint provides excerpts and an argument. The complete original emails and auction logs are absent from the public material assembled for this analysis.

The publication timeline also needs care. The action began on August 31, 2026. The 181-page version used here is docket document 113, filed on October 28. Figures repeated in the November 5 announcement are already visible in Exhibit A dated September 28, now accessible on the FTC’s website. We have not established exactly when those figures first became publicly available. The October 27 order keeps some passages under seal; it addresses their confidentiality. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01">S01</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s04">S04</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s16">S16</a></sup>

These dates explain how recent news can shed light on practices alleged to have occurred years earlier. They also make clear why a November announcement should not be read as a new judgment against Amazon.

## The bid ceiling can move

Even before the price is set, the bid can change. For an advertiser, the number entered in a field is sometimes the starting point for a series of authorized adjustments.

The official Sponsored Products help page, updated on September 28, 2026, provides a particularly clear example. A $1 base bid receives a 50% adjustment for the top of search results, taking it to $1.50. With the dynamic bids (up and down) strategy, it can then reach $3. The maximum illustrated calculation is 1 × 1.50 × 2. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s07">S07</a></sup>

The final step depends on the opportunity the system identifies. The $3 figure is a possible maximum adjusted bid in this official example. The price is calculated afterward; the illustration does not describe an automatic $3 charge.

Examining a cost therefore requires the base bid, placement adjustments and chosen bidding strategy. Comparing a click price with the initial bid alone can give an incomplete picture of the applicable ceiling. Conversely, finding that the click stayed below the adjusted bid tells us that the limit was respected, without explaining the rule that set the price within it.

This distinction gives the merchant something concrete to work with. The settings specify how much discretion the seller delegates to the system. Their economic value depends on the clicks obtained, and therefore on how often those clicks lead to sales and how much contribution those sales generate.

## Clicks and the seller’s margin

To trace the effect, keep the three prices from our first illustration. Imagine a product sold for $50 before tax. Variable costs excluding advertising (assumed to include the product, commissions, logistics and returns) are $35 per order. That leaves $15 of contribution before advertising. Every parameter is an illustrative assumption. [M02](#methode-m02)

One hundred clicks produce ten attributed orders, an attributed conversion rate of 10%. The corresponding sales are $500; variable costs excluding advertising total $350. The contribution available to pay for acquisition is therefore $150.

At $1.20 per click, the campaign costs $120 and leaves $30 of contribution. At $1.60, it costs $160, leaving a negative balance of $10. At $2, spending reaches $200 and the balance falls to minus $50. Clicks, orders and unit contribution have been held constant to isolate the effect of the advertising price.

Dashboard metrics tell the same story in a different form. [ACoS](/en/glossary/acos/), or *advertising cost of sales*, divides advertising expenditure by attributed sales. In our three cases, it is 24%, 32% and 40%. [ROAS](/en/glossary/roas/), *return on ad spend*, reverses the calculation: attributed sales divided by advertising expenditure. These measures describe revenue and acquisition spending; margin requires a further calculation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s08">S08</a></sup> [M02](#methode-m02)

In this example, contribution before advertising is 30% of the selling price. An ACoS of 30% consumes it entirely. The same threshold can be expressed as a $1.50 CPC: a ten-in-a-hundred chance of an order, multiplied by $15 of contribution. The result after advertising remains a contribution measure on attributed sales. Fixed costs, taxes and any future effects are outside the model.

The calculation also tests the strongest economic argument for a more expensive click. Better conversion can offset its price. With the same $15 of unit contribution, a $1.60 CPC requires an attributed conversion rate of approximately 10.67% to break even on this basis, compared with 8% at $1.20. A useful comparison therefore brings together price, traffic quality and margin. A rise in CPC alone cannot establish a campaign’s profitability. [M02](#methode-m02)

## The orders advertising actually adds

A difficulty remains even when every dashboard calculation is correct. An attributed sale is a sale linked to an advertising interaction under a measurement rule. That rule records a customer journey. To determine advertising’s contribution, we also need to estimate what would have happened without it. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s09">S09</a></sup>

Return to the first scenario: $120 spent, ten attributed orders and $500 in sales. ROAS is approximately 4.17. Now suppose that only six orders are additional, and that the other four would have occurred without the campaign. This 60% share is chosen for the demonstration. It does not come from an Amazon measurement. [M02](#methode-m02)

Attributed contribution after advertising remains $30: ten orders at $15, less $120 in spending. Incremental contribution falls to minus $30: six orders at $15, less the same $120. The same commercial dashboard can therefore accompany very different economic conclusions, depending on how many sales the advertising actually caused.

<AmazonAuctionFigure lang="en" kind="incrementality" number="02" caption="Fictional assumptions: ten attributed orders, of which six are caused by the campaign.">

100 clicks, $120 of spending, $15 of contribution per order before advertising. Attributed contribution is 150 − 120 = $30. With an assumed 60% share of additional orders, incremental contribution is 90 − 120 = −$30. Fixed costs and taxes are excluded.

</AmazonAuctionFigure>

This additional effect is often called [*incrementality*](/en/glossary/incrementalite/). Here it reduces the break-even CPC to $0.90: a 10% attributed conversion rate × a 60% share of additional orders × $15 of contribution. The equation exposes the weakness of a threshold that assumes all attributed sales would disappear if the campaign stopped.

To estimate a causal effect, a properly designed experiment uses random assignment to create comparable groups, with and without the exposure being studied. Research by Gordon, Zettelmeyer, Bhargava and Chapsky shows why observational methods can produce results that differ from experiments in advertising measurement. Their study concerns Facebook. It offers a methodological lesson, not an estimate of the incrementality of Amazon ads. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s15">S15</a></sup>

Even an experiment requires care. The duration must be defined, delayed purchases considered and contamination between groups checked. Simply stopping a campaign during a weak week mixes the effect of advertising with changes in demand. We deliberately leave these complications outside our model. In a real decision, they can change the result.

Access to this information changes the seller’s economic bargaining position. A merchant with a better understanding of the incremental contribution of its campaigns can decide how much to bid for traffic. The auction operator still has a different view: it sees the allocation of placements and the billing rules. Performance measures alone do not reconstruct that mechanism.

## Amazon’s defense and the right comparison

Amazon argues that its reserves support a more relevant ranking system. In its August 31 response, the company cites an inflation-adjusted average CPC that was stable between 2019 and 2024, and an improvement in conversion rates of more than 24% between 2021 and 2025 for individual Sponsored Products advertisers. These series come from Amazon. They cover different periods, and this investigation found no public independent confirmation of their underlying data. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05">S05</a></sup>

On the information provided to customers, Amazon says its console had described the bid as a maximum since 2018. The company characterizes the materials cited against it as outdated or simplified and says they were subsequently corrected. It also argues that advertisers adjust their bids in response to observed results. These arguments bear directly on the allegation of deception. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05">S05</a></sup>

The substance of the argument deserves examination. A platform can improve the match between products and shoppers, generate more sales per click and provide a more useful service. A seller may then accept higher spending while retaining more contribution. The preceding model shows exactly how that offset works.

The comparison must still fit the question. To measure the value of better ranking, compare selection rules. To isolate a reserve’s effect on price, hold ranking, traffic quality and competing bids as constant as possible, then change the pricing rule. Changing all these elements at once prevents the result from being attributed to a single mechanism.

A stable average CPC over several years can thus coexist with a price higher than it would have been without certain reserves. It can also conceal different trends across campaigns. Conversely, reconstructing a lower theoretical price does not by itself establish the full alleged harm: advertiser behavior, the information they received and the validity of the comparison scenario must also be considered.

The complaint and Amazon’s response offer two readings of the same market. The task of examining the documents is to preserve the questions their figures actually answer. Combining performance metrics, counterfactual differences and allegations of deception would create a false common measure.

## A $68.6 billion business

The financial scale helps explain the stakes. Amazon’s global *Advertising services* revenue rose from $31.160 billion in 2021 to $68.635 billion in 2025, in current dollars. The published accounts show the same revenue category and an identical 2023 figure in the two reports used to join the series. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s12">S12, p. 69</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13">S13, p. 69</a></sup>

The category covers several formats, including sponsored ads, display and video. It extends beyond the search auctions discussed in this article. Revenue growth may reflect more placements, clicks or impressions, or a different mix of formats, as well as changes in price. The series measures the scale of the business; it does not isolate additional revenue attributable to reserves.

Results published on October 29 put advertising revenue at $19.637 billion in the third quarter of 2026, compared with $17.703 billion a year earlier. Amazon reported growth of 11% in current dollars and 12% excluding foreign exchange effects. The calendar complicates the comparison: Prime Day moved to the second quarter in most large countries in 2026, whereas the event fell entirely in the third quarter in 2025. The quantified adjustment provided for group sales is not a published adjustment for Ads. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s10">S10</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s11">S11</a></sup>

These revenues do not reveal an operating margin for advertising either. Amazon reports operating results for its North America, International and AWS segments. Subtracting the group’s selling and marketing expenses from advertising revenue alone would combine mismatched accounting categories. The accounts let us measure revenue while leaving part of the business’s internal economics undisclosed. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s11">S11</a></sup>

## The possible effect on shoppers’ prices

The economic chain can extend beyond the seller. If acquiring an additional order becomes more expensive, a merchant can accept lower contribution, cut back some campaigns, change its product range or seek to raise prices. The choice depends on competition, customer responses and other sales channels. These are possible transmission mechanisms whose strength needs to be measured.

The evidence assembled here establishes no rate of pass-through from these reserves to retail prices. Calculating one would require linking identified advertising-cost changes to products and their prices, while separating other changes in costs and demand. Higher advertising costs can squeeze margin without immediately reaching the consumer; better sales can also cushion the pressure.

The issue also matters to merchants outside the United States buying visibility on Amazon. The material assembled here does not, however, allow us to quantify the consequences for those merchants. A figure for advertising auctions in France would not by itself measure the exposure of French businesses: where an ad is shown is distinct from the seller’s nationality. The accounting revenues presented are global. The litigation is a US proceeding.

## The price within the ceiling

Amazon publicly describes the existence of reserves. How they were presented to advertisers, their effects and whether the alleged practices were deceptive remain central to the litigation. The court decisions reviewed concern matters including procedure and confidentiality; they do not decide liability on the merits. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03">S03</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s04">S04</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05">S05</a></sup>

Further progress would depend on the rules actually applied in each period, the versions of the information received by advertisers and data allowing a comparable scenario to be reconstructed. Assessing the value of a click would also require measures of conversion, contribution and incrementality appropriate to the seller concerned.

This investigation rests on a practical distinction. The bid sets an amount the merchant authorizes the system to spend. The auction rules determine how much of that amount is charged. Margin and the sales actually added determine what the expenditure delivers for the seller. Understanding these three steps makes it possible to examine a promise of performance with the right questions, while identifying precisely which questions the litigation must address.
