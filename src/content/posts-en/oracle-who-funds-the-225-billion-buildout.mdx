A customer can help fund a data centre before running a single workload there. It pays for future services upfront, gives the cloud provider time to install the equipment, then draws down the capacity it has purchased. That change in timing sits at the centre of Oracle’s financing plan: build heavily now while limiting the additional money raised in capital markets.

On October 28, 2026, Oracle outlined a path to **at least $225 billion in revenue in fiscal 2030**. It expects to raise another $20 billion in capital markets to support the plan, excluding refinancing of existing debt. Customer prepayments, supplier financing and customer-provided equipment are meant to reduce the initial cash requirement. The latest accounts already show the importance of prepayments. They reveal less about how all the payments will fit together between now and 2030. [1](#source-1) [4](#source-4)

## The revenue target is older than the financing promise

The $225 billion target appeared in Oracle’s 2025 financial presentation. The October 2026 announcement adds a more explicit funding timetable: capital spending peaks in fiscal 2027 and 2028, free cash flow turns positive in the second half of fiscal 2028, and reaches at least $30 billion in fiscal 2029. Oracle’s published non-GAAP definition of [free cash flow](/en/glossary/fcf/) is operating cash flow less capital expenditures. [1](#source-1) [2](#source-2) [3](#source-3)

Those fiscal labels need translating into calendar dates. Oracle’s financial year ends on May 31. The second half of fiscal 2028 therefore runs from **December 2027 through May 2028**. Fiscal 2030 covers June 2029 to May 2030. Timing matters when equipment has to be ordered before the resulting capacity can earn revenue. [1](#source-1) [5](#source-5)

The starting point is established: revenue of $67.357 billion in the year ended May 2026. Reaching exactly $225 billion four fiscal years later would require compound annual growth of roughly **35.2%**, calculated by l0g. That average measures the distance between the endpoints; it says nothing about the exact route. We have not carried forward the intervening annual forecasts from the 2025 presentation as though they were updated 2026 guidance. [1](#source-1) [2](#source-2) [5](#source-5)

## Another $20 billion from capital markets

Oracle currently expects the additional funding to comprise **$10 billion of equity and $10 billion of gross debt**. This comes after the $20 billion stock offering completed in the first quarter of fiscal 2027. Net proceeds from that offering, after issuance costs, were $19.909 billion. Refinancing existing debt is expressly excluded from the new funding commitment. [1](#source-1) [4](#source-4)

Replacing a maturing bond can require a substantial new issue without providing equivalent additional money for construction. Conversely, a supplier that allows payment after delivery can fund a project without Oracle issuing a bond. The commitment concerns the additional capital-market funding identified in the plan, with an important exclusion. Understanding it also requires following obligations created elsewhere.

Equity has its own economic cost. Issuing shares provides money with no scheduled principal repayment, while spreading future earnings across more shares. The first-quarter offering forms part of the plan’s starting position. Adding it to the prospective $20 billion can describe those two successive funding rounds. It would not measure the total cost of the infrastructure, which draws on other resources and generates payments across different periods.

## Three ways to change who pays first

**Customer prepayments** move cash receipts closer to the construction date. Oracle receives money and remains responsible for delivering the contracted services. Revenue is subsequently recognised as performance occurs. Cash for the prepaid portion has already arrived; delivering it will subsequently use equipment, electricity and staff.

**Supplier financing** pushes out a payment. Equipment can become available before its purchase price has been settled in full. The repayment schedule then matters: cash preserved during construction can become a payment obligation once the first facilities are operating. Interest, collateral and contractual protections determine the cost of that flexibility.

**Customer-provided hardware** changes the division of investment more fundamentally. Oracle does not have to advance the purchase price of equipment supplied by its customer. It must still fund and operate the remaining parts of the service. The customer, meanwhile, carries the cost and contractual risks associated with its hardware. Revenue and margins under that arrangement cannot be compared mechanically with a service in which Oracle supplies everything. Oracle identifies all three mechanisms; their individual future shares remain to be documented. Its June announcement had already combined prepaid services and customer-supplied hardware, bringing together resources with different economic characteristics. [6](#source-6) [1](#source-1)

<OracleFigure id="02" lang="en" />

There is a credible economic rationale. A large customer reserving scarce capacity may be willing to provide money or equipment to secure delivery. The cloud provider lowers its initial financing requirement. Whether the arrangement remains attractive depends on how service delivery matches the obligations still to be paid. None of these mechanisms, considered alone, establishes how much cash will be available in 2029.

## Oracle’s illustrative funding bridge

The official 28 October slides go further than the release. Oracle illustrates roughly **6 gigawatts** of remaining construction and **$220 billion of capital spending** from Q2 fiscal 2027 through Q4 fiscal 2028. The calculation excludes 850 megawatts already delivered in Q1. Here $220 billion measures spending; the $225 billion revenue ambition refers to annual sales in fiscal 2030. [9](#source-9)

Oracle attributes an approximately **50% reduction in upfront cash requirements** in this illustration to the three mechanisms combined: vendor financing, customer hardware and prepayments. Other cash flows, after other needs, then complete the bridge to $20 billion of remaining capital raising. The individual bars carry no dollar figures, so their heights cannot establish separate contributions for prepayments or supplier credit. [9](#source-9)

The slide sets out a funding rationale built on illustrative investment per gigawatt. Equipment payment dates and the delivery of prepaid services still need tracking. Receiving money earlier and reducing upfront spending helps initial liquidity; service economics must subsequently support the remaining obligations.

## Nearly half the quarter’s operating cash came from identified prepayments

The accounts for **June 1 to August 31, 2026** put numbers on the mechanism. Oracle reported $23.103 billion in operating cash flow. Within that total, a separate line identifies **$11.363 billion of customer prepayments with a significant financing component**. Dividing one by the other gives approximately **49.2%**. [4](#source-4)

The accounting terminology is revealing. A sufficiently long gap between payment and delivery can make financing a substantive part of the transaction. Oracle says it recognises the associated interest expense separately from revenue; the expense was still immaterial in this quarter. Customers are providing a resource that finances the interval before the service is performed. The operating cash-flow classification needs to be read alongside that explanation. [4](#source-4)

[Capital expenditures](/en/glossary/capex/) were $28.499 billion, against operating cash flow of $23.103 billion. The difference produces Oracle’s published free cash flow: **negative $5.396 billion**. Mechanically subtracting just the identified prepayment line, with everything else unchanged, would produce negative $16.759 billion. This isolates the contribution of prepayments with other flows held constant. An actual loss of prepayments could prompt changes in investment or financing, which a full forecast would have to model. [3](#source-3) [4](#source-4)

<OracleFigure id="03" lang="en" />

New contracts can keep supplying prepayments as earlier contracts are fulfilled. The potential pressure point comes when delivery of prepaid services consumes resources while fresh advances slow. Assessing that risk requires the prepayment schedule, the pace of service delivery and the expected flow of new business. Multiplying one quarter’s $11.363 billion by four would conceal those timing questions rather than answer them.

## One customer dollar must only appear once

Oracle also publishes a supplemental measure called **net cash outlay for capital expenditures**. It subtracts the identified prepayments from capex and incorporates certain short-term financing cash flows involving manufacturing partners. For the quarter, the measure comes to **$17.966 billion**. [3](#source-3)

It addresses a useful question: how much of the investment outlay remains to be funded after those contributions and repayments? But a reader must use consistent definitions on both sides of the calculation. Subtracting $17.966 billion from reported operating cash flow of $23.103 billion would count the benefit of the same prepayments twice. They already increase operating cash flow and also reduce net cash outlay. The apparent positive $5.137 billion would be an inconsistent calculation. **Oracle does not report that figure as free cash flow.** [3](#source-3)

The consistent alternative also removes the advances from operating cash flow. It compares $11.740 billion with $17.966 billion, leaving **negative $6.226 billion**. This is more negative than published free cash flow because it includes $830 million of net repayments of short-term financing related to capex, recorded in financing activities. It is not the overall movement in cash: dividends, debt transactions, share issuance and other cash flows still have to be considered separately. [3](#source-3) [4](#source-4)

<OracleFigure id="04" lang="en" />

This reconciliation will matter when reading future results. Supplemental measures can provide useful information while still requiring care when combined. Nor should every historical financing arrangement with manufacturers be assumed to have the same terms as the supplier-financing strategy in the long-term plan.

## Customer contracts meet fifteen to nineteen years of rent

At August 31, Oracle had **$664 billion in [remaining performance obligations](/en/glossary/rpo/)**, or RPO: contracted services still to be recognised as revenue. It expected to recognise approximately 13% over the following twelve months, 37% in months thirteen through thirty-six, and 34% in months thirty-seven through sixty. The remaining roughly 16% falls beyond that window. [4](#source-4)

The first twelve months imply approximately $86 billion of revenue recognition by arithmetic. That window runs from September 2026 through August 2027, whereas fiscal 2027 ends in May. Comparing it directly with the $90 billion annual revenue target would mix different periods. Cash has another timetable again. Some services have already been prepaid, and the costs of performing them still need to be met. [1](#source-1) [4](#source-4)

Property commitments stretch much further. The same quarterly filing identifies **$288 billion of additional lease commitments** that have not yet commenced, almost entirely for data centres. Commencement is generally expected between the second quarter of fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2029, with **terms of fifteen to nineteen years**. Those commitments were not yet included in balance-sheet lease liabilities or in the maturity table for leases already under way. [4](#source-4)

<OracleFigure id="05" lang="en" />

The $288 billion represents future commitments spread over time. It is not a debt payable immediately, and should not simply be added to liabilities already measured at present value. Yet the future rent is an economic obligation. Aggregate contracted revenue across all customers cannot establish whether each building will remain occupied and profitable for its full lease term.

Oracle’s own annual report identifies the mismatch. Lease terms, renewal options and pricing adjustments typically do not match the duration and pricing of customer contracts. It also describes the risk of being left with multiyear commitments after demand has been overestimated or a major customer fails to pay. Prepayments mitigate exposure, while leaving a need to assess counterparties and contractual protections. [5](#source-5)

## Read the leverage definition all the way through

Oracle targets a ratio of net debt to adjusted EBITDA below 3.5 in fiscal 2029. EBITDA starts from earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, but adjustments can change its meaning materially. The release’s footnote includes borrowings, supplier financing and both operating and finance lease liabilities in net debt, then deducts cash and marketable securities. The denominator is based on Oracle’s non-GAAP results, adding back depreciation and operating lease expense. [1](#source-1)

That numerator is broader than bonds less cash. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that rents are entirely absent from the announced leverage measure. Equally, the adjusted earnings used to assess leverage add back operating lease expense. The ratio does not directly measure cash left after paying rent and replacing equipment.

A sound assessment would follow the same definition over time alongside actual payment schedules. Two businesses can report similar numerical leverage ratios while facing very different maturities, guarantees and replacement costs.

## The favourable case has a real operating foundation

Questions about financing should not obscure revenue already earned. Oracle’s cloud infrastructure business recorded **$7.388 billion in first-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue**, compared with $3.347 billion a year earlier, an increase of roughly 121% in reported dollars. These are recognised revenues over comparable quarters, distinct from the order book awaiting execution. [3](#source-3)

Contracted demand can provide the visibility needed to plan investment. Advances bring in cash. Customer-owned equipment reduces purchases that Oracle has to fund. Supplier credit can move payment closer to the receipts generated by the assets. If facilities are delivered on time, used as expected and operated profitably, new capital-market financing can indeed be substantially smaller than gross investment expenditure.

As an installed fleet begins generating revenue, lower expansion spending can release significant free cash flow. That is the economic mechanism behind the announced trajectory. It remains exposed to power costs, operating expenditure, deferred payments and equipment replacement. A revenue target alone determines neither a margin nor the cash that will ultimately be available.

The counter-test matters as well. Lower capex can reflect more efficient financing, the completion of a construction cycle or slower expansion. Distinguishing those explanations requires looking at new capacity, revenue and remaining payment obligations together. A single spending chart would leave too much out.

## The plan now has to meet real payment dates

The accounts let us reconstruct the reported quarter; the slides now provide an illustrative funding bridge. Independently testing whether $20 billion of additional raising will suffice through May 2030 would still require a consolidated timetable for new prepayments, service delivery, supplier credit and project payments. Those details would make Oracle’s assumptions testable across periods. [9](#source-9)

A convincing test would reconcile those flows with cash receipts, operating expenditure, capex, rent, debt service and shareholder distributions. It would also isolate the refinancing excluded from the headline commitment. The risks interact. Construction delays may defer revenue but also postpone some purchases. Cutting investment may preserve cash while reducing future capacity. Simply adding independent shocks together could produce a dramatic but economically inconsistent stress test.

The accompanying interactive tool therefore performs a narrower, reproducible exercise. It changes quarterly prepayments and capex, explicitly holds other flows constant, and reconciles two consistent ways of viewing the cash result. It explains the mechanism without pretending to forecast Oracle’s entire financial position through 2030.

**Customer funding makes the plan more plausible while shifting part of the analysis towards contract quality and delivery schedules.** The decisive question is how much cash remains when prepaid services must be delivered and deferred obligations fall due. The $20 billion commitment is a funding objective to track. Subsequent filings will need to show how Oracle is progressing towards it.
