Reports of restrictions in Ukraine this week raise a more specific question than whether a satellite network went down: who can change the permitted uses of a connection after an armed force has come to depend on it? Public evidence still does not establish who made the decision. It does explain why buying connectivity, owning a terminal and controlling service continuity are different forms of control.

## The number that changes the story

One hundred miles per hour, roughly 160 kilometres per hour. In the reports examined by US senators on 15 October, that figure describes how fast a terminal is moving. Internet throughput and message transmission time measure different properties. The same document mentions other reported thresholds, so the available observations do not establish a single rule operating uniformly across the network. [S06](#source-s06)

The distinction matters. A throughput cap allows less information to pass each second. A movement-based access rule can disable service when a terminal falls outside its permitted operating conditions, while other terminals remain connected. Under that second arrangement, aggregate traffic might look relatively reassuring even if particular users experienced a serious loss of service. That is a possible consequence of the permission model, rather than a measurement of what happened in Ukraine.

Starlink’s public commercial documentation describes an alert triggered when a terminal moves faster than its service policy permits and service is disabled. This establishes that the network can make access conditional on use. It does not disclose the settings of the government service supplied to Ukraine or identify who might have requested a change. [S10](#source-s10)

The language carries political weight. A “slowdown” suggests pages taking longer to load. A “Starlink shutdown” suggests that every connection disappeared. An investigation must preserve the distinction between affected uses, observed symptoms and the still-unknown scale of the incident. The public documents reviewed for this article do not include the technical logs needed to quantify that scale.

## A second network governs permission

SpaceX describes Starshield as a government offering built on Starlink technology, encompassing communications, Earth observation and hosted payloads. The name therefore covers more than an enhanced internet subscription. Available evidence about Ukrainian communications does not describe every government programme associated with the brand. [S09](#source-s09)

Understanding the risk requires following two paths. One carries information through user equipment, the space segment, ground infrastructure and destination services. The other governs whether that transport is allowed: contracts, service administration, customer entitlements and the rules implemented by the operator. A failure along either path can leave a user without service, although responsibility and possible remedies may be quite different.

Owning the terminal gives control of a local device. It does not automatically confer administrative power over a distant network. Paying for a subscription buys rights defined by an agreement; those rights may depend on an intermediary purchaser, government approval or an authorised use. Being the beneficiary country does not necessarily make a government the authority entitled to order changes. Beneficiary, payer, contracting customer and service administrator may be different organisations.

That separation is why “Musk or Washington?” is too narrow a question when assessing the safeguards a customer needs. The investigation must establish who could decide, who had to approve, who could execute and who had to notify affected users. Several functions may sit in one organisation, or a decision may travel through several of them. The diagram below illustrates this general distinction. It does not purport to reveal Ukraine’s actual network routing or contractual chain.

## Restoration accounts and contested responsibility

On 15 October, the Kyiv Independent reported a restoration, qualified by uncertainty over its completeness. Reuters that day attributed the restriction order to the White House, citing unnamed officials. On 16 October, Donald Trump denied responsibility, said he did not think Musk was behind the restrictions and said the United States had not imposed them. [S01](#source-s01) [S02](#source-s02) [S03](#source-s03)

Reuters still reported frontline difficulties and Volodymyr Zelensky’s assessment of incomplete restoration on 16 October. On 17 October, UNN relayed remarks from Ukraine’s chargé d’affaires Denys Senik, given in an interview on Friday 16: he was still awaiting confirmation from the Pentagon and SpaceX despite improvements. These accounts are not comparable network measurements. [S04](#source-s04) [S08](#source-s08)

It would therefore be premature to treat “restored” as the end of the incident. Recovery may apply to particular accounts, functions or observation periods. Unless the scope of each statement is known, improvement and continuing disruption can coexist. Their apparent contradiction does not, on its own, establish that one account is false or that another represents the experience of the entire country.

Responsibility remains contested as well. Reuters’ attribution relies on anonymous sources; the presidential denial comes from a party whose conduct is in question. Neither replaces a documented instruction linked to an execution record. Musk has described Starshield as controlled by the US government. That interested-party statement is not an ownership and governance audit of every asset associated with Starlink or Starshield. [S02](#source-s02) [S03](#source-s03) [S07](#source-s07)

The Telegraph published a more specific account on 16 October: citing four anonymous sources, it attributed a proposal to restrict the service to Musk and reported Trump’s agreement. That allegation conflicts with the denials. It adds another account without a published order or accessible technical record that could settle responsibility. [S27](#source-s27)

## Switching costs are also measured in time

The economic problem begins when a connection becomes an input into other investments. Organisations adapt equipment, procedures and skills around an ability to communicate. Part of the value of those investments then depends on retaining that ability. If access conditions change, buying another subscription may not restore the same useful service.

Consider a civilian emergency agency that has equipped its response centres for a particular network. A competing service may exist, yet require different terminals, [interoperability](/en/glossary/interoperabilite/) testing, a new support arrangement and different access rights. This analytical example makes no claim about specific Ukrainian equipment. It illustrates why the price of a competing offer is not a complete measure of the cost of switching.

Time becomes an economic resource. A solution available in six months may provide meaningful leverage in a contract negotiation while offering little help during an interruption today. The supplier or authority controlling the integrated service then holds an additional advantage: its customer cannot immediately turn a theoretical alternative into actual continuity. That advantage exists even without an intention to exercise coercive pressure.

This is the problem of relationship-specific investment. Spending creates its full value within a particular arrangement and cannot be redeployed elsewhere on equal terms. Reducing the resulting dependence takes preparation before a dispute or failure occurs. The same logic applies to cloud computing, identity software and payment infrastructure. In each case, an exit option should be evaluated against the service it can preserve, rather than the number of alternative contracts held.

Prepared [reversibility](/en/glossary/reversibilite/) requires more than a standby supplier named in a presentation. An organisation must fund usable capacity, keep it compatible and establish that it can actually bring the service into use. The institutional summary of the United States’ 2024 commercial space integration strategy stresses preparation and exercises before a crisis. Commercial capability and preparation for its limits belong within the same policy. [S24](#source-s24) [S25](#source-s25)

## Encryption and continuity address different risks

A message can be protected against interception and still be impossible to send. Information security distinguishes confidentiality, integrity and [service availability](/en/glossary/disponibilite-de-service/). Strengthening the first does not establish the third. Cryptographic protection, contractual obligations and continuity arrangements address different risks. [S11](#source-s11)

This distinction also prevents every outage from being labelled a cyberattack. The European Union attributed the 24 February 2022 KA-SAT attack to Russia and described disruption beyond Ukraine. That documented precedent involved a hostile cyber operation. It does not establish the cause of the restrictions reported in October 2026. [S23](#source-s23)

A hardware fault, an attack, a configuration mistake and an administrative decision can each prevent a user from communicating. They require different investigations and safeguards. Where a service rule is applied by the network, authority and procedure become central: which provision permits a change, who controls its scope, who receives notice and how can an error be corrected?

Such rules are not inherently illegitimate. A critical service must administer its users, obligations and risks. The problem arises when a beneficiary has built a strong dependency without being able to verify how the rules can change. A broad promise of “security” does not resolve the issue unless it explains authorised interruptions, coordination and remedies.

Reports concerning intelligence sharing must also remain distinct from those concerning satellite communications. Coincidence does not demonstrate a common cause. The senators specifically ask investigators to determine whether a link exists. Combining every reported malfunction into one account would manufacture certainty where attribution remains unresolved. [S06](#source-s06) [S26](#source-s26)

## Two bills can conceal one dependency

Diversification has a trap: it may change the commercial intermediary without changing the underlying system. Two contracts can depend on the same operator, service administration or infrastructure. They add counterparties and negotiating options, but do not necessarily provide independent capacity against the risk being considered.

A useful assessment looks for common failure points. If one authority suspends access, adding resellers does not address that cause. Two genuinely separate networks may still depend on local power or shared user equipment. Independence is always relative to a scenario. Counting suppliers without specifying the scenario can produce a misleading picture of resilience.

Ground infrastructure provides a concrete financial example. In January, Eutelsat announced that it would not proceed with a sale of passive ground-segment assets from which it had expected about €550 million in net proceeds. Reuters reported that France had blocked the transaction on national-security grounds. The figure represents expected disposal proceeds, not a cash loss of that amount. [S21](#source-s21) [S22](#source-s22)

The episode illustrates a trade-off between financing and control of assets deemed sensitive. It does not prove that owning antennas gives control over every condition of satellite service. Ownership may strengthen bargaining power or prevent particular changes while technical administration and authorisations remain governed elsewhere. Effective control must be assessed by connecting the assets an organisation owns to the decisions it can actually make.

The distinction is particularly important when governments support infrastructure companies financially. A capital contribution can help preserve a supplier, but the service rights obtained by a public customer still require examination. Conversely, a carefully specified capacity contract may deliver useful continuity without public ownership of every component. Neither the shareholding nor the procurement announcement should be treated as a complete description of control.

## CENTAURE reserves OneWeb capacity for French forces

France’s approach offers a concrete example of purchasing defined service rights. The DGA announced that CENTAURE had been signed on 15 June 2026 under the NEXUS framework. CENTAURE reserves OneWeb capacity for French forces; the DGA places the purchase within a hybrid approach complementing Syracuse IV. [S12](#source-s12)

The ministry’s equipment description identifies guaranteed throughput, measured in megabits per second, in specified areas, together with management tools for the armed forces. This detail matters: the purchased capability has a quantity, a scope and an intended use. Those definitions offer a more useful starting point for assessing continuity than a general claim of global coverage. [S13](#source-s13)

Eutelsat reports an initial firm commitment of approximately **€115 million excluding VAT over four years**, within a ceiling of approximately **€300 million excluding VAT for up to eight years**. These are the release’s footnoted tax-exclusive figures. The ceiling is neither money already spent nor secured revenue. The amounts come from the operator; the DGA confirms the contract. [S14](#source-s14) [S12](#source-s12)

The agreement illuminates the mechanism without providing a price for replacing Starshield in Ukraine. Areas, uses, equipment and responsibilities are not interchangeable. Dividing its value by an assumed terminal count, or comparing it with an aid budget on a different basis, would produce an attractive number with little operational meaning.

The useful point lies in the service definition. Guaranteed capacity can establish what the customer reserves and how it can administer that resource. Evaluating its robustness would still require the full continuity commitments, exceptions, change procedures and evidence of performance. The public documents reviewed do not disclose all those terms. Calling a service sovereign does not remove the need to inspect them.

This also changes the way a purchaser should read a headline price. An agreement may buy capacity, management tools and service-security work over different periods. Without the underlying allocation, a simple annual average is an accounting convenience rather than a demonstrated delivery schedule. The contract envelope is useful evidence of a commitment; it is not a public map of every service delivered in return.

## GOVSATCOM and IRIS²: current capacity and deployment milestones

Europe’s timetable needs to be read in stages. GOVSATCOM pools existing government satellite resources and began operating in January 2026; EUSPA announced an initial operational use by Cyprus in March. The roadmap provides for expansion to commercial resources in 2027. The programme organises access to existing capacity rather than waiting for an entirely new constellation. [S15](#source-s15) [S16](#source-s16)

For IRIS², the Commission’s updated communication of 7 August separates **first launches planned for 2029** from **initial secure government services from 2030**. The announced architecture contains 348 satellites: 330 in low Earth orbit and 18 in medium Earth orbit. These are deployment plans. [S17](#source-s17) [S18](#source-s18)

Airbus announced in September that it was developing an initial layer of at least 66 satellites, with deliveries planned for 2029. Industrial delivery, launch and usable service are distinct milestones. Collapsing them into a single date would create a false impression of immediate availability. [S19](#source-s19)

European capabilities can expand available options, but access for a particular beneficiary depends on authorisation, equipment and the service required. None of the reviewed announcements demonstrates an immediate, complete substitute for every Ukrainian use of Starlink or Starshield. Satellite numbers alone cannot yield a measure of useful capacity either: network design, coverage, terminals and access rules all matter.

Nor does reserving capacity for one government establish its availability for another. Transferring an entitlement may require a separate decision, agreement or technical arrangement. The public announcements should therefore be read as evidence of expanding options, not as proof that all those options have already been made available to Ukraine on equivalent terms.

## The strongest counterargument deserves a hearing

Dependence is not sufficient grounds for rejecting commercial infrastructure. Capability available in time may be worth more than a perfectly controlled system still under development. US commercial integration policy explicitly accepts that trade-off and seeks to manage it. A serious comparison must assess usable services and their conditions, rather than assume public or private ownership settles the issue. [S24](#source-s24) [S25](#source-s25)

Building a second chain costs money, potentially drawing resources from other needs. Poorly prepared duplication can introduce complexity that reduces reliability. A more useful objective is to identify the functions whose interruption would be most damaging, the causes that threaten them and the continuity capacity that would actually reduce the harm. That does not require every component to be duplicated identically.

The benefit of an alternative may be hard to see during normal operations. Reserved capacity, compatible equipment and a continuity exercise do not necessarily increase everyday traffic. Their value becomes visible when the main service fails or its conditions change. This is a form of operational insurance whose cost should be compared with the consequences of interruption, consequences that the current public record does not quantify for Ukraine.

An operator’s nationality matters because it can change the relevant authorities and the interests involved. It does not, by itself, describe technical quality or effective governance. Control over service rules, visibility into changes and the ability to activate another connection require specific safeguards, including when the provider is domestic or European.

The practical economic comparison is therefore not merely between two subscription prices. It is between service arrangements over time, including preparation, continuing compatibility and the consequences of losing access. A cheaper primary service combined with a credible fallback may be preferable to a costlier single system; in another setting, stronger guaranteed rights may be the more valuable investment. Public evidence here is insufficient to price either choice for Ukraine.

## The records that could settle the issue

Attribution would require the configuration-change timeline, its scope, the instructions received, the notifications sent and the applicable contractual provisions. Matching those records could distinguish political direction, operator action, error or a combination of causes. These are investigative questions, not conclusions that can be extracted from a restoration announcement.

The senators’ request to preserve records addresses that need. Their 1 November date concerns an initial response requested; it guarantees neither the opening of an investigation nor a final public finding by that date. As of 17 October, the documents reviewed do not settle responsibility. [S05](#source-s05) [S06](#source-s06)

The available evidence supports a specific conclusion. Connectivity depends on the permissions that make a link usable. When a state builds critical functions around that link, the authority able to alter those permissions becomes part of its risk exposure. Capacity contracts, change procedures and alternatives that can actually be used then belong in the infrastructure assessment alongside the satellites themselves.
