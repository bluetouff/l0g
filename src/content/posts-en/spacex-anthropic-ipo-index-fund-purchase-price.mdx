SpaceX sold shares to the public at $135. An Irish index fund available to investors in France reported acquiring 2,497,064 of them at a total cost of $398.952 million in the financial year ended 31 July 2026. That works out to **$159.77 a share**. That is $24.77 above the offer price. SpaceX was raising capital in June. The fund was tracking a July change in the Nasdaq-100 as part of its [index-investing mandate](/en/glossary/gestion-passive/). To say how much the index timetable cost the fund, we would need to know how many shares it could have secured at $135.

[S01](#source-S01) · [S05](#source-S05)

The evidence sits on printed page 374 of the iShares VII annual report, in an unaudited schedule of material purchases and sales. Estimates of how much index funds might spend on the next big tech flotation are easy to find. Here the buyer gives both the number of shares acquired and what they cost. On 12 November [l0g examined](/en/analysis/anthropic-ipo-index-funds-price-formation/) a $19 billion estimate of potential passive demand for Anthropic. In the six days since, index providers have published timetables and amended a rule. The SpaceX case shows what admission to an index can mean for someone who already owns shares in an [exchange-traded fund (ETF)](/en/glossary/etf/) tracking the Nasdaq-100.

## The buyer who comes after the IPO

SpaceX started trading on Nasdaq on 12 June. By the time the offering closed on 15 June, the company had sold 638,888,888 shares at $135, including the underwriters’ option. That produced **$86.25 billion gross**. SpaceX reported $85.675 billion net of underwriting discounts and offering costs. This was money for the company. Subsequent purchases on the stock market pay whoever sells the shares there.

[S05](#source-S05) · [S06](#source-S06) · [S07](#source-S07)

Eleven days later, Nasdaq announced that SpaceX would join the Nasdaq-100 **before the market opened on 7 July**. The difference between that wording and “buy on 7 July” matters to a fund that aims to track the index by holding its stocks. Wait for the opening bell and the fund can miss an overnight move. Buy too soon and it holds a stock the index still excludes. Shares can also enter an ETF when an authorised participant delivers a basket of securities in exchange for new fund units. The manager has a benchmark to track. The inventory records the shares that arrived, whatever route they took.

[S08](#source-S08) · [S03](#source-S03) · [S04](#source-S04)

We checked the daily holdings of the **iShares NASDAQ 100 UCITS ETF**, a physically replicated fund domiciled in Ireland. Its 2 July file has no line for SPCX. Its 6 July file, dated the day before index entry took effect, already shows **2,457,717 shares**. On 7 July the number is identical. Yet the value of the holding fell **$26.91 million** as the valuation price went from $160.42 to $149.47. Anyone trying to recover the fund’s purchases from the dollar change on the official entry date would have read the transaction backwards.

[S34](#source-S34) · [S35](#source-S35) · [S36](#source-S36)

The annual report offers a separate check. Its 2,497,064 shares purchased are a cumulative figure for the financial year. The 31 July schedule of investments shows 2,471,688 shares left in the portfolio, or 0.95% of net assets. The daily holdings file agrees to the share. The $159.77 average cost comes from the purchases schedule. The $108.37 valuation price in the July-end file is a single day’s reading. Calculating a loss on every share acquired from the difference between those prices would assume the fund had kept them all.

## Three million more shares on a Friday

September brought a second event, more revealing than the flotation itself. A company’s **free float** is the portion of its stock available to trade. At the time of the SpaceX IPO, some existing shareholders had agreed to keep their stock off the market for a period. When a lock-up expires, shares already in existence can become available. The index can assign the company a higher weight without a dollar of fresh capital going to the issuer. Invesco put SpaceX’s free float at nearly 24% by the reference date for the September review.

[S10](#source-S10) · [S26](#source-S26)

On Thursday 17 September the Irish fund held 2,531,081 SpaceX shares. On Friday the figure was 5,588,487. The stock price went down. Valued **at Friday’s price**, the 3,057,406 additional shares contributed $466.90 million to the position. The lower price wiped $5.32 million off the shares already on Thursday’s books. Before rounding, the two effects reconcile to the cent with the **$461.58 million increase** in value. The calculation explains a balance-sheet change. It reveals neither the execution price nor how many shares might have arrived in kind.

[S38](#source-S38) · [S39](#source-S39)

Could the fund simply have got bigger? We matched the **101 equity lines** by ticker across the two days. Of the 100 other lines, 71 showed a fall in share count. The median change was **−1.38%**. The largest increase among them was 13.96%. SpaceX was up **120.79%** in shares held. In its September review, Invesco reported a SpaceX weight increase from 1.28% to 2.82%, stated as effective on 22 September. Its US-listed QQQ ETF recorded $4.792 billion in net outflows for the month. An inflow spread evenly across the Irish fund would struggle to explain why its SpaceX position doubled while most other share counts fell.

On **a hypothetical $1 billion of assets**, a weight rising from 1.28% to 2.82% takes a target position from $12.8 million to $28.2 million. At unchanged prices and fund size, for a fund starting precisely at the old weight, the adjustment is $15.4 million. To put a dollar figure on market-wide demand, one would need the assets tracking each relevant benchmark, existing exposures and the replication method. A single portfolio can serve several share classes. Adding their assets to the fund total would count the same money twice.

A French investor can gain exposure to substantially the same market through a different arrangement. A synthetically replicated ETF can receive its benchmark’s performance through a swap with a bank. Its holdings and the counterparty’s hedges then follow a different mechanism from the physically replicated fund examined here.

## December has more than one index date

**MSCI** says that if trading starts on 19 November and Anthropic clears its tests, entry into its Global Standard indexes would take place at the close on **30 November**, effective **1 December**. **FTSE Russell** issued a specific notice for its US index family on 13 November. It will test investable market capitalisation against a $17.942 billion threshold after the first close. If the shares start trading on 19 or 20 November and qualify, the usual fifth-session timetable would be too close to Thanksgiving for funds to replicate comfortably. Entry would take place after the **11 December** close, effective at the open on **14 December**. FTSE’s global index family has a different conditional date again, **21 December**.

[S17](#source-S17) · [S29](#source-S29) · [S31](#source-S31)

The eligible share count matters as much as the date. In a technical update dated 13 November, FTSE Russell excluded over-allotment shares from the initial calculation for fast entry to its US indexes, alongside shares still locked up. MSCI says an option publicly confirmed as exercised before its decision on the first or second trading day can enter its calculation. Two providers reading the same prospectus can reach different figures for investable capital.

**Nasdaq** screens a fast entrant on its full market value at the close of the seventh trading session. It must rank in the top 40 relative to existing index companies and pass the other tests, including a minimum trading-value requirement. For weighting, however, the share count for an eligible listed line is capped at three times its freely floating shares. Eligibility answers one question and weight another. With trading starting on 19 November, the Thanksgiving holiday puts day seven on 30 November, the annual review’s reference date. The fifteenth session falls on 10 December, in the month of that review. Under our reading of the rulebook, a successful admission would take effect at the **21 December open**. That date is our conditional calculation.

## How much can an index move the price?

A 2017 change to CRSP’s index entry rules gave Chris Murray and Marco Sammon a way to compare US IPOs affected by accelerated inclusion with other offerings. They compared actual proceeds with the amount implied by the proposed share count and the midpoint of the filed price range. On that measure, they estimate that fast-tracked issuers raised about 6.2% more than comparable deals. Some of the relative share-price gain around inclusion subsequently unwound. Their work supports the proposition that a buyer expected tomorrow can influence an offer priced today. It supplies no coefficient one could reliably apply to Anthropic’s proposed 825 million shares.

[S20](#source-S20)

The ECB’s study published on 18 November adds two qualifications. In its January 1995 to July 2026 sample of offerings raising at least €1 billion, the median return at one month was 4.97%, against 1.00% for the market comparison. By the one-year mark the market comparison was ahead, at 9.70% against 7.61% for the IPOs. Sample companies belonged at some point to the S&P 500 or EURO STOXX 300. A fund that came in after the IPO earned a return that depended on its own purchase price. The two published statistics use different weighting methods. The ECB’s regression on passive ownership, moreover, concerns **follow-on** offerings by already listed companies. The authors see a mechanism that could amplify the equity cycle. They judged that these offerings did not represent a systemic risk at the time of their study.

[S18](#source-S18) · [S19](#source-S19)

On 11 June Senator Elizabeth Warren asked index providers what role SpaceX had played in the changes to their rules. Nasdaq had aired its own reasoning on 8 May. Companies were staying private longer and could go public at a substantial size with comparatively few shares available to trade. The public record establishes when the rules changed and when SpaceX joined the index. How much influence the company had remains an open question.

[S21](#source-S21) · [S22](#source-S22)

An issuer can shape its offer size, share classes and the timetable on which insiders gain the right to sell. Index providers decide how much of that stock will count in a saver’s portfolio. We can date the September change in the SpaceX holding unusually closely. The company had banked its IPO proceeds three months earlier. Still, one European fund added three million shares to its reported position on a Friday when the stock fell. If Anthropic enters these indexes in December, the first records worth comparing will be the fund’s holdings on the day before the change and the day after.
