Price rises reach households through very different budgets. Housing costs, necessary journeys and shopping habits shape each household's exposure. Understanding the pressure on a budget means looking at the level of prices, the composition of spending and the money left available.

On 30 October 2026, France's statistical office, Insee, provisionally estimated that consumer prices had risen by **3.4% over the year to October**, following a final reading of **3.1% in September**. The first figure, a [year-on-year rate](/en/glossary/glissement-annuel/), compares October with October; the second compares September with September. Final October results are scheduled for 13 November. This sequence of dates may sound administrative, yet it defines precisely the question that each rate answers. [R02](#source-r02) [R03](#source-r03)

At the checkout, the comparison often reaches further back. We remember a price from before the sharp increases, the cost of filling the car, or how much of the budget food used to take. Insee documents that shift in price levels: between the 2015 average and the first half of 2026, food prices rose by approximately **35%**, compared with **23% for consumer prices overall**. These cumulative changes cover a different period from today's headline figure. [R01](#source-r01)

The gap between a national index and everyday experience deserves a closer look. A family buying more food, someone who needs a car for work, a tenant and a homeowner who has paid off their mortgage face different constraints. Their circumstances also depend on the products they choose, the support they receive and their income. A household label captures only part of that picture.

Making sense of the difference begins with separating these questions: which prices changed, how much of the budget was exposed to them, and what resources were available to meet the resulting bill?

## Past rises stay in the total

Inflation measures a change in prices between two dates. The price level carries the accumulated effect of successive changes. This simple distinction is essential when reading a budget. The European Central Bank also uses it to explain differences between measured and perceived inflation. [R12](#source-r12)

Consider a constructed example. A €100 basket rises in price by 10%, taking its cost to €110. The following year, the increase slows to 2%. The basket now costs €112.20. The pace has eased while the bill has continued to grow. The increases compound: multiplying 1.10 by 1.02 gives 1.122, equivalent to a cumulative rise of 12.2%.

This helps explain why an improvement in the annual inflation rate can leave a budget under pressure. The same arithmetic works in the other direction: a price reduction reverses part of earlier increases, according to its size and the price to which it applies. The starting level always matters.

Remembering an older price therefore provides information about a longer period. Frequent purchases can also make particular changes more noticeable. The ECB describes this mechanism for everyday shopping. It offers one possible explanation for perceptions; assessing the constraints of income and expenditure also requires looking at the actual amounts involved. [R12](#source-r12)

## Each euro gives a price its weight

The **consumer price index, or CPI**, tracks prices for products with comparable characteristics. Each product contributes to the result in proportion to the expenditure it represents. This [weighting](/en/glossary/ponderation-de-consommation/) gives more influence to categories accounting for a larger share of national consumption. It describes a collective pattern of spending, updated annually. [R05](#source-r05)

A household has its own allocation. Suppose a category represents 10% of its starting basket and prices in that category rise by 20%. In a simple calculation with fixed weights, it contributes 2 percentage points to the basket's increase. With a weight of 25%, the same price change contributes 5 points. Exposure changes with the share of spending devoted to that category.

The example keeps the two ingredients separate. The category's price increase stays the same; its importance in the budget changes. Reading both together explains how a common movement in prices can produce different overall results.

The 2026 data provide a revealing example. Over the common window from **December 2025 to September 2026**, the energy index rises by approximately **22.17%**, the food index by **1.43%** and the services index by **2.26%**. These are nine-month changes, using final data available on 30 October. Our calculation uses five broad categories: food, tobacco, manufactured goods, energy and services. [R03](#source-r03) [R04](#source-r04)

In the national weighting structure for 2026, energy accounts for **7.64%** of the basket. Over this window, it contributes approximately **1.69 percentage points** to the calculated increase, out of a total close to **3.12%**. This illustrates how a relatively small category can make a substantial contribution when its prices move sharply. The contributions shown are our calculations from the published indices and weights. [R04](#source-r04)

These calculations require a consistent calendar. The CPI is linked annually: its weights evolve, and successive annual segments are joined together. Insee's simulator and Eurostat's documentation explain the procedure. Simply averaging index levels across ten years would leave out that linking process. Our tool uses a single window within 2026 to make the effect of weighting easy to follow. [R06](#source-r06) [R11](#source-r11)

Since January 2026, the CPI has used a 2025 base and an updated detailed classification. Insee has reconstructed the historical series used here on that basis. The number assigned to the base year provides a calculation reference; comparisons depend on ratios between observations within the same series. The documentation explains the changes in classification and how series are linked. [R05](#source-r05) [R15](#source-r15)

## Housing commits income before you shop

Housing expenditure helps explain why national weights can differ from an individual household's experience. Rent absorbs a substantial share of some tenants' resources. Its CPI weight is spread across the consumption of all households, including those paying no rent. Home purchases and housing-loan repayments fall outside the consumption expenditure tracked by the index. [R14](#source-r14)

Insee also measures housing through an indicator designed to describe its budget burden: the **[housing expenditure-to-income ratio](/en/glossary/taux-d-effort-en-logement/)**, known in French as the *taux d'effort*. It relates expenditure on the main home to income. For the net ratio, housing benefits are deducted from expenditure. A study published on 23 September 2026 describes circumstances observed **in early 2024**. It provides a structural reference from a period preceding the latest price increases. [R07](#source-r07)

In that study, the net ratio reaches **25.4%** for private tenants, **24.0%** for social tenants and **23.3%** for homeowners still repaying a loan used to acquire their home. It is **7.4%** for homeowners with no such loan outstanding. These ratios compare the sum of expenditure with the sum of income within each group. Expenditure includes the principal repaid by mortgage borrowers; income is measured before direct taxes and excludes housing benefits. [R07](#source-r07)

Those definitions are essential to interpreting the figure. The bar for a group cannot reconstruct a particular tenant's budget. It does help explain why housing leaves households with different amounts of room to adjust their spending, before any comparison of grocery price increases begins.

**[Pre-committed expenditure](/en/glossary/depenses-pre-engagees/)** provides another perspective. It covers spending tied to contracts that are difficult to renegotiate in the short term: housing and associated charges, certain subscriptions, insurance and financial services, according to the statistical definition. The term describes the contractual commitment. Food can remain essential while falling outside this category. [R13](#source-r13)

Someone who depends on a car faces a different constraint again. Fuel spending can vary with the journeys made; the scope for reducing those journeys depends on the workplace, working hours and available alternatives. Here, we treat that relationship as something to examine within a budget, without assigning a supposedly common spending share to all rural households.

## A smaller bill can hide a different purchase

Expenditure depends on price, quantity and the product selected. Buying less or switching products can contain the amount paid while comparable prices continue to rise.

Here is a second constructed example. Product A rises from €1 to €1.40; product B rises from €2 to €2.50. Their respective price increases are 40% and 25%. Someone who previously bought one unit of B for €2 and then switches to one unit of A for €1.40 sees the bill fall by 30%. The purchase has changed. Measuring prices requires following A and B separately through time.

The price tier chosen can also affect the inflation a shopper encounters. An exploratory Insee study published in March 2026 finds that, between January 2022 and December 2025, the cumulative price rise for its entry-level basket exceeded that for the overall basket studied by **10 percentage points**. Its coverage is specific: food excluding fresh products in metropolitan France's hypermarkets and supermarkets, excluding hard-discount chains. Products absent at either end of the period are removed, and baskets are reconstituted annually. [R08](#source-r08)

This finding encourages a closer look inside a broad category such as “food”. Two people allocating the same budget share to food may buy different products through different retail channels. Our five-category tool keeps the average price movement within each category. Readers need to be able to see that level of detail, and what it leaves unresolved.

Comparing quality is part of the same task. When a pack becomes smaller and that characteristic is observed, the CPI methodology adjusts the price comparison. Product replacements also require adjustments to bring comparable characteristics into line. Deterioration that is harder to observe leaves greater uncertainty. Insee's methodological article explains these choices and their limits. [R10](#source-r10)

For a household, a statistical quality adjustment and a cash payment address distinct concerns. A more capable appliance may represent a measurable improvement while requiring a sum that is difficult to finance. That difference belongs in any discussion connecting a price indicator with a household budget.

## Three baskets, one time window

To make these relationships tangible, we constructed three starting baskets of **€2,000**, valued at December 2025 conditions. The amount is chosen for illustration. The first uses the proportions of the national 2026 weights; the other two allocate 20% to energy and 30% to food respectively. The tool shows every allocation. These are examples with deliberately chosen compositions.

The national allocation serves as a reference structure against which the two chosen compositions can be compared. All three calculations begin with the same sum and follow the same calendar, leaving the allocation of that sum as the distinction to examine.

The calculation multiplies each starting amount by the ratio between its September 2026 index and its December 2025 index, then adds the resulting costs. Every euro in the basket therefore follows its category's index. Monetary results are rounded to the nearest cent within each category before being added together. ## Purchasing power has an income side

A budget's constraints depend on its resources as well as prices. Higher income can absorb part of an increase in spending; lost income can put a budget under pressure even when prices stay unchanged. Analysing purchasing power therefore requires an income measure consistent with the expenditure and price index used for comparison. National accounts use their own consumption deflator for this calculation. [R01](#source-r01) [R09](#source-r09)

Healthcare illustrates another boundary. The CPI tracks medicines and outpatient care before public reimbursement. The European **harmonised index of consumer prices, or HICP**, accounts for those reimbursements under its conventions. A change in reimbursement can therefore alter the amount borne by a patient without an equivalent rise in the price observed by the CPI. Housing benefits and France's energy voucher have a further distinction: they are not deducted in the HICP. [R09](#source-r09)

These differences in scope explain why a bank statement requires a complementary reading. To trace the source of pressure, it helps to examine comparable prices, quantities purchased, products selected, commitments and income separately. Each change has its own route through to the money left available.

The national index remains useful for following an economy-wide price trend through an explicit methodology. An individual budget supplies the detail of exposure and the possibilities for adjustment. In this investigation, the accumulated food-price gap, the recent energy shock and housing commitments each explain part of the pressure. Bringing them together makes the bill easier to understand and supports more precise questions about the relationship between a published rate and everyday experience.
