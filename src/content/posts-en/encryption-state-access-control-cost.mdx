**The economic cost of a backdoor · Part 3 of 7**

A legal authorization has to become a technical instruction and, eventually, a flow of data. Along the way, people activate access, protect secrets and check the records. This investigation follows that infrastructure and its funding in France, the United States and China.

For 2025, the U.S. Courts report records 296 federal wiretaps in which encryption was encountered. In 269 of them, the authorities reported being unable to decrypt the communications. Published on June 30, 2026, the report gives a concrete measure of the difficulty investigators face. It also leaves open the question that matters to a business, an engineer or a taxpayer: what would have to be built to make an additional route of access possible, and who would bear the consequences? <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R01" aria-label="Source R01">R01</a></sup>

An authorization sets an objective and a scope. To deliver a result, it needs a technical capability: a carrier able to isolate the targeted communications, a provider holding a key, an accessible device, or a mechanism added for the purpose. This is where the debate over backdoors acquires an economic dimension. An access function has to be designed, operated, protected and supervised. That work may continue after the case that made it necessary has closed.

This third article follows the passage from law to infrastructure. French, American and Chinese legislation offers different answers to the question of assistance. Technical standards help explain the work required to put those answers into practice. Read together, they reveal a shared concern: **the scope of an individual request can differ substantially from the reach of the system built to execute it.**

## Investigative obstacles, with limits to the evidence

The same U.S. report records 229 state wiretaps in which encryption was encountered, including 217 with a reported inability to obtain plaintext. These figures count reported wiretaps, using information collected about the investigations to which they relate. They count neither messages nor people. An inaccessible communication may deny investigators useful evidence; the outcome of the investigation also depends on the other evidence available. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R01" aria-label="Source R01">R01</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R02" aria-label="Source R02">R02</a></sup>

<EncryptionControlFigure lang="en" kind="wiretaps" />

The gray portions of the chart need explaining. The arithmetic remainders are 27 for federal wiretaps and 12 for state wiretaps. They are labeled “Other wiretaps with encryption encountered.” The non-federal reporting guide provides for a missing answer to the question about inability to obtain plaintext to be filled in as “No.” We found no public count of answers completed in this way for 2025 in the documents examined. Treating these remainders as counts of successful decryption would therefore go beyond the evidence. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R02" aria-label="Source R02">R02</a></sup>

The coverage has further limits. The report identifies 249 federal authorizations and 80 state authorizations for which no corresponding prosecutor’s report was received. Interceptions governed by the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, or FISA, are excluded. These gaps prevent the bars from yielding a general rate of investigations stalled by encryption. They do not erase the difficulties that were reported. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R01" aria-label="Source R01">R01</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R25" aria-label="Source R25">R25</a></sup>

That is the strongest starting point for taking investigators’ requests seriously: some potentially useful content remains beyond their reach. Evaluating a response then requires knowing what it would make readable, under which circumstances and with what additional exposure. A count of judicial requests, on its own, supplies none of those answers.

## The different operations behind an access demand

“Access” covers several different operations. A provider may hand over data it can already read. A person may know the secret needed to decrypt a file. A mechanism may capture content on the device where it appears. A legislative change may seek to require a capability that the product did not previously have. Each situation involves different resources, rules and risks. The legislation examined here has to be read at that level of detail. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R03" aria-label="Source R03">R03</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R06" aria-label="Source R06">R06</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R09" aria-label="Source R09">R09</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R12" aria-label="Source R12">R12</a></sup>

In this article, a **backdoor** means an additional route for reading content introduced into an architecture to provide privileged access beyond the readers allowed by its original protection. This is a working definition. A demand for data already accessible to a provider does not, by itself, establish that such a route has been added.

U.S. law draws this distinction clearly. The Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act, or CALEA, requires interception capabilities from carriers within its scope. Section 1002 also limits those obligations: the subchapter does not authorize law enforcement to impose a specific technical design, and it excludes information services from the requirements of subsection (a). Its decryption provision has two cumulative conditions: the carrier must have supplied the encryption and must possess the information needed to decrypt. This provision has a defined scope; it does not summarize every U.S. power to obtain access to data. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R03" aria-label="Source R03">R03</a></sup>

The economic question follows immediately. Producing available data takes work. Creating a new route for reading it entails a design decision and subsequent maintenance. Conflating the two can assign the cost of transforming a service to a routine administrative response, or reduce that transformation to a formality.

## France’s different routes to assistance

French criminal law provides a first example. Under the conditions it specifies, Article 434-15-2 of the Criminal Code criminalizes refusing to provide or use secret means of decryption **known** to the person served with the demand. For this analysis, the decisive point is whether that person possesses the knowledge. The provision penalizes a refusal within a defined framework; its wording alone does not establish a duty to create an entirely new capability. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R09" aria-label="Source R09">R09</a></sup>

In its decision of March 30, 2018, the Constitutional Council upheld the challenged first paragraph. Its reasoning takes account, among other things, of knowledge of the secret means of decryption and the already identified data to which the request relates. The decision concerns the provision examined. Assessing another technique would require examining its own legal powers and safeguards. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R10" aria-label="Source R10">R10</a></sup>

Computer data capture follows a different route. Within the framework for special investigative techniques, the Code of Criminal Procedure allows the collection of data that is stored, displayed on a screen, entered or received through certain peripherals. The device is then the point of access to the content. Authorizing the measure, implementing it technically and overseeing its execution are separate functions. Depending on the procedural setting, authorization involves either the judge responsible for liberties and detention, acting on a prosecutor’s application, or an investigating judge after receiving the prosecutor’s opinion. In the urgent circumstances defined by the code, the investigating judge may act before receiving that opinion. Appointing a technician takes place within the framework of an authorised measure. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R11" aria-label="Source R11">R11</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R12" aria-label="Source R12">R12</a></sup>

Intelligence gathering under the administrative framework follows another regime. Article L. 871-1 of the Internal Security Code governs the provision of decryption information by certain providers and, in its second sentence, its use by providers, who may demonstrate that they cannot comply. The implementing rules require a written decision stating its reasons, issued by the prime minister or a delegate, notified to the provider and communicated to the chair of the Commission nationale de contrôle des techniques de renseignement, France’s intelligence oversight commission, known as the CNCTR. That communication has its own role in the procedure. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R06" aria-label="Source R06">R06</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R07" aria-label="Source R07">R07</a></sup>

The CNCTR also describes on-site and remote oversight of implementation, with access to records and the intelligence concerned within its remit. These checks can examine how an authorized technique has been used. Assessing a system’s security also requires knowing who can change its permissions, alter its logs or redirect its delivery destination. Legal oversight and technical scrutiny meet here, examining different aspects of the same process. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R14" aria-label="Source R14">R14</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R22" aria-label="Source R22">R22</a></sup>

For a provider, these distinctions are practical. The team handling a request must identify its legal basis, the authority issuing it, the data concerned and the capability actually available. The internal arrangements required cannot be inferred from the word “decryption” appearing in a demand.

## Instructions and data travel on separate paths

Consider an abstract example: a competent authority requests the communications of an identified target over a specified period. The example describes the functions of a system. It does not reproduce a particular case or network.

One path carries the **instructions**. The request arrives, its scope is translated into technical parameters, and the necessary permissions are activated. A second path carries the **data**: the content or associated information is collected, prepared and delivered to the intended recipient. This distinction is familiar in computer security. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s (NIST) zero trust architecture separates the decision to allow access, the establishment of a connection and the enforcement of the rules. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R23" aria-label="Source R23">R23</a></sup>

<EncryptionControlFigure lang="en" kind="control" />

The lawful interception architecture published by the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) in August 2026 likewise describes separate roles and functions, spanning the requesting authority, the provider and the recipient of the results. A standard gives systems a common language; applicable law determines the authorizations. Security then depends on what each component can do and on the checks performed between them. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R20" aria-label="Source R20">R20</a></sup>

The delivery path itself needs protection. Another ETSI specification provides encryption mechanisms for handing over intercepted information over IP. That protection aims to prevent the result from being exposed in transit. It addresses a separate question from reading the original content: securing the delivery channel does not explain how the provider obtained the plaintext. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R21" aria-label="Source R21">R21</a></sup>

The U.S. provisions illustrate this transition to execution. For activation within a carrier’s switching premises, Section 1004 of CALEA requires lawful authorization and the affirmative intervention of an officer or employee of the carrier. Section 229(b)(2) provides for rules requiring secure and accurate records of interceptions and access, whether authorized or unauthorized. The framework thus combines a condition for activation with a recordkeeping obligation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R04" aria-label="Source R04">R04</a></sup>

A simple error shows why these distinctions matter. An incorrectly translated identifier could direct a valid request toward the wrong target. An inadequately checked destination could send the result to the wrong recipient. These are operational scenarios; no frequency is estimated here. They show why accurate execution forms part of the cost of a measure even when its legal basis is established.

## When an authorization ends, the infrastructure may remain

The duration of a measure is an essential limit. Several time frames apply to the same access capability. The authorized period governs an operation. The equipment or software executing it may have been deployed earlier and may remain available afterward. Data already collected follows the retention and processing rules applicable to its use.

<EncryptionControlFigure lang="en" kind="lifetimes" />

The ETSI standard reflects this separation: moving to the inactive state stops the production of intercepted information while still allowing the delivery of communications already captured. Collection ending and every copy disappearing are therefore separate events. The timeline illustrates that logic without assigning a universal retention period. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R20" aria-label="Source R20">R20</a></sup>

The economic implication is an inference: if a capability remains installed, its components, permissions and interfaces continue to require appropriate management. A one-off request may use infrastructure that persists. The budget for the operation and the budget for maintaining that infrastructure need not follow the same timetable.

The question becomes more acute when a reading capability is added to a widely distributed product. An authorization may concern a single person. The mechanism designed to answer future requests may serve a much larger population. The difference depends on the design: selection by target, separation of environments, custody of secrets and the actual means of activation. Only a precise account of the architecture makes it possible to assess that reach.

## Audit trails need controls of their own

An [audit log](/en/glossary/journal-d-audit/) records events: an activation, a change in permissions, a delivery of data. Its value for oversight depends on how it is protected. If the same technical identity can trigger an operation and erase its record, the reviewer relies on evidence that the person or system under review can alter.

NIST addresses this problem through separation of duties, [least privilege](/en/glossary/moindre-privilege/), which limits permissions to necessary operations, and protection of audit information. The controls it describes include separate storage and restricted administrative permissions. These measures help limit the power concentrated in a single account or role and must be adapted to the system concerned. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R22" aria-label="Source R22">R22</a></sup>



Separation has a tangible cost. ETSI states that keeping trust domains separate requires network and personnel resources. Where domains are combined to simplify the architecture, the standard calls for security analysis and compensating measures. The trade-off is therefore present within the mechanism itself: a leaner arrangement can concentrate more technical power. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R20" aria-label="Source R20">R20</a></sup>

That concentration also attracts attackers. In its April 24, 2025 alert on the campaign known as Salt Typhoon, the FBI described the theft of call data logs, a limited number of private communications and the copying of certain information subject to U.S. court-ordered law enforcement requests. It attributed the campaign to actors affiliated with China. The source records the U.S. authorities’ findings and attribution; it does not establish that lawful interception interfaces were the points of entry or that end-to-end encryption was broken. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R26" aria-label="Source R26">R26</a></sup>

For this analysis, the case chiefly illustrates the value of information gathered around lawful access. An interception chain contains data, targets, parameters and records. Protecting it requires considering each of those objects.

The strongest argument for an organized access system lies precisely in the ability to define responsibilities, limits and evidence of use. The standards show that this engineering work exists. A further difficulty arises when a general route of access is demanded for content that the provider was previously unable to read. The research report *Keys Under Doormats* raised the complexity and governance problems associated with such exceptional access in 2015. Any particular architecture still needs to be assessed through its own mechanisms. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R24" aria-label="Source R24">R24</a></sup>

## China’s rules combine assistance with cryptographic protection

The Chinese comparison requires reading two sets of provisions together. Since the amendment that took effect on January 1, 2026, the general obligation to provide technical assistance appears in **Article 30** of the Cybersecurity Law, formerly Article 28. It requires network operators to support public security and state security bodies in their lawful national security and criminal investigation activities. The renumbering resulted from the insertion of new articles; the assistance obligation predates 2026. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R15" aria-label="Source R15">R15</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R16" aria-label="Source R16">R16</a></sup>

The Counterterrorism Law is more explicit about the technology. Article 18 refers to the technical interfaces and decryption assistance expected of telecommunications operators and internet service providers for the lawful prevention and investigation of terrorist activities covered by the law. It describes neither a universal key nor a single architecture imposed on every product. The details of a request and its implementation have to be established separately. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R17" aria-label="Source R17">R17</a></sup>

Alongside these duties, the Cryptography Law provides for lawful use of commercial cryptography and protections for certain proprietary information. It also prohibits forced transfers of commercial cryptographic technology through administrative means. These provisions have to be read alongside the assistance obligations; they do not establish a general exemption from every decryption request. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R18" aria-label="Source R18">R18</a></sup>

The 2025 regulation on critical information infrastructure gives this protective policy a practical form. Effective since August 1, 2025, it requires, among other things, qualified personnel for key management, operations and auditing, funding for cryptographic activities, and assessments before operation and at least annually afterward. Regulatory scrutiny of a key management mechanism does not establish that the secret values of the keys are handed over. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R19" aria-label="Source R19">R19</a></sup>

These Chinese government texts document written obligations. They cannot, on their own, reconstruct providers’ architectures or confidential practices. Their economic significance is that the state can require both cooperation with its agencies and sustained investment in cryptographic security. For a business within their scope, both requirements form part of its operating framework. How they fit together in practice depends on the requests, the product and the conditions of oversight.

## Public budgets cover some of the bill

The picture of a provider left to fund every form of cooperation alone is too simple. Under Article R. 873-1 of France’s Internal Security Code, the full costs of implementing L. 871-1 are met from the budget of the prime minister’s services, provided they have actually been incurred and are duly substantiated. The provision sets a funding rule; it supplies no figure for actual expenditure. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R08" aria-label="Source R08">R08</a></sup>

The Postal and Electronic Communications Code also distinguishes different kinds of work. Within the scope of Article D. 98-7, studies, engineering, design and deployment, as well as maintenance or rental, are covered by an agreement with the state. Processing requests is subject to rates set by ministerial order. The same framework requires the relevant minister to approve the technical choices. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R13" aria-label="Source R13">R13</a></sup>



Funding therefore attaches to defined tasks: building capabilities, maintaining them and handling a request. Agreements and tariffs allocate certain costs between the business and the state. For the economy as a whole, the work still has to be done; reimbursement changes who finances it.

The United States also provides for cost payments under CALEA. Section 1008 distinguishes equipment by deployment date and makes payments conditional on matters including available appropriations and, where applicable, a determination of whether compliance is reasonably achievable. It does not provide general insurance against every economic consequence of an access mechanism. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R05" aria-label="Source R05">R05</a></sup>

One figure shows why keeping these scopes separate matters. The U.S. report for 2025 publishes an average cost of **$92,963 in current dollars**, covering **804 authorizations with reported cost data**, for installing and monitoring wiretaps. The reporting instructions allow some expenditure associated with several related wiretaps to be grouped on a single form. This average describes the operations reported within that framework. It provides no measure of the additional cost of introducing a backdoor. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R01" aria-label="Source R01">R01</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R02" aria-label="Source R02">R02</a></sup>

Estimating that additional cost would require comparing a service with and without the proposed capability: design work, operations, oversight, replacement of components, incidents attributable to the change, and customers’ responses. Observed compliance expenditure supplies only part of that comparison. The reimbursement provisions cited do not put a price on a customer’s loss of confidentiality or its possible commercial consequences.

This investigation therefore produces no worldwide monetary total. The verified documents support a more precise conclusion: **cooperation has direct costs that the law can allocate; creating an additional capability requires its own technical and economic assessment.** Adding figures that measure other things would give a hypothesis the appearance of measurement.

## Assessing the consequences of an additional reader

A proposal for access can be examined by following how it would work. What information can the provider already read? What new capability would it have to acquire? Who can activate it, change its target or destination, and then verify what happened? What becomes of it when the authorization ends? These questions connect promised safeguards to actions that can be checked.

The investigators’ argument remains: access to certain communications may supply evidence they lack. The economic response is to examine the means needed to obtain that benefit and how their costs would be shared. The arrangements for separation, oversight and funding show how concrete that discussion can become. They also set a standard for scrutiny: a lasting capability calls for assessment over its entire life, including its authorized users, its administrators and the people whose data it could expose.

The signature on an authorization begins the process. Control of the infrastructure determines what follows.

### Reading guide

**End-to-end encryption:** protection designed so that the endpoints of a conversation hold the means to read its content. Devices and their copies retain their own security considerations. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R27" aria-label="Source R27">R27</a></sup> **Plaintext:** content in readable form, before encryption or after decryption. **Control plane:** the functions that decide on and activate an operation. **Audit log:** records intended to support examination of what happened. **Least privilege:** limiting technical permissions to the operations needed for a role. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R23" aria-label="Source R23">R23</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-R22" aria-label="Source R22">R22</a></sup>
