A company rents computing power in Malaysia. It sends jobs to a server and retrieves the results thousands of miles away. The machines stay where they are. Under a new US export rule, the identity of that customer can change the terms on which the data centre buys or uses certain equipment. [S01](#source-S01)

Speaking in Shenzhen on 18 November, Malaysia’s investment, trade and industry minister, Johari Abdul Ghani, said he was seeking details from Washington on licensing and third-party computing services. Kuala Lumpur intends to brief the country’s **117 approved data-centre projects** once it receives the official requirements, he told Bernama. Some of the facilities are still under construction. [S03](#source-S03)

The US Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) announced the measure on 16 November. The interim final rule was published in the *Federal Register* on **19 November 2026** and took effect that day. It introduces two controls that can apply to the same equipment. One follows the shipment. The other follows the company that gets to use the computing power. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02)

## The server stays put

Malaysia’s trade ministry (MITI) described Speedmatrix in a 13 November statement as a tenant that brings in its own servers and chips. The arrangement illustrates how a data centre can do business. A host provides space, electricity and cooling. Another company runs the hardware, and a customer in another country can rent the compute. [S04](#source-S04)

The US Export Administration Regulations (EAR) cover US items and certain foreign-made equipment. They already treat a change in end use or end user within the same foreign country as an in-country transfer. The November rule applies that principle to changes in how compute is supplied remotely. An operator can need a licence to reassign capacity long after the servers arrived. [S01](#source-S01) · [S05](#source-S05)

## Two checks on one shipment

The destination rule covers exports, re-exports and in-country transfers of specified advanced computing equipment to or within Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil. BIS reviews licence applications case by case. It weighs the effectiveness of a destination’s export controls and its cooperation with US authorities. [S01](#source-S01)

An exclusion applies when the ultimate consignee or [end user](/en/glossary/utilisateur-final/) is headquartered in the United States or has a US-headquartered ultimate parent. The exporter, re-exporter or transferor submits a monthly report. The separate end-use control still applies where its conditions are met. [S01](#source-S01)

<MalaysiaComputeFigure lang="en" kind="controls" number={2} sourceHref="#source-S01" />

That second check covers specified equipment when the supplier knows it is destined for a data centre that provides or will provide direct or indirect access to **more than 12 million aggregate TPP** for a company headquartered in Macau or a Country Group D:5 destination, which includes China. The same applies when the customer’s ultimate parent is headquartered there. BIS reviews these applications case by case as well. [S01](#source-S01)

[Aggregate TPP](/en/glossary/tpp-agrege/) sums the theoretical *total processing performance* of covered chips using a regulatory measure that takes account of operation throughput and bit length. The rule can count capacity across sites that are linked physically or virtually to share resources. It measures the compute available to the customer, separate from the power drawn by a building. [S01](#source-S01)

Other US export controls remain in force, including section 744.23 where the covered company is the primary remote user. Section 744.24 makes an exception for tools designed and used exclusively for commercial recommendations, subject to the required disclosures to BIS. Data centres already providing the covered service before publication have **90 days** from the effective date to obtain a licence to continue it under the transition provision. Our [analysis of the measure as announced](/en/analysis/nvidia-bis-remote-access-cloud-controls/) traces its relationship to earlier rules. [S01](#source-S01)

## The supplier inherits a question about the customer

A chip supplier knows who placed the order. The new control requires it to draw on the data centre’s due diligence on its own customers, including company ownership and control. A failure or refusal to supply the required information raises a red flag. While that flag remains unresolved, the rule presumes that the supplier knows about the covered end use. The EAR definition of knowledge also includes awareness of a high probability. [S01](#source-S01) · [S06](#source-S06)

## Malaysia tracks the movement of chips

Malaysia’s own rules ask a different question. As of 13 November, MITI said importing AI chips into the country required no Malaysian permit. Since July 2025, high-performance US AI chips leaving, transiting or being transshipped through Malaysia have been subject to a local permit regime. The ministry is checking under Malaysian law whether equipment brought in by Speedmatrix was later moved out of the country. [S04](#source-S04) · [S07](#source-S07)

Singapore’s Ministry of Home Affairs said on 12 November that its Megaspeed investigation covers servers shipped to Malaysia and possible onward movements. The US Commerce Department opened a separate investigation in 2024 into potential export-control violations, according to a joint statement with the Justice Department. The Justice Department says it later took the lead in the US criminal investigation. [S08](#source-S08) · [S10](#source-S10)

## Kulai’s contracts have their own timetable

In its quarterly report dated 19 November, YTL Power describes a **600 MW** data-centre park in Kulai with plans to expand to **1,200 MW**. It reports **298 MW under contract** and **13.5 billion ringgit invested** in its data-centre business. These figures describe different things. Contracted megawatts tell us about capacity commitments. Exposure to the US measure depends on the equipment installed and the customers entitled to use it. [S09](#source-S09)

A licence delay can shift a customer’s installation date. Who bears the cost depends on when payments begin, whether an installation can be deferred, and whether the operator can lease the capacity to someone else. Construction, customer demand and grid connections each run on their own schedule, as [our analysis of chip demand and the power grid](/en/analysis/tsmc-chip-demand-data-center-power-risk/) explains.

The next milestones are licensing decisions, commissioning dates and Malaysia’s guidance on the customers served from its data centres. A machine can already cross a regulatory line without leaving its rack. The customer can be enough to change its status. [S01](#source-S01) · [S03](#source-S03)

## Scope of the analysis

The public record covers the rules and the ongoing investigations. Chip inventories, customer contracts and YTL’s revenue exposure to the rule remain undisclosed in the records reviewed. The Kulai figures are YTL’s disclosures for the quarter ended 30 September 2026. [S04](#source-S04) · [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09)
