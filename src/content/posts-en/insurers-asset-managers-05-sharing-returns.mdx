*When investment firms buy insurers · Article 5/8 · Documentary investigation as of 23 October 2026*

**Two five-year contracts from Athene and Global Atlantic offer almost the same rate. Their terms, sales commissions and the accounts of Apollo and KKR open up a harder question: how is the income generated by savers’ money shared?**

Following the investigation into [affiliated investments at Apollo, Brookfield and KKR](/en/analysis/insurers-affiliated-investments-apollo-brookfield-kkr/), this fifth article follows the income from a savings contract into the group’s different businesses.

On Athene’s rate card, the number is printed large: **6.05% for five years**, for the premium level and state used in this comparison. Global Atlantic’s card advertises **6.00%** for a contract of the same duration. The documents carry dates just two days apart, 14 and 12 October 2026. They turn an insurer’s promise into something anyone can grasp: a sum of money, a rate and a time horizon. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Consider a wholly hypothetical comparison. A 60-year-old Florida resident pays $150,000 into a contract outside a retirement account, leaves the interest to accumulate and holds it until the five-year withdrawal-charge period has actually expired, making no withdrawals or additional payments. **Under the annual-compounding assumption used in our calculation**, the published rates produce approximately **$201,208 at Athene and $200,734 at Forethought, the insurer that issues SecureFore II under the Global Atlantic brand**. The calculated difference is $474 before tax. Global Atlantic specifies an annual effective rate; for Athene, annual compounding is a modeling assumption consistent with a historical MaxRate 5 illustration prepared in North Carolina in 2023. Values remain unrounded until the final result. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a>, <a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

That small difference opens a much larger investigation. Athene belongs to the Apollo group; Global Atlantic is part of KKR. In both groups, insurance brings obligations to honor and assets to manage, while asset management and corporate financing generate other revenues. Understanding who earns what means following these relationships while keeping the original contract in view. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a>, <a href="#source-s21" aria-label="Source 21">21</a></sup>

## Bringing two rates onto comparable terms

The products selected are *multi-year guaranteed annuities*, or MYGAs: US insurance contracts that guarantee an interest rate for several years. Here, we examine their accumulation phase. A lifetime annuity paying a monthly income would call for a different comparison, because its payments combine investment income, the return of capital and a guarantee tied to how long the recipient lives. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s26" aria-label="Source 26">26</a>, <a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source 27">27</a></sup>

We compare **Athene MaxRate 5**, issued by Athene Annuity and Life Company, with **five-year SecureFore II**, issued by Forethought Life Insurance Company. A $150,000 premium places both contracts in their $100,000-and-above rate tier. Both accept a 60-year-old applicant. Florida is a deliberate choice: the versions offered can vary by state. Athene, for instance, publishes a different rate and withdrawal-charge schedule for another group of states. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

This establishes a common duration and a comparable promise of growth. Differences remain in what each contract provides, particularly the ability to take money out. The guarantees also depend on each issuing insurer’s capacity to meet its obligations. Even a carefully matched rate comparison captures only part of the contract. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Both cards were retrieved again on 23 October and still carry their respective effective dates of 14 and 12 October 2026. They are public schedules: insurers may change new-business offers before purchase, and a personalized quote would establish the applicable terms. Our comparison aligns a holding period and calculation inputs using the documents accessible on the verification date. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

## Access to the money has a price

At Athene, the annual free withdrawal allowance equals the guaranteed rate multiplied by the accumulated value at the most recent contract anniversary. The right begins in year one; its specific first-year reference base remains to be confirmed in the contract documents because the current brochure leaves it unstated. At our selected rate, the proportion used is 6.05%. SecureFore II allows 10% a year: the initial premium is the base in year one, followed by the contract value at the beginning of each year. Withdrawals within the prescribed limits are exempt from the withdrawal charges and market value adjustment applicable to excess withdrawals. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

For perspective, on the same $150,000 base, 6.05% is $9,075 and 10% is $15,000. These amounts illustrate the two proportions; the amount available during an actual contract’s life will depend on its prescribed base and any withdrawals already made. Our five-year projection assumes that this access remains unused.

The charges beyond the allowance differ too. The Florida version of MaxRate 5 specifies a 10% withdrawal charge in each of the five years. SecureFore II’s schedule starts at 8% for the first two years, then falls to 7%, 6% and 5%. These percentages apply to withdrawals subject to charges under the contract’s terms. Treating them as an annual reduction in the entire account balance would distort the comparison. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a>, <a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

There is also the *market value adjustment*, or MVA. On withdrawals to which it applies, this adjustment can increase or reduce the amount received, depending on interest-rate movements and the contractual formula. It operates alongside withdrawal charges. For Florida, Athene’s brochure describes a 0.25 percentage-point interest-rate threshold: a fall smaller than that threshold leaves a negative adjustment. A precise withdrawal illustration therefore requires the applicable clause, the date and the relevant parameters. The charge percentages alone cannot produce that calculation. Waivers also exist for certain health circumstances, with conditions specific to each product. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

For someone confident of holding the contract for five years, the additional five basis points have a measurable value. Someone who may need to withdraw part of the capital might place greater weight on the wider allowance and the charge schedule. Their preference will depend on a need for access that a column labeled “return” leaves unexplained.

## The commission begins with a sale

Athene’s documents state that the professional selling the contract receives a sales commission, although the public rate card gives no exact amount. The SEC explains the broader economics of fixed annuities: costs can be embedded in the credited rate through the difference between what the insurer earns on its investments and what it promises the customer. An absence of an explicitly stated annual charge therefore says little, by itself, about the economic cost of distribution. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source 27">27</a></sup>

A public schedule offers a view of this compensation, within a specific set of limits. BetterAnnuityComp, a website recruiting distributors, advertises an October 2026 starting commission of **2% on MaxRate 5** for issue ages 0–70, and **2.5% on five-year SecureFore II** for ages 0–80. This level is offered to producers with at least one year’s experience; the schedule does not specify a state. The site describes the commissions as payable at issue and says some distributors can receive more. At those levels, a $150,000 premium would correspond to **$3,000 or $3,750**. This is the intermediary’s advertised offer. Our research obtained neither independent confirmation from the insurers nor a payment statement establishing that these amounts were paid on a contract. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

These conditional amounts expose a practical issue in distribution. Two products can offer customers very similar rates while promising different compensation to the salesperson. Assessing a particular sale would require the advice given, the customer’s needs and the compensation actually agreed. The schedule identifies information worth requesting: the intermediary’s actual agreed compensation, any payments elsewhere in the distribution chain, and the circumstances in which commission must be repaid following an early exit.

In our accumulation example, deducting $3,000 from the initial capital and then applying 6.05% would invent a charge outside the contractual assumptions used. The guaranteed rate governs the growth of the account. For the insurer, compensation paid to the distributor is a cost that the product’s economics must fund. The two perspectives describe the same contract using different timing and calculation bases.

That timing difference also appears in the accounts. Global Atlantic explains that certain commissions and expenses directly attributable to successfully acquiring contracts are recorded as deferred acquisition costs, then amortized over the expected contract life. In the first half of 2026, its fixed-rate annuity table adds $38 million to these costs and amortizes $63 million. Those figures describe the accounting for a portfolio spanning several generations of sales. They cannot establish a reliable average commission for a SecureFore II contract purchased today. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s23" aria-label="Source 23">23</a>, <a href="#source-s18" aria-label="Source 18">18</a></sup>

## At Athene, more assets and a narrower margin

Once a contract has been sold, the insurer must generate the resources to fund its promise. Athene’s accounts show how that works at the company level. For the first half of 2026, management’s presentation starts with **$7.795 billion of net investment earnings**. After adding $73 million of management-fee revenue received by Athene and accounting for the cost of its obligations, other operating expenses and financing, it reaches **$1.596 billion of [Spread Related Earnings](/en/glossary/sre/)**, or SRE. This is a pre-tax operating measure adjusted from accounts prepared under US generally accepted accounting principles, or US GAAP. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

The largest deduction, $5.749 billion, is called *cost of funds*. It combines several elements: interest credited to fixed strategies, the cost of options supporting indexed contracts, institutional obligations, amortization of acquisition costs and other expenses. Describing the entire amount as interest paid to savers would erase activities and costs that this investigation is trying to understand. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

The year-on-year movement is revealing. Average net invested assets rise by about 15.4%, while SRE falls by 1.7%. The annualized investment earned rate increases from 5.14% to 5.16%, while the cost-of-funds rate climbs from 3.57% to 3.80%. A company can thus manage considerably more money while its margin narrows. The figures establish that movement; they cannot attribute all of it to competition for customers or to a particular pricing decision. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

That 5.16% portfolio rate calls for care when we return to the MaxRate card: **the portfolio’s 5.16% and the 6.05% offered in October belong to different comparisons**. The first rate summarizes a portfolio of assets and obligations during the first six months of the year. The second is a new offer at a different date, for a particular product and duration. Establishing its profitability would require the returns on the assets used to back that new cohort of contracts, together with its funding and costs.

## Management fees come before the insurer’s earnings

The word “net” in investment earnings carries part of the story. In its half-year report, Athene records **$785 million in management fees incurred for Apollo**, after any fee waivers and rebates. The fees contain several components of compensation. The disclosed amount excludes certain fees charged within investment funds and fees borne by reinsurance partners, so it does not capture every layer of investment-management charges connected to the business. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

These fees are included in calculating Athene’s GAAP net investment income and appear at Apollo as intersegment revenue. Deducting them again from income already reported net would count the expense twice. Reversing the calculation also requires care: the $785 million includes the minority partners’ share in the ACRA vehicles, whereas the earnings bridge above reflects Athene’s economic share. Simply adding $785 million to $7.795 billion would therefore fail to reconstruct a consistent gross investment-income figure. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a>, <a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a>, <a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup>

For an investigation into how the income is shared, the crucial point is the service being paid for. The manager earns revenue by administering and directing investments, then bears its own expenses. The insurer calculates earnings after the charges attributable to its business. Apollo’s consolidated accounts eliminate internal transactions, preventing billing between subsidiaries from being confused with fresh revenue from outside the group. The economic question remains: how are revenues, costs and responsibilities allocated between the businesses? <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup>

## KKR sets out the revenues connected to its insurer

KKR offers another useful perspective because it brings several sources of income together in an explicit table. In the first half of 2026, its insurance business generates **$548.550 million of pre-tax operating earnings**, under its adjusted, non-GAAP definition. The management fees paid to KKR have already reduced the investment income used to calculate that result. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

The group then adds three components: **$289.460 million** of management fees from the insurance segment, **$125.578 million** of fees on reinsurance vehicles that bring in third-party capital, and **$33.817 million** of capital-markets fees from transactions facilitated by Global Atlantic. Each of these three lines is net of the compensation expense counted in KKR’s fee-related earnings, or [FRE](/en/glossary/fre/), measure, which excludes equity-based compensation. Together, the components reach **$997.405 million**, labeled *Total Insurance Economics*. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

The table shows how one group can earn money by insuring obligations, managing the associated assets, bringing in investment partners and arranging financing. The services and payers differ. KKR attributes reinsurance-vehicle management fees to third parties; linking them to the holder of our $150,000 contract would require further evidence. The published total also leaves the allocation of some manager overhead across these activities unresolved. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

A careful reading therefore keeps the $997.405 million distinct from the insurer’s own net profit or money deducted from customers’ accounts. The measure describes a broader economic contribution under KKR’s conventions. It makes the group’s interest in several complementary businesses visible while leaving the final margin on an individual product unresolved. Athene’s $1.596 billion of SRE and KKR’s $997.405 million also cover different scopes. Ranking them as competing measures of profitability would imply a precision the figures cannot support.

## An additional return can pay for useful work

The groups’ case deserves examination in its strongest form. In its annual report, Athene argues that originating loans directly can save on intermediary costs, improve knowledge of borrowers and allow it to negotiate financing terms. Global Atlantic emphasizes [asset-liability management](/en/glossary/alm/), matching the duration of its obligations with that of less liquid investments. An insurer expecting to hold an asset for a long time may, in theory, be better placed to bear its illiquidity than an investor who could need to sell tomorrow. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a>, <a href="#source-s22" aria-label="Source 22">22</a></sup>

The working paper by Meisenzahl, Overpeck and Polacek, published by the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago and revised on 27 April 2026, studies this mechanism in a specific setting. Its findings concentrate on indexed annuities, for which certain private investments improve maturity and cash-flow matching. The authors also link these investments to market-share gains by private-equity-owned insurers. Higher yields accompany lower liquidity and security-specific distress or default profiles. Applying these observations therefore requires preserving the distinction between indexed annuities and fixed-rate contracts. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s24" aria-label="Source 24">24</a>, <a href="#source-s25" aria-label="Source 25">25</a></sup>

Measuring the additional return reaching a MaxRate or SecureFore II customer would require a product-specific analysis. A better-organized financing business could both support a competitive customer rate and leave more income with the group. It could also create an advantage largely absorbed by fees or compensation for risk. Distinguishing these possibilities requires comparable obligations and assets, examined over the same period and after all relevant costs.

Both contracts examined here come from groups connected to large alternative asset managers. On their own, they cannot serve as an experiment measuring the effect of that ownership against a mutual or an independent insurer. Their closely matched rates support a specific commercial comparison, while leaving the broader merits of different ownership structures unresolved.

## From contract growth to group earnings

Under a fixed contract held through the chosen guarantee term, the policyholder earns the promised growth. The distributor is paid for the sale under its commercial agreement. The manager receives income for investing the assets, and the insurer retains the earnings left after meeting its obligations and expenses, while bearing the associated risks. Where these businesses belong to the same group, the shareholder can benefit from several sources of earnings. Adding them together requires accounting for costs and eliminating internal transactions within the scope being examined. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a>, <a href="#source-s21" aria-label="Source 21">21</a></sup>

To establish the additional income actually retained from a new sale, a precise link between these levels is still missing: the actual distribution commission, the assets attributed to the cohort, every layer of fees, the cost of guarantees and capital, expected losses and the value of withdrawal rights. The public reports reviewed here do not disclose that calculation for our two products. The quote, applicable terms and economic data for that cohort of contracts would allow the comparison to proceed.

Under our annual-compounding assumption, the five-year projection shows about $474 in additional growth, alongside different rules for accessing the money. For the investigator, the decisive task is to trace the same money from the contract to the investments and the businesses within the group. Only then can an additional return be assessed as a shared benefit, payment for a useful service or a margin retained.

### Method and scope

This investigation draws on public documents verified through 23 October 2026. The documentary comparison uses rate cards and brochures; a personalized quote and contract documents would establish the terms of a purchase. Global Atlantic explicitly describes its rate as annual effective. For Athene, our calculation assumes annual compounding, checked for consistency against a 2023 MaxRate 5 illustration for a 65-year-old man in North Carolina with a qualified contract. That check has a historical and methodological scope. The Florida rates come from October 2026 schedules, and simulated five-year values assume the withdrawal-charge period has actually expired. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a>, <a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

The commission figures come from an explicitly identified distributor’s advertised offer. Half-year accounts, their definitions and their reconciliations are examined separately from October pricing. No interview, request for comment or individual payment is presented as having taken place. The illustrations show calculations or representations of the cited data; they introduce no invented flows between contracts and assets.
