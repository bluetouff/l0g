A business financed by a [private credit](/en/glossary/credit-prive/) fund may keep a bank credit line to pay suppliers when cash comes in slowly. The fund may have its own line. If the same slowdown hits several businesses and drains cash from the funds that finance them, the requests can arrive together. The Financial Stability Board (FSB) is trying to measure that channel of contagion. [S01](#source-S01)

The Federal Reserve’s **13 November** and the ECB’s **18 November 2026** stability reports track two different relationships. The Fed measures large reporting banking groups’ credit commitments to a broad set of financial institutions. The ECB tracks protection against losses that euro area banks buy from investors. The reports cover different markets and contracts. The connection to examine is how the investors taking on the risk fund themselves. [S02](#source-S02) · [S03](#source-S03)

## Banks can buy protection and keep the loans

A bank can keep the loans, the interest and the customer relationship while paying an investor to absorb a slice of losses. The ECB calls this a [synthetic risk transfer](/en/analysis/the-risk-that-goes-in-circles/). In a funded deal, cash is put up in advance as collateral. With unfunded protection, the bank relies more heavily on the guarantor’s ability to pay when losses occur. In regulatory language, recognition of [significant risk transfer](/en/glossary/srt/) can qualify a bank for capital relief. [S03](#source-S03) · [S04](#source-S04)

Take a hypothetical **€100 million** loan portfolio. The bank bears the first **€2 million** of losses and the investor covers the next **€8 million**. If losses reach **€6 million**, the bank absorbs **€2 million** and the investor **€4 million**. At **€15 million**, the protection is exhausted. The bank bears **€7 million** across the first-loss slice and the losses above the guarantee. [S03](#source-S03)

<RiskReturnFigure lang="en" kind="transfer" />

At the end of June **2026**, the ECB counts about **€630 billion in underlying loans** and **€120 billion in committed protection**. It estimates relief equivalent to **40 basis points** on the euro area banking system’s [CET1 ratio](/en/glossary/cet1/), and **49 basis points** for issuing banks. The basis points describe an effect on the capital ratio through risk-weighted assets. The protection itself covers the losses specified in the contract. [S03](#source-S03)

The ECB currently sees limited risks to financial stability. Most deals are funded. Specialist investors are active and the protection often matches the loans’ maturity. Renewal poses a separate problem. Existing funded protection can keep absorbing losses until it expires while new protection becomes scarcer or costs more. A bank relying on that relief to make loans then has to revisit its lending plans or set more capital aside. [S03](#source-S03) · [S04](#source-S04)

## Banks also lend to investors

The large banking groups in the Fed’s data had **more than $2.8 trillion in credit commitments** to other financial institutions in the **second quarter of 2026**. The total captures committed credit lines and term loans. The group comprising private equity, business development companies (BDCs) and private credit accounts for about **a quarter**. Commitments to vehicles including special purpose entities, CLOs and ABS, which fund pools of assets, rose **nearly 30%** over the year. [S02](#source-S02)

The FSB measures a narrower relationship. Its **2024** data cover about **$220 billion in bank lines** to private credit funds across **five jurisdictions**, with uneven coverage of drawn and available amounts. The FSB cites commercial estimates of **$270 billion to $500 billion**, covering different dates and populations. We previously examined [this gap in the data](/en/analysis/private-credit-risk-begins-where-data-ends/). It also prevents the figures from being added together. [S01](#source-S01)

Collateral varies by facility. A subscription line typically relies on investors’ capital commitments. A net asset value facility uses the fund’s assets as its borrowing base. For some risk transfer positions financed through [repo](/en/glossary/repo/), the BIS reports **40–60% haircuts**, daily margining and additional collateral. Its market contacts suggest the banks providing the financing are usually different from those that transferred the risk. Leverage looks modest in the cases observed. The market-wide picture remains incomplete. [S01](#source-S01) · [S04](#source-S04)

## One shock can prompt several draws

When the business draws on its line, the bank has to deliver the funds. If a fund that borrows from the same bank also draws on its facility, two commitments turn into immediate funding needs. Any later losses depend on repayment and collateral. A shock can therefore bring liquidity demand back to banks before a lender records a default. [S01](#source-S01) · [S05](#source-S05)

<RiskReturnFigure lang="en" kind="liquidity" />

At the onset of the pandemic, US companies that borrowed from both banks and private lenders drew more heavily on their bank lines than comparable companies that borrowed only from banks, in a study by Sharjil Haque, Simon Mayer and Irina Stefanescu. The study tracked company borrowing. Simultaneous draws by funds are part of the stress scenario the FSB describes. [S01](#source-S01) · [S08](#source-S08)

A second hypothetical example shows how a loss can reach a lender. A fund invests **€8 million** in a slice of protection. It puts up **€4 million** and borrows the rest from another bank, with the slice as its sole collateral. A **€4 million** loss wipes out the fund’s stake and leaves **€4 million** in collateral. A **€6 million** loss leaves **€2 million** against a **€4 million** loan. A sale at that price would leave the lender **€2 million** short in this example. Recourse, margin calls and other collateral could change the outcome in a real deal. [S04](#source-S04)

<RiskReturnFigure lang="en" kind="waterfall" />

The funding horizon also affects the response. An investor with long-dated liabilities can wait. Withdrawals, margin calls or tighter refinancing terms can force a fund or insurer to find cash. In November, the Fed reported that leverage at the largest US life insurers remained historically high and that their non-traditional liabilities rose in the first half of the year. The risk depends on how readily they can sell or finance their assets and on the terms attached to those liabilities. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02)

## Testing the response to a common shock

On **19 June 2026**, the Bank of England published the scenario for its exercise on private markets. It wants to see how participants respond to the same shock. A bank may want to preserve cash just as a business and its fund are counting on it. The exercise puts those decisions alongside one another. The Bank of England expects to publish its findings in the **first half of 2027**. [S06](#source-S06) · [S07](#source-S07)

The November figures show the scale of protected loans and bank commitments. To follow the risk through the system, supervisors would need to trace each slice of protection to the balance sheet that holds it, the banks that provide funding and the dates those commitments come due. A transfer can absorb losses exactly as intended and still leave banks with cash to provide when several clients ask for it at once. [S01](#source-S01) · [S03](#source-S03) · [S04](#source-S04)
