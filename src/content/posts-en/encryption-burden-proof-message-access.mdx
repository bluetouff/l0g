**The economic cost of a backdoor · Part 7 of 7**

A mandate for access to encrypted messages should be judged by the useful information it delivers, the risks it adds and the resources needed to oversee it. From the FBI’s data collection to China’s compliance requirements and Europeans’ views on privacy, this final instalment examines the evidence a public decision needs.

On 21 October 2026, the US Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) published an administrative notice seeking renewal of its *Lawful Access Data Collection*. The programme gathers reports of investigations affected by encryption. Comments remain open until 20 November. A month earlier, the agency had acknowledged limited participation and little reported data. Establishing the scale of the investigative problem remains a work in progress. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X01" aria-label="Source X01">X01</a></sup>

The notice concerns voluntary statistical reporting. It offers a concrete starting point for the question that closes this series: what evidence should justify requiring access to encrypted data? An investigative obstacle, a technical solution and a public benefit each need examination. Connecting them takes work, explicit assumptions and institutions capable of checking the results. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X01" aria-label="Source X01">X01</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup>

Here, the **burden of proof** means that requirement for public evaluation. An access mandate should be specific enough to describe, compare with available alternatives and reassess in operation. This is the final instalment’s editorial proposition, informed by American, European and Chinese documents. It applies to the benefits sought as well as the risks others would bear.

## From reported obstacles to investigative outcomes

The FBI began accepting LADC submissions on 1 January 2024. Its launch bulletin reported the absence of a nationally representative count. The original methodology described voluntary, incident-based submissions containing information about devices and access attempts. The 2026 notices confirm voluntary participation and, crucially, warn about its limited extent. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X02" aria-label="Source X02">X02</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X01" aria-label="Source X01">X01</a></sup>

That limitation changes how a figure should be read. Agencies best equipped to report may also handle the most complex cases. One case can involve several devices. A technical obstacle might delay an investigation, leave a question unanswered or be overcome through other evidence. Diagnosis requires the counting unit, the collection’s coverage and what subsequently happened to the case. The US Congress’s audit institution, the Government Accountability Office (GAO), frames data reliability in its guide through accuracy, completeness and suitability for the intended use. We apply that method here; the GAO document does not audit LADC. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X06" aria-label="Source X06">X06</a></sup>

An American precedent explains the concern. In a note appended to a 2017 encryption speech, the Justice Department acknowledges that a figure of **7,800 devices** had been cited incorrectly because of an error in FBI methodology. The corrected number would be substantially lower, it says, without supplying it. The page does not establish when the correction was inserted. A number can acquire considerable influence before its construction is settled. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X03" aria-label="Source X03">X03</a></sup>

The decisive measure would be the **additional benefit attributable to a specified policy**. Consider a category of investigations defined in advance: comparable offences and deadlines, documented resources. Would a new capability deliver useful information sooner? Would it change a decision, corroborate a lead or clear someone of suspicion? How much would existing means have achieved? These questions extend the National Academies’ evaluation framework, which separates inaccessible devices from their implications for investigations. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup>

The comparison will be imperfect. Cases differ, agencies learn and targets may change their behaviour. A credible evaluation must explain how it handles those differences. It could use anonymized retrospective case reviews, controlled technical tests and independent scrutiny of assumptions. The appropriate method depends on the proposed system and legal constraints.



## The strongest case for access

The content of a conversation can change the course of an investigation. In a contribution published on 4 May 2026, France’s Parliamentary Delegation for Intelligence argues that message content can be essential to establish the role of an organizer or a more distant accomplice. Connections between correspondents do not always reveal what their instructions mean. The delegation says targeted computer-data collection requires time, expertise and resources that constrain its use, particularly in emergencies. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X11" aria-label="Source X11">X11</a></sup>

This is a substantive argument. Information that arrives after an operational deadline may lose much of its value. An assessment confined to computing costs would miss the harm associated with a delayed investigation or a poorly understood threat. The National Academies’ framework explicitly considers whether access is available soon enough and at the required scale. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup>

Part of the delegation’s underlying work is classified. Its public contribution supplies no sample from which to estimate how many additional cases a particular architecture would resolve. It identifies an operational need and advocates targeted, supervised access. Turning that need into policy still requires a description of the capability, its intended users and its scope. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X11" aria-label="Source X11">X11</a></sup>

Precision also improves the debate. Obtaining data already held by a provider, accessing a device and changing a service to make content accessible involve different technical arrangements. The word “access” leaves those differences unresolved. The National Academies recommend assessing each proposal through its capabilities, security implications, effects on rights, costs and international context. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup>

## Testing the whole system

Assessing a proposal means following authorization as closely as cryptography. Who can initiate access? Which devices and software versions are covered? How would the system detect an erroneous order, a compromised administrator account or use beyond the authorized scope? What oversight remains as the service changes? Integration and operation need examining alongside the promised capability. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X08" aria-label="Source X08">X08</a></sup>

The cryptographic-module validation FAQ from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) provides a precise reference point. A module is evaluated within a defined [validation boundary](/en/glossary/perimetre-de-validation/). Embedding it in a product does not automatically validate the product or its use of the module. Validation remains valuable within that scope. Our inference is that an access mechanism also requires evaluation of the surrounding system: authorization decisions, audit records and maintenance procedures. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X05" aria-label="Source X05">X05</a></sup>

The European Data Protection Board made a related demand in November 2024: specify and assess concrete technical measures to determine their necessity, proportionality and effectiveness. It observes that a legally targeted intervention can require a technical change with broader reach. It calls for objective evidence, including relevant statistics, while recognizing the concern that certain offences may go unpunished. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X08" aria-label="Source X08">X08</a></sup>

The European timetable shows why this work needs to be documented. A June 2025 Commission communication envisaged a technology roadmap on encryption. A January 2026 Council note expected results in the fourth quarter of 2026, following an initial second-quarter target. By 7 November, we had found no public confirmation of final publication. A policy deadline describes the work programme; the technical case depends on the results available for examination. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X09" aria-label="Source X09">X09</a></sup>

## The construction of the $250.6 billion AES estimate

The economics of encryption offers an instructive example. A study commissioned by NIST and published in September 2018 assessed the programme behind AES, an encryption standard. It estimated **$250.6 billion in gross benefits**, accumulated from 1998 to 2017 in constant 2017 dollars. The estimate concerns the AES programme over 1996–2017. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X19" aria-label="Source X19">X19</a></sup>

Understanding the result requires examining its comparison scenario. The authors estimated development, adoption and interoperability costs that organizations would have incurred without NIST’s programme. Their [counterfactual](/en/glossary/contrefactuel/) describes the industry’s path towards an alternative standard. This alternative history permits an estimate of avoided costs, while requiring assumptions about companies’ choices and timing. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X19" aria-label="Source X19">X19</a></sup>

The survey supplied information for direct estimates from **74 respondents**. A model supplied estimates for **another 95**, bringing the initial group to **169**. The authors then extrapolated to selected sectors using respondents’ share of employment, retaining sectors with at least ten respondents. Past benefits were accumulated to 2017 at a **7% real annual rate**. Each stage adds an assumption to the calculation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X19" aria-label="Source X19">X19</a></sup>

<EncryptionProofFigure lang="en" kind="estimate" />

The uncertainty appendix matters as much as the headline figure. The authors acknowledge that uncertainty in the initial estimates, which they consider the most important, cannot be quantified. Benefits could be much lower or higher. Describing the result as a “minimum” reflects excluded benefit categories and sectors; it does not establish a guaranteed statistical lower bound. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X19" aria-label="Source X19">X19</a></sup>

The methodological lesson is useful: shared infrastructure can save organizations from rebuilding the same solutions, but estimating that benefit requires a plausible replacement scenario. The AES total remains tied to this scenario, period and survey. Applying an arbitrary percentage to it to price a backdoor would produce an unsupported number.

A specified access mandate would require the same effort of definition: the changes imposed, resources consumed and events capable of causing harm. Design costs can be documented through quotations and working time. Expected losses also require assumptions about incident probabilities and severity. When those parameters are unknown, an explicitly hypothetical sensitivity analysis can show what the result depends on. Presenting the resulting total as observed would mislead.

## The value people place on confidentiality

A survey by the European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA), published on 11 May 2026, supplies a different kind of evidence. Between 6 and 30 November 2025, **26,453 people aged 15 and over** across the European Union answered questions about private online communications. Across all respondents, including non-users, **77%** considered encryption very or fairly important. The results are weighted. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X07" aria-label="Source X07">X07</a></sup>

The concern question specifies different actors and access without the person’s knowledge or permission. In France, 64% expressed concern about people engaged in criminal activity, 50% about companies and 33% about law enforcement. The figure presents all six categories on a consistent basis alongside the EU aggregate, which includes France. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X07" aria-label="Source X07">X07</a></sup>

<EncryptionProofFigure lang="en" kind="trust" />

These answers describe expectations and concerns. They establish neither willingness to pay nor a view on a defined judicial-access mandate. Someone can value confidentiality highly and approve of certain forms of access under conditions. Differences of a few percentage points between France and the EU remain descriptive: the report reviewed supplies no confidence intervals for these comparisons. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X07" aria-label="Source X07">X07</a></sup>

An economic claim therefore needs another step. To establish that a particular architecture would cost a provider customers, an analysis would have to observe or estimate their response to that architecture against a credible comparison. The survey supplies no such elasticity. It helps identify the safeguards people may expect, a question that must then be connected to purchasing decisions, usage and rights.

The distribution of consequences creates another difficulty. In 2006, Ross Anderson and Tyler Moore explained how security incentives become distorted when the actor choosing protection differs from the actor bearing the loss. Applied here, their framework suggests tracing separately the authority demanding a capability, the company building it, the agencies using it and the people whose data could be exposed. This is an analytical application, without an estimate of current losses. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X20" aria-label="Source X20">X20</a></sup>

Public reimbursement can shift a bill between a provider and taxpayers. The working hours are still consumed. Compensation for victims, if available, follows the harm. Distinguishing financial transfers from resources used helps separate the government budget, company spending and costs to society. It also raises the question of who would fund continuing risk reduction. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X20" aria-label="Source X20">X20</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup>

## Safeguards need staff

France’s National Commission for the Control of Intelligence Techniques (CNCTR) gives that question a practical form in its 2025 annual report. It describes more technical work with unchanged resources and considers itself at its capacity limit. Its scrutiny follows data through collection, use, traceability and destruction. This assessment belongs to intelligence oversight and its particular legal framework; it illustrates the human work behind a safeguard. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X10" aria-label="Source X10">X10</a></sup>

The CNCTR reports 120 inspections and 100,813 requests for individual domestic intelligence techniques in 2025. Dividing one by the other would create a meaningless coverage rate: an inspection can examine several techniques, while requests include renewals. The economic question lies in the depth of scrutiny, the access granted to inspectors and the expertise required. The commission also observes that closer examination can uncover more anomalies. Any rise in detected problems must be interpreted alongside the intensity of oversight. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X10" aria-label="Source X10">X10</a></sup>

The FRA’s October 2026 report on data protection in policing and justice adds another perspective. Interviewees describe needs for training, expertise and resources among those responsible for safeguards. This qualitative research comprises 132 interviews conducted between January and July 2025. It supplies no representative non-compliance rate. No operational police or judicial interview was secured in France; the European findings retain that limitation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X13" aria-label="Source X13">X13</a></sup>

China supplies another documentary example, in a different field: compliance in the use of cryptography. Its 2023 regulations define commercial cryptography through the protection and authentication of information outside the state-secrets category. They call for coordination between assessments to avoid duplication. That requirement establishes an organizational objective; savings achieved would still need to be measured. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X15" aria-label="Source X15">X15</a></sup>

New rules for the relevant electronic authentication providers took effect on 1 July 2026. They require compliance assessment at least annually, remediation and submission of the report to the competent authority. Relevant technical and security-management staff must receive at least **20 hours of training each year**. Administrative inspections are free of charge. Both provisions can apply together: an inspection without a fee leaves the operator with duties that consume working time. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X14" aria-label="Source X14">X14</a></sup>



Other texts describe the oversight process. For certain important networks and systems subject to commercial-cryptography requirements, assessment begins at design, continues before operation and recurs at least annually. Self-assessment is permitted under conditions. Testing bodies face independence and record-retention requirements; failure in certain checks requires remediation and suspension of the affected testing activity. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X16" aria-label="Source X16">X16</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X17" aria-label="Source X17">X17</a></sup>

A Beijing notice dated 21 January 2026 adds a detail that matters for information quality: results must be filed even when the assessment fails, within thirty days of the report’s completion. This gives the administration a channel for receiving adverse results. Filing alone establishes nothing about public disclosure, completeness of reporting or effective correction of defects. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X18" aria-label="Source X18">X18</a></sup>

These Chinese documents describe security duties within defined fields. They establish no general ability to decrypt communications. Their comparative value lies in the recurring work of oversight: conduct an assessment, preserve records, address failures and repeat the process. Safeguards, rights and institutional independence remain specific to each regime. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X14" aria-label="Source X14">X14</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X16" aria-label="Source X16">X16</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X17" aria-label="Source X17">X17</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X18" aria-label="Source X18">X18</a></sup>

## Build in a chance to reconsider

A European precedent shows how monitoring can be written into legislation. The electronic-evidence regulation requires statistics on orders, execution, refusals, timing, remedies, and costs claimed or reimbursed. Evaluation is due by **18 August 2029**. The regulation concerns production of data and expressly creates no duty to decrypt. Its monitoring arrangements are useful here as a precedent from a field distinct from exceptional access. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X12" aria-label="Source X12">X12</a></sup>

Such statistics can describe activity and some costs. Assessing another policy’s additional benefit would require evidence of its actual usefulness and the alternatives available. Evaluation would also need to consider incidents, disputed uses and consequences for people outside the investigations. Continuing scrutiny is part of the National Academies’ framework. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup>

Drawing on these documents, l0g proposes a decision rule: **match the breadth of an obligation to the strength of the evidence, then arrange for reassessment**. That begins with a technical description precise enough for independent examination. It requires a documented need, comparison with other means and analysis of who bears the risk.

Funding safeguards belongs in the initial decision. Appointing an oversight body gives it a task; access, expertise and resources enable it to carry that task out. Procedures for correcting, suspending or withdrawing a system should also be prepared before deployment. A change in the product or the threat can alter the original balance. This is our recommendation, informed by the monitoring and evaluation requirements in the sources. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X04" aria-label="Source X04">X04</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X08" aria-label="Source X08">X08</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-X10" aria-label="Source X10">X10</a></sup>

<EncryptionProofFigure lang="en" kind="review" />

The public record examined here documents investigative needs, risk mechanisms, duties and some resources committed. It leaves open the causal benefit of an architecture yet to be specified and its full social cost. A useful number first requires a defined proposal, comparison scenario, time horizon and affected population.

Advocates of a new access capability must demonstrate its contribution and safeguards. Those proposing to forgo it must examine the cost of lost information and the actual reach of alternatives. Serious public decision-making makes both arguments available for scrutiny, with their assumptions and limits. It then equips itself to recognize a fulfilled promise, an unnecessary expense or a risk that has become too great.
