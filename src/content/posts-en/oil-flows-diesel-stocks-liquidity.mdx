On 20 October, Reuters reported that two US Department of Energy officials were working with refiners to increase diesel production. The request followed a quarter in which US refineries had already operated at an average 95% of capacity, according to the Energy Information Administration. Together, those facts capture the difficulty facing the oil market: returning crude cargoes help replenish supply, while the availability of usable fuel still depends on refinery operations, shipping and delivery schedules. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a>, <a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Following that chain changes the way oil risk looks. A barrel can exist, have a buyer and count towards global inventories while remaining far from the refinery or depot that needs it. Its price can be hedged, even as its owner must finance the voyage and meet [margin calls](/en/glossary/appel-de-marge/). The owner may have to fund hedge payments before the cargo reaches its buyer and generates cash.

This investigation brings together public material from the International Energy Agency (IEA), the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), emergency-reserve announcements and the rules governing hedging markets. Its evidence cut-off is 21 October 2026, before the US weekly release scheduled for later that day. Observations, forecasts and illustrative calculations are identified throughout. Full source URLs, relevant passages and access details appear at the end.

## Inventories are changing location

The IEA’s public report of 14 October offers a revealing starting point. In September, observed on-land inventories fell by **113 million barrels**, while oil on water rose by **103 million barrels**. The net change in observed global stocks was therefore a decline of **10 million barrels**. The IEA associates the increase at sea with a rebound in exports. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

For the eventual buyer, the location of those stocks matters. Material already held in a depot can respond relatively quickly to an order, subject to its specification and the available distribution network. A cargo at sea must complete its voyage, secure a discharge slot and enter the industrial supply chain. Adding both categories is useful for measuring observed inventories; assessing immediate pressure also requires knowing where the barrels are.

The figures do not identify 103 million barrels as stranded, or trace individual barrels from onshore tanks to ships. More cargoes at sea can reflect higher departures, longer journeys or a combination of the two. The aggregate publication does not separate those contributions. That distinction matters because additional oil in transit may be preparing the ground for an improvement in supply, even while usable stocks at destination are temporarily falling. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

A simple example illustrates the role of time. Imagine a route carrying a sustained one million barrels a day. With a twenty-day transit, roughly twenty million barrels would be at sea. At thirty-five days, the same steady flow would require thirty-five million barrels in transit. The extra fifteen million would occupy ships and tie up financing. During the transition between the two steady states, arrivals could be delayed before the new pattern settles. This is an illustration of the relationship between throughput, transit time and inventory, rather than a measurement of any route in October.

The EIA describes this type of pressure in its 6 October outlook: longer routes reduce vessel availability and, alongside insurance costs, raise the cost of delivered supplies. A fall in the price of crude at origin can consequently be partly absorbed by a more expensive voyage. For a refinery, the relevant input cost includes getting the feedstock to its plant. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Maritime reporting needs similar care. In its 18 October advisory, the Joint Maritime Information Center said claims that two supertankers had struck mines on 17 October were unsubstantiated. Treating those claims as confirmed losses would insert an unproven supply disruption into the analysis. Security reports, vessel positions and discharged volumes provide different kinds of evidence, each with its own limits. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

## Refining has its own pace

Once crude arrives, it must still become usable fuel. A refinery separates petroleum into fractions, converts some molecules and treats the resulting products to meet the required specifications. Its equipment and the composition of its feedstock shape the range of outputs it can produce. Diesel is one part of that connected production process; increasing its share depends on operational adjustments and available conversion capacity. The EIA’s technical description explains why an additional barrel of crude does not automatically yield an additional barrel of diesel. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

Trade data also show a gap between crude supply and product availability. The IEA puts Middle Eastern refined-product exports at **1.9 million barrels a day in September 2026**, compared with **4.7 million before the war**, a calculated decline of about 60%. The aggregate includes several products, and the public summary does not specify the exact month used for the pre-war comparison. It indicates a substantial reduction in regional product supply without, on its own, measuring the global diesel shortfall. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

US refinery utilisation averaging 95% in the third quarter helps explain the scope for a response. Plants already running intensively have less room to compensate quickly for disruption elsewhere. The national percentage measures the use of refining capacity across different sites. The remaining five percentage points cannot simply be treated as immediately available diesel capacity. Estimating that would require information about the units involved, their condition, maintenance requirements and suitable crude supplies. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-S20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

High diesel values can encourage refiners to adjust their output, and additional supply can eventually ease the market. Industrial changes nevertheless take time to turn a price signal into delivered fuel. Risk emerges during that interval when local inventories have to bridge the gap.

## Diesel can rise while crude falls

Price series make the divergence visible beyond the daily Brent headlines. Between June and July 2026, the EIA’s monthly average physical Brent price fell from **$85.40 to $83.76 a barrel**, a calculated decline of **1.9%**. Over the same period, ultra-low-sulphur diesel quoted in New York rose from **$3.401 to $3.913 a gallon**, an increase of **15.1%**. The two markets moved in opposite directions. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

To make the levels comparable, the chart converts diesel into dollars per barrel using 42 US gallons to the petroleum barrel. In September, the diesel average was therefore **$208.15 a barrel**, against **$114.16 for Brent**, giving a difference of approximately **$93.99**. All nine months of available 2026 observations are displayed to place the summer movement in a broader context. These are monthly averages of physical spot quotations published by the EIA using Refinitiv data, rather than futures prices. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

The gap indicates the relative value of the refined product, but it needs careful interpretation. The crude and diesel quotations refer to different markets and locations. The comparison does not follow a single cargo from its purchase through the sale of all the products made from it. It also excludes refinery energy use, operating costs, freight and other components of revenue. Treating the $93.99 difference as a refinery’s net profit would therefore misrepresent its economics. The EIA and CME explain crude–product price differences as gross margin indicators whose conventions need to be specified. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a>, <a href="#source-S16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

There is a practical consequence for businesses buying diesel. A hedge linked only to crude can leave them exposed to changes in that differential. Compensation may cover too little of the increase in the fuel bill; in a scenario where crude falls while diesel rises, a long crude hedge can itself lose value as the bill grows. Product quality, delivery location and timing can introduce further differences between the instrument and the actual purchase. This exposure to a mismatch, commonly called [basis risk](/en/glossary/risque-de-base/), depends on the hedge contract as well as the physical market. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

Retail prices add more stages: distributors’ earlier purchases, transport, storage, taxes and commercial terms. Wholesale series help explain the pressure moving through the supply chain. Converting a one-dollar movement in Brent into an immediate, fixed change at the pump would require assumptions that those series alone cannot support. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

## Winter inventories are built locally

US inventory data provide a second test, this time of available quantities. In the 15 October release, covering the week ended 9 October, US [distillate](/en/glossary/distillats/) stocks stood at **102.611 million barrels**, compared with **117.030 million** in the corresponding reporting week of 2025, a decline of **12.3%**. The EIA notes that the East Coast consumes more distillate than it produces and relies on supplies from the US Gulf Coast and on imports. More expensive or less reliable deliveries make the regional inventory buffer more significant. A relatively better national supply position can coexist with a local difficulty if the product fails to reach the right depot at the right time. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The distillate category in these tables includes diesel and heating oil across several sulphur categories, making it broader than road diesel alone. The chart matches the same ordinal reporting weeks of 2025 and 2026, from January to early October. Its final pair compares Friday 9 October 2026 with Friday 10 October 2025. It makes no adjustment for weather, demand or capacity changes. The annual decline is an inventory observation, and the contribution of each disruption cannot be inferred from these series alone. The same discipline is needed when discussing days of supply: a stock measured in barrels can only become a measure of time after selecting a relevant consumption rate and a consistent scope.

The underlying supply mechanism is straightforward. Production and incoming deliveries must cover outflows while allowing the available buffer to rebuild. Higher production can be absorbed by stronger exports or demand before it appears as additional stocks. The EIA describes this interaction between production, trade and inventories in its diesel analysis. Monitoring refinery operations alone would consequently miss part of the pressure. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

## A hedged cargo can still need cash

Transit time also has a financial expression. Consider an **entirely hypothetical example**. A trader buys and pays for one million barrels at $100 each, committing $100 million. At the same time, the trader sells an equivalent quantity of futures at $100 to hedge a fall in the inventory’s value. Assume that the cargo and hedge match perfectly, with no costs, initial margin or delivery risk.

During the voyage, both prices rise to $120. The cargo is now worth $120 million, although it has yet to be sold. The short futures position, meanwhile, accumulates a $20 million loss that is settled in cash through [variation margin](/en/glossary/marge-de-variation/). The trader must finance **an additional $20 million** while the gain on the physical cargo remains unrealised. When the cargo is sold, the $120 million receipt, less the $20 million paid on the hedge, leaves economic proceeds of $100 million before costs. The model fixes the selling price while retaining an interim liquidity requirement.

Cash reserves, a bank facility or assets that can be used to raise funding can help bridge the mismatch. Their availability, cost and conditions then matter alongside the expected return on the transaction. In an actual trade, physical and futures prices can also diverge, bringing back the basis risk discussed earlier. The quality of a hedge therefore has to be considered together with the ability to fund it until settlement. Even the basic carrying cost gives a sense of the importance of time. At a hypothetical annual rate of 6%, keeping $100 million financed for an extra fifteen days costs approximately **$247,000**, using a 365-day year. This calculation excludes the financing of margin calls, compounding, freight and insurance. It illustrates a timing cost without estimating any observed voyage or credit facility.

## From reserve announcements to delivered fuel

Emergency reserves call for the same attention to product and timing. Our analysis of [crude and diesel reserves](/en/analysis/oil-reserves-crude-diesel-contents-delivery/#refining-determines-which-fuel-becomes-available) explains the industrial steps between storage and delivery. The G7’s 2 October statement refers to **100 million barrels in total over four months**, including an accelerated diesel component in the first twenty days. A White House fact sheet published on 5 October instead describes an agreement for **100 million barrels of refined diesel from European strategic reserves**. The two formulations use different product scopes. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a>, <a href="#source-S12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

The IEA’s 15 October statement says around 100 million barrels remain to be released under the collective action, with plans including **more than 20 million barrels of diesel** as soon as possible. That diesel volume is a component of the remaining total and a lower bound, since the statement says “over”. Treating it as a ceiling, or adding it to the 100 million barrels, would change the meaning of the announcement. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup>

The public material examined for this investigation contains no confirmation of a separate agreement covering 100 million barrels of European diesel. That gap does not establish the authors’ intentions. It does require preserving the scope of each statement and showing readers the documentary discrepancy rather than treating the broadest formulation as an established quantity.

The economic value of a reserve release then depends on the product, its location and the actual delivery schedule. Crude still needs processing. Finished diesel can address a depot’s requirement more directly, provided transport and specifications permit. A four-month announcement, by itself, says little about the rate of deliveries in any particular week. The IEA also refers to national circumstances in implementing releases. Assessing their effect on market tightness requires tracking actual withdrawals and destinations as well as the announced total. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a>, <a href="#source-S20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

## The conditions for relief can be tracked

Several developments could improve the balance. OFAC’s General License 135, dated 9 October, authorises certain transactions involving the sale, delivery, offloading and import of Russian-origin diesel until 12:01 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time on 7 April 2027, subject to its terms. It creates legal room for trade without determining how much fuel is produced or shipped. On 20 October, Reuters reported Russian cargo departures signalling Turkey and Brazil as destinations. That evidence documents renewed movements; the indicated destinations do not yet establish completed discharges. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a>, <a href="#source-S21" aria-label="Source 21">21</a></sup>

The EIA’s October outlook also envisages a gradual improvement in supply and a slower drawdown of global inventories, while high prices weigh on demand. This is a forecast based on information available at the beginning of the month. Repairs, more normal shipping routes and changes in consumption could therefore relieve pressure, with their timing and execution determining how quickly the effect reaches buyers. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Four developments would be especially informative: arrivals of refined products that lead to a sustained rebuilding of regional inventories; a narrowing of crude–diesel price differences using consistent locations and conventions; lower delivered costs and shorter delivery times; and reserve withdrawals documented by product and destination. Taken together, they would provide stronger evidence of relief than an isolated move in Brent or a report of departing ships.

The vulnerability most clearly supported by the evidence is the need to synchronise the supply chain. More cargoes at sea can prepare the way for improvement, while refining and local inventories determine how that improvement reaches users. Financial hedges accompany the journey with payment schedules of their own. Following the product, its location and the date at which it becomes available gives an industrial buyer or lender a more useful picture of oil risk. Our investigation of [energy bills and their financing](/en/analysis/dollar-facture-energie/#a-protected-price-can-still-require-cash-before-delivery) adds the role of exchange rates.
