*When funds buy insurers · Article 4/8 · Documentary investigation as of 23 October 2026*

**At Apollo, Brookfield and KKR, insurance provides a durable source of capital that can also finance businesses within the same corporate family. Their accounts reveal several of these connections, provided we distinguish the manager choosing the investments, the vehicle holding them and the company responsible for repayment.**

Following the investigations into [insurer acquisitions](/en/analysis/why-investment-firms-buy-insurers/) and the [Jackson–Athene treaty](/en/analysis/bermuda-reinsurance-jackson-athene/), this fourth article traces investments between related parties through to the borrower and the transaction price.

On 7 May 2026, a subsidiary of Brookfield Infrastructure Partners refinanced a $450 million loan with subsidiaries of Brookfield Wealth Solutions, the insurance group within the wider Brookfield organisation. The transaction also appears in the accounts of American National Group Inc. and its subsidiaries, which sit within that insurance business. Lender and borrower thus publish two parts of the same story: an insurance group holds a claim on businesses in the Brookfield family, carrying a fixed interest rate of 6.33% and a final maturity in 2036. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a>, <a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

This is a tangible connection between a promise to a policyholder and the financing of a business. The insurer invests its resources to meet future benefits; a company borrows and agrees to pay interest. Their place within the same wider organisation raises a further question: who protects the insurer’s terms when the financing also serves the interests of its shareholder or business partners?

That connection can take very different forms. An insurer may pay its manager to buy a bond issued by an independent company, invest in a fund organised by that manager, acquire an asset sold by a related company, or lend directly to its parent. The reports examined at Athene, Global Atlantic and Brookfield describe each of these situations. Putting them all behind a single arrow would obscure the essential information: whose ability to pay determines repayment. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a>, <a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a>, <a href="#source-s12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

## One label, several destinations

The term “[related party](/en/glossary/partie-liee/)” sounds as though it identifies a destination. It actually identifies a relationship, whose nature still needs to be established. US reporting rules distinguish, among other things, control, a connection to the issuer, an affiliated company’s role in arranging an investment and the involvement of a related manager. These disclosures help identify potential conflicts even when the underlying claims are on businesses outside the group. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s23" aria-label="Source 23">23</a>, <a href="#source-s24" aria-label="Source 24">24</a>, <a href="#source-s21" aria-label="Source 21">21</a></sup>

Athene, part of Apollo, provides an especially useful breakdown. At 30 June 2026, it classified **$44.025 billion of net invested assets** as related party, within a net portfolio of $314.090 billion. The largest category, at $24.778 billion, consists of securitisations of unaffiliated assets managed by Apollo or a related platform. A [securitisation](/en/glossary/abs/) pools claims in a vehicle that issues securities for investors to buy; Athene explains that, in this category, the collateral, borrower or other obligor is generally unaffiliated with Athene. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The manager therefore sits at the centre of the decisions and fees, while payment comes from the assets being financed. The reader needs to look through the vehicle: which companies pay the interest, which properties generate the rent, which claims supply the repayments? The Apollo connection may matter for asset selection, fees and the choices made along the way; credit quality also depends on those ultimate borrowers.

Athene’s other three categories comprise $8.468 billion invested in Apollo funds, $7.061 billion in platforms that arrange or provide financing, and $3.718 billion in retirement and insurance platforms. Together, they amount to $19.247 billion. A fund interest provides exposure to a portfolio; an equity stake bears the results of a business; a debt security confers a right to payment according to its seniority and security. This classification gives the investigation a starting point for tracing exposures, separating manager relationships from direct loans to Apollo. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The platform disclosures make the distinction more concrete. At the end of 2025, Athene described its MidCap Financial exposure as including investments through funds and fixed-income securities. MidCap may also originate or source loans to which Athene gains exposure by buying securities issued by MidCap affiliates, retain some of the origination fees paid by borrowers and act as agent for the lenders. A single business relationship can therefore bring together invested equity, credit and paid services. Understanding what the insurer earns requires following each role and each payment to its recipient. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The qualification “net invested assets” matters here. Athene also publishes a broader presentation of related-party investments under US generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) totalling $70.616 billion. For its net measure, the company looks through funds-withheld reinsurance portfolios to the underlying assets, adjusts consolidated vehicles and includes its economic share of ACRA investments while excluding other investors’ share. Valuation bases also differ. The $70.616 billion and $44.025 billion figures therefore answer different questions about the scope of the accounts; the overall investment reconciliation describes these adjustments across the portfolio. Our chart uses the four components of the same net measure, whose sum can be checked. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## A mandate gives discretion; a purchase transfers rights

An investment mandate gives a manager authority to choose investments. Its size indicates the reach of that service. Brookfield Asset Management, or BAM, reported $150 billion of fee-bearing capital from Brookfield Wealth Solutions at the end of June 2026, divided among liquid credit, non-fund private investment and private funds. This breakdown measures the asset-management business. Identifying the insurers’ borrowers requires tracing the securities held and the assets financed. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a>, <a href="#source-s12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

The legal structure deserves as much attention as the name above the door. In the June 2026 accounts, BAM is the manager and BWS the insurance group. Brookfield Corporation accounts for its BWS investment using the equity method and holds all its Class C shares, which carry the residual economic interest after distributions to Class A and B shareholders. BAM’s management relationship with BWS is therefore distinct from Brookfield Corporation’s economic rights. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

Buying an interest in a fund creates another relationship. The insurer acquires an economic interest in the vehicle’s investments, while the manager continues to oversee their selection and monitoring. If the interest is purchased from a group company, that company is also the seller; the transfer price then determines how value is divided between two related parties.

Global Atlantic supplies a dated example. In January 2024, it bought minority interests in two funds from its ultimate parent, KKR: $555 million in Diversified Core Infrastructure Fund and $353 million in KKR Property Partners Americas. The published purchase prices therefore total **$908 million**. The transaction transferred interests already held by KKR; the note reviewed does not specify how the purchase was settled. Describing the price as a management fee, or as a new subscription invested entirely in projects, would change the nature of the transaction. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup>

Transfers can also run in a different direction. In the first half of 2026, Global Atlantic received $247 million of capital contributions from its parent in the form of interests in debt securities and funds, recorded at fair value on contribution. These assets entered its accounts through a capital transaction. Their subsequent appearance in a related-party portfolio is therefore insufficient evidence of a cash outflow from the insurer to KKR. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

These two events, more than two years apart, show why a balance-sheet snapshot needs the history of the asset alongside it. A holding may originate in a purchase, a capital contribution or a restructuring. Assessing the 2024 price would require the valuations and terms offered to comparable third parties; assessing the 2026 contribution would require examining the assets received, their valuation and their ability to generate the expected cash flows. The public notes establish the transactions and their accounting treatment, while leaving that economic assessment to further investigation.

## A loan traced from both ends

The Brookfield refinancing takes us further in identifying the credit relationship. In its June 2026 accounts, American National Group Inc., or ANGI, reported approximately $3.8 billion of private loans to Brookfield subsidiaries. BWS, which indirectly includes ANGI, reported approximately $4.1 billion. These are overlapping scopes: the second figure covers a larger group containing the first. The $450 million loan cited in both reports is one named example of these relationships. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a>, <a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

The accounts of Brookfield Infrastructure Partners, or BIP, specify the refinancing terms: fixed interest of 6.33% a year, an anticipated repayment date of 7 May 2031 and a final maturity of 7 May 2036. They state that the loan is non-recourse to the BIP partnership and describe it as having been agreed at arm’s length on market terms. That last assessment is the borrower’s published position; the documents collected contain neither competing offers nor an independent valuation that would allow it to be verified for this transaction. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

The absence of recourse to BIP marks a useful boundary. The group name may suggest a shared pool of financial strength, but the creditor must exercise the contractual rights it holds against the borrowers and any guarantees or security supporting the loan. Readers cannot assume that the partnership would repay on their behalf. The notes reviewed leave the individual signatory subsidiaries, the debt’s seniority, its security and the precise rights attached to the anticipated 2031 repayment date still to be established. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a>, <a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

The word “refinancing” prevents another misreading. The transaction concerns an existing loan; its $450 million amount does not automatically represent an equivalent additional outflow during the half-year. Measuring the net movement in cash would require reconciling repayment of the old financing, funding of the new loan and any fees. A transaction amount, a closing balance and money collected from policyholders describe three different stages.

Risk analysis then begins with the borrower. If its income can cover debt service over time, the insurer may secure an investment suited to its liabilities. If that income weakens, the loan’s value depends on the assets, its seniority, the security and negotiations with other creditors. A shared corporate affiliation may make information easier to obtain and financing easier to arrange; it may also complicate the defence of the lender’s interests when other companies in the family hold competing positions.

BWS acknowledges this possibility in its annual report. Different Brookfield accounts may occupy different levels of the same company’s capital structure, one as creditor and another as shareholder. The group describes position limits and, in certain circumstances, a passive strategy that leaves tranche decisions to the third-party investors controlling it or an independent agent acting for its investors. Establishing the procedure used for this loan would require the decision specific to the transaction. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

## A claim belongs to a particular company

Athene discloses an even more direct relationship: **its holding company, AHL, had a $279 million receivable from Apollo Global Management** at 30 June 2026. The loan sits within a $500 million unsecured [revolving credit facility](/en/glossary/credit-renouvelable/). It carries the applicable US federal mid-term rate and matures on 13 December 2028, or earlier if AHL requests repayment. A facility in the opposite direction, allowing Athene to borrow from Apollo, had no outstanding balance at that date. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Here, the parent company is the borrower, and the facility’s contractual capacity is distinct from the amount actually lent. The identified creditor is a holding company: the documents do not trace the $279 million back to an individual insurance contract, or establish that it can be added to the $44.025 billion of net related-party investments without a specific reconciliation. It deserves its own line on the map, naming both companies and showing the direction of the debt. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

At Apollo group level, internal balances and transactions are eliminated on [consolidation](/en/glossary/perimetre-de-consolidation/), as its accounting policy explains. This presentation shows the group as a whole in its dealings with the outside world. It can, however, obscure an individual company’s liquidity needs for a reader moving too quickly through the accounts: in that company’s own accounts, a claim on its parent remains a right to payment dependent on a borrower. Consolidation does not replace the contract governing that payment. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The same change of scope matters for revenue. A fee charged by the manager to the insurance business is income for one and an expense for the other; simply adding their separate accounts does not measure group profit. Within a fund, further charges may affect the return reaching the insurer. A revenue map therefore needs to identify the payer, recipient, vehicle and accounting eliminations. It lays the groundwork for investigating how returns are shared, without inferring that division from assets under management alone. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup>

## The US affiliated-investment universe: $321 billion

A publication from the NAIC, the association of US state insurance regulators, places these cases in a wider context. Published in September 2026, it identifies approximately **$321 billion of affiliated investments held by US life insurers at the end of 2025**, or roughly 5% of their cash and invested assets. The data cover four reporting schedules and encompass several types of securities and ownership interests. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s22" aria-label="Source 22">22</a></sup>

Within that same scope, approximately **28% is reported as credit exposure to an affiliate**. The difference between this figure and the total demonstrates the value of the distinction followed throughout this article. The relationship criteria used to monitor a portfolio extend beyond debt owed by a related company. The remaining 72% may still carry economic, governance or management dependencies that this statistic does not quantify; presenting it as free of all group-related risk would be equally misleading. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s22" aria-label="Source 22">22</a></sup>

Concentration also deserves attention. According to the NAIC, ten insurers with a “PE relationship” account for almost half of total affiliated investments. The report does not name those ten companies or provide a complete operational definition of that category. It therefore provides no basis for attributing the total to Apollo, Brookfield and KKR, or for directly comparing its $321 billion with the three groups’ global consolidated figures. The figures retain the difference between the text’s $321 billion and the table’s $322 billion, for which the publication supplies no reconciliation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s22" aria-label="Source 22">22</a></sup>

In its letter of 24 September 2026 to Senator Elizabeth Warren, the NAIC sets out the states’ supervisory tools: detailed reporting, group supervision and prior review of certain material transactions between affiliates. It also notes the potential benefits of less liquid investments for insurers able to hold them. This framework explains how an affiliated relationship can be permitted while remaining subject to supervision; it provides no individual endorsement of the price or risk of every asset. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

## Price is the decisive test

The strongest economic argument for these structures lies in bringing the businesses together. A team that sources borrowers, structures financing and understands insurance liabilities may be able to construct investments that are difficult to obtain in the conventional bond market. Maturities and cash flows may align more closely, while reduced liquidity may command additional compensation.

A working paper published by the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago and revised on 27 April 2026 offers empirical support for part of that argument. Looking in particular at indexed annuities, it finds that major issuers use private and floating-rate investments whose shorter maturities can better match those of the products they sell. The authors also interpret their findings as evidence of better cash-flow matching. The results concern defined categories of products and portfolios; they do not certify every private loan or related-party transaction. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

A working paper entitled *Buying from the Family*, in its September 2026 version, approaches the issue from the purchase price, comparing transactions in the same securities on the same dates. Among privately placed structured securities classified as primary-market purchases, the authors estimate an additional price premium of approximately 47 cents per $100 of face value for the combination of an insurer directly majority-owned by a private equity firm and a purchase of a security issued by an affiliate. Their ownership category distinguishes direct ownership by the private equity firm from ownership through one of its buyout funds. This is a purchase-price difference estimated by regression. Its interpretation depends on the sample and the classification of primary-market transactions, inferred using an algorithm. The coefficient describes the interaction between affiliation and direct ownership, measured per $100 of face value at purchase. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s17" aria-label="Source 17">17</a></sup>

The two studies bring different evidence to the same question. One examines how a portfolio fits its liabilities; the other looks for a possible transfer of value when an asset is bought. Financing may have an appropriate maturity and still be overpriced. A related manager may also supply a good asset on competitive terms. Ownership links identify where to scrutinise the choices being made, while prices, contract terms and performance help establish their consequences.

Governance procedures therefore matter. At Athene, the functions of the former conflicts committee were transferred to the audit committee on 5 August 2025. Its policy reserves certain decisions for disinterested directors, while providing for pre-approvals and exemptions. The report also acknowledges that the committee may rely on information supplied by Apollo without independent verification. The investigation needs the decision that applied to a particular transaction, the comparables used and the insurer’s effective ability to choose another investment. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## The contracts still missing from the map

The public record establishes a specific finding: some of the resources invested within these groups finance related companies, some pass through vehicles managed within the same corporate family, and securities also move through sales and capital contributions. For each route, a different document reveals the economic responsibilities: an investment mandate, a security, a loan agreement, the vehicle’s accounts or a conflicts policy. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a>, <a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a>, <a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a>, <a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

The investigation identifies several of these connections and measures some of the balances. Detailed terms remain open for many financings: the full security package, the division of fees, contemporaneous valuations and the identity of every company holding the assets. This investigation relies on the cited publications. The groups’ positions are attributed to their authors; detailed contractual terms and independent price comparisons still to be obtained are identified in the text.

For a policyholder expecting payment in twenty years, the question becomes more concrete. Which income will support the company responsible for paying, what rights will it hold if a borrower runs into difficulty, and who will be able to decide on a restructuring? A useful map connects corporate relationships with obligations to pay, showing where interests meet and where they may diverge.
