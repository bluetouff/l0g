Jakarta’s stock market closed 11.57% lower on Friday, October 16, 2026, its steepest daily fall since January 1998, according to Reuters. The previous day in New York, MSCI had proposed removing Indonesia from its emerging-market indexes. The announcement changed the outlook for investors whose mandate is to own the securities selected by the index provider. [Reuters, October 16 market report](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/msci-weighs-downgrading-indonesia-standalone-market-status-2026-10-15/); [MSCI announcement, October 15](https://ir.msci.com/news-releases/news-release-details/msci-launches-consultation-proposal-reclassify-indonesia).

At the centre of the dispute is a deceptively simple question: who really owns the shares available for purchase? When apparently dispersed holdings remain under the influence of a single group, a market can look far more accessible than it is. An index built on that appearance may offer investors an exposure they will struggle to acquire and, eventually, to sell. That is the economic problem behind MSCI’s concerns about ownership transparency and suspected coordinated trading. [MSCI, grounds for the consultation](https://ir.msci.com/news-releases/news-release-details/msci-launches-consultation-proposal-reclassify-indonesia).

The consequences could extend well beyond the securities under scrutiny. A country-level classification decision can change the investment rules for very different companies, including those whose shares trade normally. Indonesia’s case is important because it connects a local ownership problem to global fund mandates and, potentially, to liquidity across an entire exchange. Understanding that connection requires separating losses in market value, transactions required by an index change, and capital actually leaving the country.

## MSCI’s proposed timetable

As of October 17, reclassification remains a proposal. MSCI will accept feedback until April 30, 2027, and announce its decision no later than June 30. Its direction of travel is clear: it expects to proceed unless sufficiently substantial improvements warrant reconsideration. Indonesia has been given a period in which to demonstrate progress, rather than simply a waiting period before a routine administrative decision. [MSCI announcement](https://ir.msci.com/news-releases/news-release-details/msci-launches-consultation-proposal-reclassify-indonesia); [consultation, pages 2 and 4](https://www.msci.com/downloads/documents/indexes/consultations/equity/Indonesia_Consultation_Oct_26.pdf#page=2).

The consultation also asks investors about Frontier classification as an alternative to Standalone status. This remains a question for respondents. [MSCI, questions, page 4](https://www.msci.com/downloads/documents/indexes/consultations/equity/Indonesia_Consultation_Oct_26.pdf#page=4).

One crucial date is still missing: implementation. The consultation explicitly asks investors what timetable would be appropriate and whether the change should take place in a single step. June 2027 is the announced deadline for a decision. Treating it as the date on which every affected portfolio must simultaneously liquidate its holdings would introduce a certainty absent from the document. [MSCI, consultation questions, page 4](https://www.msci.com/downloads/documents/indexes/consultations/equity/Indonesia_Consultation_Oct_26.pdf#page=4).

The proposed designation, *Standalone*, places the national index outside the relevant broad composites. Under MSCI’s classification framework, standalone markets belong to neither its Emerging Markets nor its Frontier Markets composite. A country index can continue to exist. The practical issue is that securities may leave the basket certain funds have promised to track, even while foreign investors remain legally able to trade them. [MSCI, market classification framework](https://www.msci.com/indexes/index-resources/market-classification).

MSCI’s influence comes through a reference that private contracts have made binding. A manager promising the exposure of a particular index has to adjust that exposure when the index changes its eligible universe. The force of the decision comes from the combination of classification rules and investment mandates.

## Free float is ultimately a question of control

A company may have a million shares outstanding without a million shares being available to outside investors. A controlling shareholder’s stake, or a strategic holding, generally behaves differently from an independent portfolio investment. **[Free float](/en/glossary/flottant/)** is an estimate of the portion of a company’s equity available to investors. Adjusting for it helps align an index weight with exposure that investors can realistically obtain. [MSCI, research on free-float adjustment](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/paper/free-float-adjustment-in-global-equities-a-two-decade-review).

Consider a fictional company with one million shares trading at $10. Its total market capitalisation is $10 million. The controlling shareholder officially owns 60%, with the remainder held through other accounts. If those accounts are independent, a simplified calculation puts the free float at 40%, or $4 million. Suppose, however, that half the remainder belongs to parties acting alongside the controller. Independently held float falls to 20%, worth $2 million. Separate accounts need not represent separate decision-makers.

This fictional example simplifies index methodology: inclusion factors, foreign ownership restrictions and calculation conventions can change the final figure. Its purpose is to show why raising a statutory minimum float is insufficient on its own. Information about beneficial owners and their relationships determines what the published percentage means economically.

With fewer independently held shares, a sequence of trades can have a larger effect on price. If transactions between connected participants also create the appearance of abundant activity, reported volume becomes a poor guide to the market’s ability to absorb an outside investor’s order. An index may recognise a large valuation while a fund tracking it has to buy from a shallow pool. This is a plausible mechanism, not evidence that any particular security has been manipulated.

MSCI acknowledges several reforms announced in Indonesia: disclosure of holders above 1%, more detailed investor categories, reporting of high ownership concentration and a roadmap towards a 15% minimum free float. Its concern is whether implementation is sustained and extends across the market. Publishing a threshold and establishing independent ownership solve different parts of the problem. [MSCI, assessment of announced reforms](https://ir.msci.com/news-releases/news-release-details/msci-launches-consultation-proposal-reclassify-indonesia).

## Enforcement brings the ownership problem into focus

On October 16, Indonesia’s Financial Services Authority, OJK, announced sanctions against parties involved in transactions or allocations concerning DSSA, BREN and CUAN. For the BREN and CUAN offerings, the regulator describes allocations to participants linked to the controlling shareholders, with concealed identities and concentrated ownership. In DSSA trading between 2024 and 2026, it says it identified organised market manipulation. These are the regulator’s published findings; the underlying evidence needed to reproduce its investigation independently is absent from the public materials reviewed here. [OJK statement, October 16](https://ojk.go.id/id/berita-dan-kegiatan/siaran-pers/Pages/OJK-Putuskan-Sanksi-Pihak-Terkait-Emiten-DSSA-BREN-dan-CUAN.aspx).

The announced fines amount to 1.5 trillion rupiah for the parties involved in DSSA, 500 billion for those connected to BREN allocations, and 100 billion for those connected to CUAN. Together they total **2.1 trillion rupiah**. Who owes the money matters as much as the total: the official wording targets parties involved in the transactions or allocations. It does not justify automatically charging those amounts against the three listed companies’ financial statements. [OJK, details of the sanctions](https://ojk.go.id/id/berita-dan-kegiatan/siaran-pers/Pages/OJK-Putuskan-Sanksi-Pihak-Terkait-Emiten-DSSA-BREN-dan-CUAN.aspx).

Petrindo, CUAN’s issuer, said it would cooperate with the authorities and that operations and commitments continued normally. BREN issued a comparable response, reported by detikFinance. These are company statements, rather than independently verified assessments of their financial condition. They do not resolve the ownership questions. [Petrindo’s official statement, October 16](https://petrindo.co.id/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/PRESS-RELEASE-Petrindo-Statement-16Okt2026-1.pdf); [BREN response reported by detikFinance](https://finance.detik.com/bursa-dan-valas/d-8710891/bren-cuan-buka-suara-usai-kena-sanksi-denda).

The announcements cut both ways. They give regulatory substance to the risk of apparently dispersed float concealing common control. They also supply new evidence of enforcement that the authorities can point to. Whether they are enough to change MSCI’s assessment remains open. The consultation asks for sustained improvements extending beyond the securities already identified. [MSCI, assessment criteria, page 3](https://www.msci.com/downloads/documents/indexes/consultations/equity/Indonesia_Consultation_Oct_26.pdf#page=3).

In a separate October 16 release, OJK says it respects MSCI’s independence, acknowledges areas for improvement and reaffirms its commitment to further reforms and enforcement. This is the regulator’s response; sustained effectiveness remains to be assessed. [OJK, response to MSCI](https://ojk.go.id/id/berita-dan-kegiatan/siaran-pers/Pages/OJK-Berkomitmen-untuk-Terus-Melanjutkan-dan-Memperkuat-Reformasi-Pasar-Modal-Indonesia.aspx).

## The index had already become much smaller

The consultation’s numerical appendix shows how much had changed before October’s sell-off. Between December 31, 2025, and September 30, 2026, the free-float-adjusted capitalisation of the **MSCI Indonesia Index**, its Standard basket of large and mid-cap stocks, fell from $119.14 billion to $53.36 billion. Its constituent count dropped from 18 to nine, and its weight in the MSCI Emerging Markets Index declined from 1.16% to 0.43%. [MSCI consultation, page 7](https://www.msci.com/downloads/documents/indexes/consultations/equity/Indonesia_Consultation_Oct_26.pdf#page=7).

The decline in adjusted capitalisation is 55.22%, calculated from the USD million amounts published in MSCI’s table. Prices, exchange rates, inclusion factors and changes to the basket may all contribute. Two snapshots cannot identify each contribution. The figures measure neither the capitalisation of the whole Indonesian exchange nor money withdrawn from the country. Lower prices revalue shares that have never changed hands as well as those that have traded.

The country’s weight introduces another denominator: the value of the other markets in the composite. Indonesia can lose weight because its shares fall, its index coverage shrinks, or other countries rise. The move from 1.16% to 0.43% is a decline of **0.73 percentage points**, or approximately 63% in relative terms. Those are different descriptions of the same change and must not be mixed up.

Since January, MSCI has frozen certain increases in Indonesia’s representation, including some inclusion-factor increases and additions. Deletions and adjustments can still occur. The freeze is therefore asymmetric: it restricts certain index changes while allowing others. Prices continue to move, and trading is not generally suspended by this policy. The April 20 notice documents the treatment before October’s consultation, although it cannot by itself explain every constituent’s contribution to the decline from 18 to nine names. [MSCI, April 20 announcement](https://app2.msci.com/webapp/index_ann/DocGet?format=html&lang=en&pub_key=L%2FzT8OMvlCI%3D).

## Prices also adjust index weights

This is where many accounts of passive outflows skip an important step. In a portfolio exactly replicating a free-float-adjusted capitalisation-weighted index, price movements change both the value of the holdings and their benchmark weights. A manager need not sell merely to reflect that decline. The adjustment can occur through prices alone.

Imagine a fictional $100 billion fund with a 0.43% Indonesian allocation: $430 million. Everything else in the portfolio is worth $99.57 billion. Assume Indonesian shares alone fall 20%, with no exchange-rate movement, no change in constituents or inclusion factors, and no subscriptions or redemptions. The Indonesian sleeve is now worth $344 million and the total fund $99.914 billion. Its new weight is approximately 0.3443%. The portfolio has reached that weight without any trade.

Now suppose an index deletion requires the Indonesian allocation to fall to zero. Holding prices at the level reached after the hypothetical shock, the fund must eliminate $344 million of exposure. For a fully physically replicated portfolio, that entails selling the remaining shares and reallocating the proceeds. Actual execution prices and implementation arrangements would affect the result. The number here is an illustration, not an outflow estimate.

The initial decline still hurts. The fund has already lost $86 million of market value. The distinction prevents a second, imaginary sale from being inferred from a weight change that prices have already produced. Investor redemptions, risk limits or liquidity needs could trigger additional transactions, but each requires separate evidence. Research on [passive investing](/en/glossary/gestion-passive/) stresses precisely this diversity of channels, including differences between conventional mutual funds and ETFs. [BIS, research on passive investing](https://www.bis.org/publications/implications-passive-investing-securities-markets).

An announcement can also bring transactions forward. An investor expecting future forced sales might reduce exposure in advance, while another might wait for more attractive entry prices. That provides a possible explanation for anticipation, rather than proof of who sold on October 16. Even an exceptionally severe session cannot reveal the split between passive portfolios, discretionary decisions and domestic investors without more granular trading and ownership data.

## The mandate determines the potential outflow

The elementary calculation is straightforward: affected assets under management multiplied by the exposure to be removed. Both inputs are difficult to establish. A comprehensive estimate would need to distinguish MSCI Emerging Markets funds, global funds whose benchmarks contain Indonesia, separately managed accounts and derivative exposures. Counting both a fund of funds and its underlying fund can double-count the same position. Treating assets merely benchmarked against an index as if they all replicate it converts a reference into an obligation.

Active management occupies that middle ground. Where the mandate allows, a manager can keep a stock that has left the benchmark. Doing so accepts [tracking risk](/en/glossary/tracking-error/), linked to fluctuations in the portfolio’s return relative to its benchmark, and may require an explanation to clients. Empirical research has found that benchmarks also influence discretionary portfolios. It cannot establish that every active manager will sell Indonesia, or provide a historical coefficient that can safely be reused to predict 2027 outflows. [Raddatz, Schmukler and Williams, research published in 2017](https://iiep.gwu.edu/international-asset-allocations-and-capital-flows-benchmark-effect).

A dedicated country [ETF](/en/glossary/etf/) raises a different question. EIDO, iShares’ Indonesia fund, uses the Indonesia IMI 25/50 benchmark, distinct from both the broad emerging-market composite and the Standard basket used in the consultation’s statistics. Removal from a global composite does not automatically imply liquidation of a country product. Its own index, fund documents and subsequent decisions need to be examined. Capitalisation and constituent figures from one universe cannot simply be applied to another. [iShares, EIDO’s objective and benchmark](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239661/ishares-msci-indonesia-etf); [MSCI, IMI 25/50 index description](https://www.msci.com/indexes/index/724020/msci-indonesia-imi-25-50-index).

Nor does the ETF label establish a one-for-one transmission to the underlying shares. A secondary-market sale of a fund unit from one investor to another need not cause the fund to sell stocks. The connection depends on creations and redemptions, intermediaries and inventory management. A fund using derivatives may also change its exposure without directly selling shares itself; the counterparty’s hedge is another layer of the analysis. [BIS, passive fund structures and flows](https://www.bis.org/publications/implications-passive-investing-securities-markets).

The public record assembled for this investigation contains no consolidated, deduplicated and current inventory of all these positions. Consequently, this article provides no aggregate estimate of “certain” forced selling. It identifies the necessary condition instead: an implemented eligibility change affecting portfolios whose mandates require an adjustment. The liquidity available to absorb the resulting orders would then matter as much as the headline exposure.

## Liquid companies can bear the cost of opaque stocks

The remaining basket contains another striking feature. At September 30, **financials accounted for 71.71% of Indonesia’s Standard basket**. This is a sector weight within one equity index. It is neither the banking sector’s share of Indonesia’s economy nor the composition of the entire exchange. It does, however, show where exposure is concentrated in a portfolio tracking that basket. [MSCI, sector weights, page 7](https://www.msci.com/downloads/documents/indexes/consultations/equity/Indonesia_Consultation_Oct_26.pdf#page=7).

Under a country-wide deletion, the adjustment would reach beyond companies whose disclosure practices are disputed. It would also affect securities held because they share the same national classification. More liquid stocks could become a convenient route for investors cutting country exposure, even where trading works comparatively well. That is the potentially damaging externality: a loss of confidence in part of the market’s infrastructure can impose costs on others.

This argument imputes no wrongdoing to financial companies and establishes no weakness in their balance sheets. A falling bank share price does not mechanically subtract the same amount from the bank’s book equity. It first reduces the value shareholders place on their ownership. Transmission becomes more tangible if the bank needs to raise capital, its customers have pledged shares as collateral, or market stress affects its funding. Those channels require balance-sheet and contractual evidence; a sector weight cannot supply it.

The distinction also matters for non-financial companies. A lower market valuation can make an equity issue more dilutive and less appealing. A group planning a share-funded acquisition may reconsider. A company with cash, no pressing refinancing requirement and no imminent equity issue can withstand a large market decline with far less immediate operational disruption. The relevant question is its financing need, rather than the size of a red candle on a trading screen.

Concentration and share-backed funding also feature in our analysis of [the KOSPI and liquidation feedback loops](/en/analysis/kospi-concentrated-liquidation-samsung-sk-hynix/). The channels discussed here require their own Indonesian evidence.

## The currency and credit channels are conditional

Foreign selling of equities is not automatically a foreign-exchange outflow. An investor can retain rupiah, buy another local stock or switch into domestic bonds. Direct pressure on the currency requires conversion and net demand for foreign currency that other participants will not absorb at the same price. The classification proposal concerns equity indexes. Any spillover to bonds would be an additional market response, rather than execution of the same index rule.

If the rupiah depreciates, a company repaying dollars out of local-currency income faces a heavier burden. Dollar export receipts and currency hedges can offset it. Moving from that mechanism to a risk assessment requires net foreign-currency debt, maturities, revenue currencies and hedging details for each borrower. MSCI’s consultation does not provide that map.

Share-backed borrowing offers another possible transmission route. A sufficiently large fall in collateral value can lead a lender to demand more collateral or partial repayment. A borrower unable to meet the request may have to sell, adding to downward price pressure. Whether such a loop operated in Indonesia on October 16, and at what scale, remains unestablished in the evidence reviewed here. We found no consolidated figures for margin calls or collateral liquidations on that session in the selected documents.

Liquidity is where these scenarios meet. An urgent seller facing a deep pool of buyers gets a different price from one facing a thin order book. Staged implementation can reduce the concentration of orders while allowing more time for anticipatory trades. Additional time changes how risk is distributed across investors and dates. That is why the implementation question in MSCI’s consultation deserves as much scrutiny as the final classification.

## FTSE offers a meaningful counterpoint

The strongest counterargument comes from another index provider. In its review published on October 6, FTSE Russell kept Indonesia classified as **Secondary Emerging**, without placing it on the Watch List. The document predates MSCI’s announcement by nine days and is therefore not a response to the October 15 consultation. The positions remain distinct: FTSE retains its existing classification, while MSCI proposes changing it. [FTSE Russell, annual announcement, page 4](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/ftse-russell/en_us/documents/country-classification/ftse-country-classification-update-2026.pdf#page=4); [October 6 publication notice](https://www.lseg.com/en/media-centre/press-releases/ftse-russell/2026/ftse-country-classification-review-september-2026).

Retention came with operational reservations. FTSE said it had postponed certain index changes in the March, June and September reviews and continued to monitor reforms. It plans a further update on the treatment of securities before the December 2026 review. The providers differ in how they respond to acknowledged difficulties, under their respective methods, procedures and timetables. [FTSE Russell, Indonesia section](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/ftse-russell/en_us/documents/country-classification/ftse-country-classification-update-2026.pdf#page=4).

For potential flows, the distinction leaves room for other outcomes. Portfolios following a different index family might remain invested, while unconstrained investors might absorb shares at lower prices. Their existence guarantees neither the size of their purchases nor the prices at which they would transact. It does prevent a prospective MSCI exclusion from being equated with the disappearance of every foreign buyer.

## The next test runs through the ownership records

Credible improvement would need to be visible along the chain linking an identity to a market order: clearer beneficial ownership, identified relationships between accounts, verifiable free float and effective follow-through by supervisors. To change MSCI’s assessment, those improvements would also have to extend beyond a handful of prominent cases. The consultation specifically seeks evidence of broad and sustained application. [MSCI, evaluation criteria, page 3](https://www.msci.com/downloads/documents/indexes/consultations/equity/Indonesia_Consultation_Oct_26.pdf#page=3).

The adverse scenario becomes more plausible if opacity persists, MSCI confirms removal and affected portfolios have little time or liquidity to adjust. Verifiable implementation of reforms, sufficient improvement to preserve eligibility, or the absorption of sales at less distressed prices would weaken it. No numerical probability is assigned here: the necessary evidence is unavailable.

The episode illustrates the reach of an index promise. For clients, it makes an investment process predictable by specifying the portfolio’s rules. For an eligible market, it creates demand whose persistence depends on meeting shared standards. When confidence in free float breaks down, that predictability can become a mechanism requiring an exit.

At the October 16 close, a severe sell-off is established and the threat of exclusion is explicit. A general liquidation remains a scenario. Indonesia’s central task is to restore enough confidence in ownership and tradability that investors do not have to discover the market’s true depth at the very moment they all want to use it.
