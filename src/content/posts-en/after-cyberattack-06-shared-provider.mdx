<p class="series-kicker">After the cyberattack, who pays? · Part 6/8</p>

<p class="standfirst">An unavailable dealership system pushes payments into the next month; disrupted blood testing puts additional pressure on blood stocks. Disclosures from CDK’s customers and the NHS reveal how a shared dependency changes daily work and distributes the consequences of a cyberattack.</p>

On 7 October 2024, the US Securities and Exchange Commission asked Asbury Automotive Group to clarify the effects of a cyberattack. The automotive retailer had described disrupted sales, additional expenses and delayed payments. The SEC wanted to understand their significance for earnings and cash flows. The affected system belonged to CDK Global, a provider used by other dealership groups too. An interruption at the supplier was appearing in the disclosures of several customers.[4](#source-04)

That correspondence opens another way of investigating the cost of cyberattacks. A business can secure its own computers while remaining dependent on the software that runs its sales, a laboratory that processes its tests, or infrastructure used by its subcontractors. When that service becomes unavailable, customers have to adapt at the same time. Each has its own staff and contracts, while part of the recovery timetable is determined elsewhere.

Disclosures from CDK’s customers, followed by NHS documents about the Synnovis attack, trace that dependency to tangible consequences: a debt is paid in a different month, unavailable testing increases demand for a particular blood group, and a replacement software system requires a gradual migration. This investigation follows those documented connections. The public record explains how the disruption travels more precisely than it measures an aggregate loss across all those affected.[2](#source-02)[10](#source-10)[8](#source-08)

The [fifth instalment on the work of recovery](/en/analysis/after-cyberattack-05-economics-of-recovery/) followed the effort required to restore usable services. This instalment examines their reliance on a shared provider.

## Shared software becomes part of everyone’s working day

A [dealer management system (DMS)](/en/glossary/dms/) connects several everyday operations. Asbury lists sales, service, inventory, customer relationship management and accounting among the functions affected by the CDK incident. AutoNation describes the same set of dependencies. The system connects a vehicle to its sales record and the administrative work surrounding it, so an interruption reaches several teams within a single dealership.[2](#source-02)[3](#source-03)

Asbury’s quarterly filing offers a particularly useful comparison. From 19 June 2024, the outage affected its locations except for the Koons dealerships, which used a different DMS. Within the same group, the boundary of the disruption followed the choice of management system. That observation helps establish the connection to CDK, while leaving the broader security of the different software products untested.[2](#source-02)

Recovery had several boundaries too. AutoNation says access to its DMS and core functions was restored on 29 June. Ancillary systems and integrations that automate processes including ordering, scheduling, payment and reporting returned by the end of July. Asbury regained CDK’s functions on 8 July, with other applications coming back online in the following weeks. Those dates describe different customers and different layers of functionality.[3](#source-03)[6](#source-06)

A management system can therefore be back online while a workshop still lacks an automation it normally relies on. AutoNation says its parts and service business continued to experience adverse effects in July. To understand the cost to the customer, a useful milestone is the return of an operation that can actually be performed, with its connections and working capacity restored. A provider’s recovery announcement needs to be read at that level.[3](#source-03)

The documented transmission here is through service unavailability. The cited disclosures establish interruptions to functions dependent on CDK; they do not establish an intrusion into every dealership’s systems. That distinction directs the investigation towards continuity: which tasks can still proceed, through what alternative process, and at what volume?

## A deferred payment changes the movement of money

The most visible effect appears in completed sales and services. In its second-quarter 2024 results, Asbury estimated that the incident reduced diluted earnings per share by $0.95 to $1.15, before any potential recoveries. This is a management estimate. It encompasses lost or deferred revenue and expenses associated with the outage, retaining uncertainty in an assessment that depends on judging what normal operations would have produced.[1](#source-01)

Cash tells a slightly different story. Asbury’s quarterly filing explains that certain amounts normally paid in June were paid in July because of the CDK outage. At 30 June, accounts payable and vehicle inventory financing balances were consequently higher than expected. That inventory borrowing, known as floor plan financing, funds vehicles before they are sold; its repayment is also a cash outflow.[2](#source-02)

The mechanism deserves attention. While a payment awaits execution, the money remains with the debtor and the obligation remains on its balance sheet. Once payment is made, cash leaves and the corresponding liability falls. A reporting date between those two moments captures the waiting period. Deferring an outflow can temporarily cushion the decline in cash even while the business is suffering operationally. It also postpones the creditor’s receipt, although that alone tells us little about the creditor’s financial position.

Asbury does not isolate the amount attributable to this timing effect. The broader change in liabilities discussed near the relevant passage combines several causes and cannot be used to reconstruct a CDK-specific sum. The available disclosure establishes a movement from June into July, with an economic mechanism that is clear and a monetary magnitude that remains unknown.[2](#source-02)

This helps explain the SEC’s questions. The regulator compared the estimated earnings impact with reported diluted earnings of $1.39 per share and requested further quantification. Asbury’s release also reported adjusted earnings of $6.40 per share, a company-defined measure that removes items including asset impairments. The choice of earnings denominator substantially changes the impression a percentage creates. The investigation therefore retains the values, their status and the discussion around them.[4](#source-04)[1](#source-01)

Asbury argued for a broader assessment. Its 30 September response said it had considered estimated effects on the quarter, the full year and subsequent periods, using quantitative and qualitative factors. On 17 October, the group proposed adding the estimated earnings range to its next quarterly filing while maintaining its assessment of the incident’s materiality for investor disclosure. It explained the absence of further detail on payables by pointing to their settlement very shortly after the reporting date. The SEC closed its review on 23 October, reminding the company of its responsibility for its disclosures.[5](#source-05)[6](#source-06)[7](#source-07)

The exchange reveals a limit in public measurement. A single provider can affect several customers, while their earnings and payment effects are reported across different categories and periods. Reconstructing the collective cost would also require tracking transactions with their counterparties, to avoid counting the same transfer more than once or treating a deferral as a permanent loss.

## In London, the fallback draws on a shared reserve

On 3 June 2024, a ransomware attack affected pathology provider Synnovis. Several NHS organisations depended on its services, including King’s College Hospital and Guy’s and St Thomas’. The NHS describes both hospital trusts as co-owners of the provider with SYNLAB. Here, the disruption travelled through laboratory work and decisions about care. NHS Blood and Transplant’s 2024/25 annual report describes the impact on systems used to determine patients’ blood groups and check compatibility for transfusions.[12](#source-12)[10](#source-10)[13](#source-13)

Difficulties in checking blood compatibility increased reliance on O-negative units. A technology problem at the pathology provider therefore changed the demand placed on a service supplying blood products to hospitals. The adaptation helped care continue, while drawing more heavily on a resource shared with other institutions. Its specialist testing team took on more work and operated longer hours, giving urgent cases priority.[10](#source-10)

In its alert of 25 July 2024, the organisation said affected London hospitals had needed 94% more O negative blood since the incident began than in the corresponding period of 2023. The comparison covers those hospitals and that period. It measures neither demand across the whole country nor an annual increase. The alert attributed pressure on stocks to higher demand alongside reduced collections, unfilled donation appointments and the usual difficulties of the summer period.[11](#source-11)

The reserve becomes the meeting point of several constraints. The cyberattack contributes to increased demand for one type of product, while collections determine the ability to replenish stocks. The documents do not allocate national stock pressure quantitatively between those causes. They do, however, establish a full mechanism from interrupted laboratory services to the blood supply service brought in to support them.

Postponed care provides another measure of disruption. NHS England’s update published on 26 September reported a cumulative 10,152 acute outpatient appointments and 1,710 elective procedures postponed at the two most affected trusts, through 22 September. These are provisional, unvalidated management figures. They count postponements and may include the same person more than once. Adding the sixteen rows in the separately published weekly table gives 10,257 appointments and 1,857 procedures, exceeding the announced cumulative figures by 105 and 147. No explanation for that difference has been established. The figures quoted here remain those of the dated announcement, with their provisional status; source note 12 explains the comparison.[12](#source-12)

By 26 September, NHS England said GPs in south-east London again had access to the full range of pathology tests, while blood transfusion systems were still being restored. The NHS update dated 10 November 2025 says Synnovis services had been fully restored by December 2024. As with the dealerships, recovery has to be followed function by function. In hospitals, it also involves backlogs and mutual aid that a simple measure of IT availability captures only partially.[12](#source-12)[13](#source-13)

## The difficulty of switching is part of the dependency

The Koons exception raises a reasonable question: why remain with a shared service when another product exists? Asbury’s later filings provide a practical answer. Its 2025 annual report, filed in February 2026, says 38 stores had moved from CDK to Tekion by 31 December. It describes potential expenses from running both systems, the work involved in the transition and temporary productivity losses as staff learn the new software.[8](#source-08)

The project continued in 2026. The second-quarter filing records 25 store conversions during that quarter and, in its analysis of the first half, discusses implementation and duplicate system costs. Those observations document a migration proceeding over time. The filings also describe expected efficiency gains and technological improvements; they do not support attributing the entire project or its budget to the 2024 cyberattack.[9](#source-09)[8](#source-08)

In banking, European supervisors have an indicator of this difficulty. An ECB analysis published on 19 February 2025 uses significant institutions’ outsourcing registers as at the end of 2023. Among contracts that were both critical and external, 73.7% of the budget concerned providers whose replacement was assessed as difficult, and 8.6% providers whose replacement was assessed as impossible. Together, those categories accounted for 82.3% of the relevant budget. These are reported assessments weighted by budget, rather than a share of banks or the results of completed migration tests.[15](#source-15)

Another difficulty can sit behind the apparent diversity of contracts. In its December 2023 toolkit, the Financial Stability Board describes an institution using the same provider directly and through other suppliers’ subcontracting arrangements. Several commercial relationships can therefore converge on a [shared technical dependency](/en/glossary/dependance-commune/). The UK National Cyber Security Centre recommends mapping suppliers, their services and subcontractors, with the depth of investigation guided by their importance to the business.[14](#source-14)[19](#source-19)

Concentration also brings benefits that the analysis needs to retain. The FSB explains that, in some circumstances, relying on a market-leading provider can pose less risk than an in-house solution or several suppliers. Size or market share therefore describes only part of the risk. The importance of the service and the practical ability to replace it help determine how severe an interruption could become.[14](#source-14)

Preparing an exit means making migration workable: moving data and applications, identifying an alternative provider or an in-house solution, and checking the skills available. Published on 16 July 2025, the ECB’s cloud outsourcing guide recommends preparing those resources and reviewing exit plans, with proportionate testing of critical migration steps. An exit clause becomes useful when the necessary resources are actually available.[16](#source-16)

## Organising recovery across the customer network

A supplier relationship allows each business to negotiate its own continuity arrangements. Shared dependencies introduce a collective constraint: several customers may need assistance, alternative capacity and restoration at the same time. A contingency plan therefore benefits from examining whether those resources will be available when the rest of the network needs them too. This is an analytical implication of the common dependencies described by the FSB, which distinguishes an individual firm’s resilience from that of the system in which it operates.[14](#source-14)

In the United Kingdom, financial oversight has begun to reach certain critical providers directly. An announcement on 10 July 2026 set out the first designations, effective on 13 July, covering AWS EMEA, Google Cloud EMEA, Microsoft Ireland Operations and Oracle UK. UK authorities connect this oversight to services whose disruption could threaten financial stability. It applies to the providers and services within that regime; it is not a general certification of their products.[17](#source-17)

On 8 October 2026, SIMEX26 brought together 38 major banks and market infrastructure operators to rehearse a global cloud services disruption. The statement published the following day says the scenario was developed for the exercise and was not informed by intelligence about a specific threat. Participants worked with the authorities on a coordinated response. The announcement does not provide detailed results, but the exercise’s scope shows the level at which preparations are now being discussed.[18](#source-18)

The CDK record brings dealership operations and payment dates into that discussion; Synnovis adds laboratory capacity and the reserves needed to sustain care. In both cases, the purchased service becomes part of work extending beyond the supplier. A precise question follows for the period before a crisis: which activities could continue, using which resources, when other customers are seeking those resources at the same time? Answering it involves the contract, the organisation of work and the collective capacity available to absorb the interruption.
