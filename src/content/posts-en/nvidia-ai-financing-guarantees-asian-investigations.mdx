**Nvidia sells the chips, and some of its customers also need its help to pay for them. When lenders ask the manufacturer to stand behind more of the equipment’s value, AI’s enormous growth promises become a negotiation over who takes the loss if rental payments stop and the servers fetch too little on resale.**

That is the pressure point in a Bloomberg investigation published on November 12. People familiar with the discussions said some customers and financial partners had been seeking stronger Nvidia backing for about a month. No transaction from the announced $500 billion partnership had closed, according to the report. The difficulties it describes began before Jensen Huang’s departure. [S01](#source-S01)

The leadership search therefore lands in the middle of an existing financial decision. Nvidia’s board must choose executives who can sell infrastructure, support its financing and set limits on the risk taken to help customers expand. Meanwhile, fresh documents from Singapore and Malaysia sharpen the scrutiny of server purchases and movements. The economic question connecting these developments is the reliability of the contracts and assets supporting credit.

## The $500 billion ambition still needs individual deals

In August, Nvidia announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR. The stated aim was to mobilise more than $500 billion of third-party capital over time through independent financing platforms. Its SEC filing describes memorandums of understanding and warns that preliminary arrangements may never become definitive agreements. The headline number expresses the intended scale. Executed transactions will show how much capital is actually committed. [S03](#source-S03) [S10](#source-S10)

Huang explained the structure in a signed August 11 blog post. Financial institutions would assess individual customers, expected revenue, equipment utilisation and future asset values. Nvidia might provide residual-value support for **up to 25% of an opportunity**, following a project-by-project assessment. Signed contracts will determine the obligations assumed by each party. [S02](#source-S02)

For a lender, reaching that stage means agreeing on equity contributions, drawdown conditions, the revenue available to service debt and remedies if the project struggles. Manufacturer support matters because it can make a potential loss more manageable for the other participants.

Bloomberg reports that some participants wanted more protection than Nvidia had proposed. Blackstone declined to comment, while Nvidia and the other partners approached had not responded by publication. The account relies on anonymous sources discussing private negotiations. [S01](#source-S01)

## A guarantee allocates losses after default

In the structure described by Bloomberg, a [special-purpose vehicle, or SPV](/en/glossary/spv/), borrows to buy equipment and leases it to a compute user. Rent covers operating costs and debt service. If the customer stops paying, finding another tenant or selling the hardware can generate replacement proceeds. A [residual value guarantee](/en/glossary/vrg/) can cover a remaining shortfall under its contractual conditions. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

Lenders therefore assess both the customer using the machines and the alternatives available if that customer fails. Equipment sought by other buyers provides more recovery options. Hardware that is difficult to move, operate or sell creates greater dependence on the original user and the guarantor. Nvidia argues that its infrastructure serves many workloads and can be redeployed across a broad customer base. This commercial proposition is one of the assumptions a financing decision must test. [S02](#source-S02)

The proposed 25% ceiling requires particular care. Huang’s post describes a share of an opportunity. Each agreement would still need to define which losses qualify, the recoveries required before a claim and the guarantor’s rights. Multiplying that percentage by the entire $500 billion ambition would manufacture a blanket obligation from optional support. [S02](#source-S02) [S03](#source-S03)

A separate published agreement illustrates why the details matter. Nvidia announced guarantees capped at $105 billion for defined land, power and building elements of the SB Energy project. The 10-Q and guarantee form describe triggers, remedies and termination provisions, with several passages redacted. This arrangement has its own scope, explored in our [analysis of residual-value protection](/en/analysis/residual-value-guarantee-ai-infrastructure-credit/). [S03](#source-S03) [S09](#source-S09)

## The next CEO will inherit a decision about risk

Colette Kress is interim CEO while retaining her finance role. Harvey C. Jones chairs the board, which has opened a search for internal and external candidates. Those are the arrangements disclosed in Nvidia’s November 12 regulatory filing. [S08](#source-S08)

Offering lenders more protection could help the next leadership team facilitate sales. Nvidia would also take on greater exposure to customers’ difficulties. Keeping its support more limited would leave investors to accept more risk, provide additional equity or renegotiate the project’s cost and timing. That choice can affect how quickly new compute capacity obtains financing.

Three succession scenarios have practical implications. An extended interim period could give the board time to reassess commitments before settling on a strategy. An internal appointment might preserve commercial relationships under tighter financial limits. An outside CEO would need to establish credibility with employees and partners while setting a fresh standard for commitments. These are governance scenarios whose consequences would depend on the board’s actual mandate.

New agreements will provide the clearest evidence of the chosen approach. Existing obligations follow their terms, including any amendment and termination rights. Future transactions will reveal how much risk Nvidia is prepared to accept to support its customers’ demand.

## Singapore brings fresh charges in the Aperia server case

A Singapore Police Force statement dated **November 13** sets out a specific judicial development. Shi Bingzhe, a Superphi director and NJ Global Financial chief operating officer at the relevant time, and both companies face money-laundering charges. The allegations concern **917 servers acquired from Aperia International through 41 transactions between May 2024 and February 2025**. [S04](#source-S04)

Shi faces 41 charges, Superphi six and NJ Global Financial 35. Police allege that the purchasers had reasonable grounds to believe the servers represented proceeds of fraud by false representation. The proceedings concern those acquisitions and the defendants’ responsibility. The equipment’s final destination and precise components remain to be documented. [S04](#source-S04)

Singapore’s new charges concern the purchasers of Aperia servers. In Malaysia, the authorities are examining hardware movements involving Megaspeed’s local affiliate, Speedmatrix. [S04](#source-S04) [S05](#source-S05)

## Malaysia examines possible Speedmatrix re-exports

On the same day, Malaysia’s investment, trade and industry ministry, MITI, said it was working with customs to determine whether chips imported by Speedmatrix had been re-exported and on what terms. Its statement describes the Malaysian Megaspeed affiliate as a data-centre tenant bringing in its own equipment. Earlier ministry visits were routine sector monitoring, it said, rejecting suggestions that they amounted to official clearance. [S05](#source-S05)

Local rules distinguish bringing hardware into the country from moving it out. According to MITI, importing AI chips currently requires no permit. Since July 2025, however, exporting, re-exporting, transhipping or transiting high-performance US AI chips from or through Malaysia has required a permit under the Strategic Trade Act 2010. The examination concerns movements still to be established and their compliance with Malaysian law. [S05](#source-S05)

Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim subsequently added an important qualification. Bernama reported on November 13 that he said the government had no indication at that stage that the chips concerned had been exported. He pledged agency cooperation and said Malaysia had yet to receive a formal request from Singapore or the United States. [S06](#source-S06)

Megaspeed has announced its own compliance review. In a statement dated November 12, it said it had engaged a US law firm to inventory its GPUs and independently review its practices, and affirmed cooperation with BIS inspections. The firm’s identity, timetable and findings remain to be published. [S07](#source-S07)

## Lenders need identifiable assets and enforceable rights

A hardware guarantee requires identifying the equipment, establishing its location and checking the rights to recover or sell it. The Asian proceedings make those questions tangible. A false-representation case and a re-export examination operate under different legal provisions, but each can shed light on information used to purchase, insure or finance equipment.

Any effect on credit will depend on the particular transaction. Delivery, compliance or ownership problems could lead its lenders to examine protections and drawdown conditions. Evidence of a financing impact would include an amended loan, deferred payment, guarantee claim or revised order. The new Asian documents currently describe the investigations and allegations set out above.

The wider risk comes from overlapping roles. A supplier selling equipment may also help finance its purchase. During a demand slowdown, it could face both weaker sales and claims under commitments made to support buyers. Our [AI financing atlas](/en/atlas/ai-financing/) follows these connections.

Washington could seek influence through that dependence, as discussed in the [fifth instalment](/en/analysis/nvidia-trump-tim-cook-lutnick-state-leverage/). Targeted public support would require an identified project, legal authority, resources and negotiated rights. The private negotiations reported here already offer an economic explanation for the financing difficulties: lenders want stronger protection. Future contracts will show how much Nvidia supplies, and whether a public institution actually enters the financing.
