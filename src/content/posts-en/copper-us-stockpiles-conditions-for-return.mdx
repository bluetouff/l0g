On **5 October 2026**, a COMEX notice added another location to the copper delivery network: Henry Bath’s warehouse in Laredo, Texas. Its approved capacity is **40,345 US short tons**, or approximately **36,600 metric tonnes**. Approval makes it a place where metal deliverable against the exchange’s contracts can be stored. The notice establishes neither how full the building is nor when it was built. It does establish another route into the US exchange-delivery system. <sup><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a></sup>

Location matters. A manufacturer needs copper it can process. A trader also compares the places where it can sell. The prospect of a customs barrier can alter that second calculation well before any duty is collected. Cargoes change destination, warehouses gain approval and metal settles into a different geography.

In September, l0g examined [the carrying costs of this inventory](/en/analysis/copper-us-stockpile-carrying-cost-tariff-uncertainty/). The next question concerns the reverse journey: **what would persuade owners to send US stockpiles to other markets?** A narrowing price gap can stop fresh arrivals while leaving existing tonnes exactly where they are. The distinction between halting a flow and unwinding a stock is central to the industrial risk.

## A large stockpile with uncertain boundaries

On 14 October, Reuters reported a Macquarie estimate of roughly **1.6 million tonnes** of US copper inventory, on and off exchanges. The same report put COMEX stocks above **714,000 tonnes**. Two days earlier, a Reuters commentary had cited a Citi estimate of approximately **1.1 million tonnes** for the United States. <sup><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a></sup>

These totals cannot be read as a forty-eight-hour increase or a statistical confidence interval. We obtained no detailed methodology that would align their observation dates and coverage. The 1.6 million figure therefore remains an attributed estimate. Exchange inventory, metal physically located in a country and the full universe of privately held copper answer different questions.

Warehouse notices offer a firmer foothold. Between late August and early October, three approvals covered copper capacity of **33,565 short tons in Memphis**, **44,092 in Chicago** and **40,345 in Laredo**. Converted into metric tonnes and rounded, these are **30,450, 40,000 and 36,600 tonnes** respectively. The figure brings those decisions together without assuming the facilities are full. <sup><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a></sup>

Nor is this selection a net national capacity balance. On 31 August, COMEX also published an approved capacity reduction at 4STOX in Tucson. Reconstructing the entire network would require every change and the preceding capacities. The three approvals support a narrower finding: operators have additional locations at which to receive copper qualifying for exchange delivery. <sup><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a></sup>

That qualification gives the metal’s owner another financial exit. The COMEX copper contract requires a specified cathode grade, an approved brand and a designated warehouse. Industrial copper and exchange-deliverable copper are therefore not interchangeable merely because their metal content looks similar. Specifications and documentation help determine the available buyer. <sup><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">[11]</a></sup>

## An undecided tariff already influences the destination

The timetable is frequently compressed. Operative clause 7 of the **30 July 2025** proclamation called for a Commerce Department update by 30 June 2026 to determine whether refined-copper duties were warranted: **15% from January 2027, then 30% from January 2028**. It set out a decision to be made. As of 14 October, the sources reviewed did not confirm adoption of those rates for refined copper. <sup><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a></sup>

Manufactured products fall under a different set of provisions, amended in April and June 2026. Applying one tariff rate indiscriminately to cathodes, wire and equipment containing copper would erase the legal distinctions that determine liability. Those texts are used here to delimit the risk, rather than to recreate the tariff schedule for every product. <sup><a href="#source-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-10" aria-label="Source 10">[10]</a></sup>

Consider the moment when a cargo’s destination is chosen. Buyers who expect a higher future US price relative to other markets may be willing to bid more today. A shipment previously intended for another customer can then become attractive to the US market, provided the gap covers the journey and associated constraints.

One operator may be reducing uncertainty over future supplies. Another may buy physical copper and simultaneously sell a matching futures contract, locking in part of the price difference. A third may retain exposure to the policy decision. All three strategies can produce the same visible event, a warehouse receipt, with very different risks attached. Inventory tonnage does not disclose the positions behind it.

The comparison requires prices that genuinely match: the same unit, admissible quality, consistent dates and known delivery terms. A spread between two screens with different maturities is not automatically an executable return. Transit time, collateral requirements and the ability to complete both legs matter to the operator moving the cargo.

## The journey back has a separate threshold

Take a **wholly hypothetical example**, in dollars per metric tonne. Shipping metal into the United States, together with the other frictions assigned to that transaction, amounts to **$300**. Moving it in the opposite direction involves **$250**. Product quality and delivery timing are comparable. Potential tariffs and the option value of waiting for a future decision are excluded.

When the US price exceeds the other market’s price by **$600**, bringing metal in leaves a $300 advantage after the assumed frictions. If the spread falls to **$100**, a new import loses its appeal. Yet sending copper already in the United States to the lower-priced market would forgo $100 in sale proceeds and require another $250 for the movement. The exporter would be **$350 worse off** than by selling locally immediately.

Reversing the arbitrage requires crossing another threshold. In this example, the US price must fall more than $250 below the alternative market’s price. At a spread of **−$400**, outward shipment offers a **$150 advantage**. Between **−$250 and +$300**, neither direction offers a geographic-arbitrage gain in the static model.

The middle band explains a form of persistence. Incoming cargoes can stop without a symmetrical incentive sending them back out. Existing holders can still sell to US users, transfer ownership or wait. The band indicates the absence of geographic arbitrage under the stated assumptions, not a compulsory freeze on every position.

The original purchase price matters to the owner’s eventual financial result. Choosing between two destinations today turns on the proceeds available and the expenditure still required. Transport already completed is a sunk cost; paying for another journey does not recover it. Losing an earlier opportunity is therefore different from receiving an economic instruction to reverse the shipment.

Policy uncertainty can extend the holding period. In commercial commentary published on 14 September, StoneX discussed the continuing attraction of holding metal in the United States while the refined-copper decision remained open. This market participant’s account illuminates an incentive, without measuring how many owners act on it. The value of waiting depends on contracts, liquidity and expectations that are not publicly observable. <sup><a href="#source-12" aria-label="Source 12">[12]</a></sup>

## Ownership can move while the cathodes stay put

Exchange delivery introduces another distinction. On COMEX, delivery transfers a **[warrant](/en/glossary/warrant-d-entrepot/)**, an electronic title to warehoused metal. Its new holder can retain it, sell it or request physical withdrawal. Repeated transactions can consequently concern the same cathodes without the metal leaving the building. <sup><a href="#source-14" aria-label="Source 14">[14]</a></sup>

Warehouse reporting distinguishes **registered** metal, covered by a warrant, from **eligible** metal that meets the requirements but has no warrant. Receipts and shipments are reported separately. A change of category and a physical movement can therefore be identified as different events. <sup><a href="#source-13" aria-label="Source 13">[13]</a></sup>

The figure follows a hypothetical **1,000-tonne** parcel. Selling it leaves the total unchanged. Cancelling its warrant and retaining it as eligible stock in this example also leaves the total unchanged. The site’s inventory falls when the tonnes leave. Their destination is still a further fact to establish.

“Off-warrant” does not imply a uniformly invisible universe. The LME has published daily reports covering part of these inventories since April 2025, with public access on a T+3 delay. That reporting does not encompass all privately held metal. What matters is the precise combination of coverage, reporting date, location and status. <sup><a href="#source-15" aria-label="Source 15">[15]</a></sup>

For an Asian or European purchaser, a US title transfer may change the counterparty without improving immediate access to copper. A local stock decline becomes more informative once its cause is known: delivery to an industrial customer, transfer to another warehouse or export. A column heading cannot establish a cargo’s route.

## Customs status and the applicable duty date

A cargo can be physically inside the United States while remaining in a [customs bonded warehouse](/en/glossary/entrepot-sous-douane/). The general rule in **19 CFR § 141.69** uses the duty rate in force at entry, subject to exceptions. For merchandise entered for warehousing, it refers to withdrawal for consumption. Specific provisions can override that treatment. <sup><a href="#source-16" aria-label="Source 16">[16]</a></sup>

Withdrawal for consumption requires formalities, an estimated-duty deposit and a permit. Unloading, presence in a building and entry for consumption are distinct events. COMEX approval by itself establishes none of the customs records for particular lots that might be stored there. <sup><a href="#source-17" aria-label="Source 17">[17]</a></sup>

<CopperBorderFigure lang="en" kind="customs" number="04" sources="16 17" caption={"Physical arrival and the date governing duty can diverge. The two routes explain general rules without establishing the regime of any actual parcel."}>
<details><summary>Method and scope</summary><p>19 CFR § 141.69 provides exceptions to the general entry rule, including warehousing; § 144.38 governs withdrawal for consumption. A future measure could introduce specific treatment. COMEX approval, a foreign-trade zone and a bonded warehouse are distinct concepts. No aggregate tax advantage is calculated.</p></details>
</CopperBorderFigure>

It follows that tonnes already on US soil cannot all be assumed to have secured a permanent tax advantage. Establishing that advantage would require their customs status, the relevant date and the provisions of a future measure, should one be adopted. This article therefore makes no calculation of duties supposedly avoided across the aggregate US stockpile.

The distinction also enters the comparison between local sale and shipment abroad. Each operator must use the regime applicable to its own parcel. Two nearby lots can offer different possibilities. Adding their weights provides a physical quantity; it does not harmonise their legal rights or their routes to market.

## Leaving the warehouse takes time of its own

An owner with a buyer, an agreed price and the necessary documents still has to load the metal. COMEX Chapter 7 sets out withdrawal procedures. The copper contract, meanwhile, specifies that it carries no freight allowance. Delivering title is therefore different from getting metal to a factory gate. <sup><a href="#source-13" aria-label="Source 13">[13]</a></sup> <sup><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">[11]</a></sup>

A **13 October** development makes this especially relevant. CME published a notice amending copper load-out procedures. Its summary gives a regulatory effective date of 28 October, pending CFTC review, and operational stages on **1 July 2027** and **1 January 2028**. The detailed attachment could not be obtained; no new load-out rate is inferred from the notice’s title. <sup><a href="#source-18" aria-label="Source 18">[18]</a></sup>

The economic distinction is straightforward. Inventory is a quantity at a date; loading capacity is a flow over time. One cannot be read directly from the other. Several holders can decide to withdraw at once while appointments, equipment and transport links impose an order on their requests.

This establishes no particular queue in Laredo, Memphis or Chicago. We obtained no loading schedules or measured waiting times. It does identify the missing evidence needed to assess a rapid release: how much metal can actually leave each location and reach its intended buyer within the relevant period? A large inventory with limited throughput provides different protection from the same inventory that can be mobilised quickly.

## Hedging the benchmark leaves a delivery exposure

Geographic concentration reaches manufacturers through the price at which they can actually obtain metal. The LME describes physical pricing as a benchmark plus premiums or discounts reflecting such factors as product, location and delivery. A benchmark hedge can leave exposure to those other components. This is **[basis risk](/en/glossary/risque-de-base/)**: the thing being purchased does not move exactly like the instrument used to hedge it. <sup><a href="#source-19" aria-label="Source 19">[19]</a></sup>

Consider a hypothetical factory planning a purchase at a **$10,000-per-tonne** benchmark plus a **$200** premium. It perfectly hedges the benchmark with a long futures position. By the purchase date, the benchmark falls to **$9,500**, while the premium rises to **$900**. The physical invoice is $10,400, compared with the $10,200 initially expected.

The long futures position loses $500, exactly as expected when the benchmark falls. Including the hedge, the purchase requires **$10,900**. The **$700 overrun** corresponds entirely to the higher premium. The benchmark hedge has performed its task in this example; access to the locally deliverable product remains exposed.

</CopperBorderFigure>

Futures gains and losses are settled daily. Cash outflows on the position may therefore precede payment for the physical parcel. A company must arrange that liquidity even when its hedge makes economic sense. No copper-market margin calls or owners’ losses are estimated here: individual portfolios are unknown. <sup><a href="#source-20" aria-label="Source 20">[20]</a></sup> Our investigation into [the liquidity required for margin calls](/en/analysis/when-the-barrel-becomes-a-margin-call/) examines that funding requirement in energy; it does not quantify copper holders’ positions.

For macroeconomic analysis, transmission is the key issue. A factory may need additional cash, postpone procurement or adjust production while the reference price eases. The response depends on delivery clauses, financial buffers and the ability to pass on changes in purchasing conditions. One price index is a poor description of all those circumstances.

## A global balance can coexist with local constraints

The strongest counterargument concerns the value of storage itself. Privately held inventory can secure supplies for nearby users, and delayed deliveries can meet genuine demand tomorrow. A high stock level is not intrinsically a misallocation. Its value depends on the service it provides and the terms on which it can be mobilised.

The global outlook may offer another offset. On 14 October, Reuters reported an ICSG forecast of a refined-copper surplus of **104,000 tonnes in 2026** and **368,000 tonnes in 2027**. The full ICSG release was unavailable. These remain attributed forecasts; no geographic breakdown is reconstructed from them. <sup><a href="#source-21" aria-label="Source 21">[21]</a></sup>

An annual global surplus describes the expected relationship between production and use. A purchaser of a particular parcel needs another answer: where, when and in what form is metal being offered? New availability can gradually ease premiums and supply pressure. The speed of adjustment also depends on existing inventories returning to circulation.

Even a fall in US stocks would leave room for interpretation. In a final simulation, inventory falls from **200,000 to 180,000 tonnes** after receipts of 40,000 tonnes. It could have supplied 60,000 tonnes to US users with no exports. Alternatively, it could have supplied 40,000 tonnes domestically and exported 20,000. The stock decline is identical; the direct effect on supply elsewhere is different.

Advance importing shifts some procurement through time. Once a stockpile has been built, foreign purchases can slow even while factories keep consuming. Conversely, strong imports can fill warehouses without signalling an equivalent acceleration in industrial activity. Our analysis of [adjustments in orders and inventories](/en/analysis/world-trade-rhythm-ai-orders/) develops this distinction between procurement and use. Measuring these relationships requires observations of use and physical flows.

The next decisive piece of evidence may therefore be less spectacular than the size of the “mountain”. It would combine a price spread consistent with outward shipment, completed physical withdrawals and a verified foreign destination. A lower US premium supplies only part of that evidence. Until the stages line up, the market can stop attracting metal while continuing to retain it.
