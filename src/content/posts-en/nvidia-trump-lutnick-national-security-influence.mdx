**Nvidia makes the chips behind much of the artificial-intelligence boom. Its chief executive, Jensen Huang, is now at the centre of an affair involving an acknowledged personal relationship, espionage allegations and sensitive sales to China. While Donald Trump defends him, press investigations describe interventions with officials examining one of Nvidia’s customers. The crisis reaches into the government responsible for policing those sales.**

*This investigation follows our [first report on the Nvidia affair](/en/analysis/nvidia-trump-warning-at-the-top/). It brings together evidence available on 11 November 2026.*

On 8 November, Jensen Huang sent Nvidia’s employees a message. He admitted being unfaithful to his wife and apologised. Then the message turned to national security: the board had opened an independent investigation, and he promised to cooperate. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">S08</a></sup>

At most companies, such an admission would primarily threaten a reputation. At Nvidia, it also raises questions about the information and decisions accessible to someone close to the chief executive. The company designs processors used to train and run AI systems. Washington treats access to some of that hardware as a matter of national power and restricts its sale to China. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">S11</a></sup>

Huang sits where corporate decisions, customer demand and American policy meet. An attempt to influence him or obtain information through him could have consequences far beyond his private life. His admission gives the investigation a starting point. Espionage remains an allegation requiring evidence.

The story becomes a matter of government accountability when it reaches the warnings delivered to Washington.

## A disputed warning aboard Air Force One

A *New York Times* investigation published on 10 November reports that Marco Rubio warned Trump of an espionage risk involving Huang aboard the presidential aircraft before the May trip to China. Trump disputes receiving that warning at the time, and the White House challenges the account. A written memo mentioned in the reporting had other recipients. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s06" aria-label="Source S06">S06</a></sup> Axios reports a classified memo was sent to Lutnick. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s07" aria-label="Source S07">S07</a></sup>

Howard Lutnick is the US commerce secretary. His department oversees the rules governing sales of sensitive American technology. Its officials can approve transactions, attach conditions or investigate where equipment actually went. He therefore occupies a central position in this affair.

The timing matters because a warning can change a decision. Officials may demand further checks before a shipment, or decide that existing safeguards are adequate. Understanding the choices made by Trump and Lutnick means establishing which risks they knew about when those choices were made.

Trump has continued to defend Huang. That public support puts the investigators in a difficult position: they are examining risks around a business leader whom the president is politically protecting. It raises the stakes for officials expected to follow the evidence wherever it leads.

The public record leaves deliberate concealment or obstruction to be established. It already raises a more immediate issue: how much freedom did the investigators retain? The handling of a company called Megaspeed brings that question into focus.

## Megaspeed brings the story back to the chips

Megaspeed is a Singapore-based data-centre operator and an Nvidia customer. A data centre houses servers, powerful computers that supply processing capacity to other businesses. Investigators have been examining whether chips connected with Megaspeed were diverted to destinations that should have been denied access.

Alice Huang’s name appears in this part of the story. Exchanges published by Singapore’s government associate her with Megaspeed. The *Wall Street Journal* had reported suspicions about her connections to Jensen Huang and Chinese intelligence. On 31 October, Singapore firmly denied conducting the espionage investigation into her that the newspaper had attributed to its authorities. It separately confirmed that its investigation into Megaspeed continued. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source S09">S09</a></sup>

The two inquiries concern different forms of access. One involves the possibility of obtaining sensitive information through a personal relationship. The other follows purchases, intermediaries and the organisations ultimately using the machines. Evidence about one can guide questions about the other, but each accusation still needs its own support.

Megaspeed disputes illegal transfers. A statement dated 23 December 2025, still displayed on its website, asserts compliance and cites inspections. The corresponding inspection reports are unavailable to us. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s24" aria-label="Source S24">S24</a></sup>

Washington’s concerns about this customer long preceded the public revelations about Huang’s relationship. The case was already sensitive enough for Nvidia’s senior lawyer to contact the department investigating it.

## Nvidia’s lawyer calls the investigators

According to the *New York Times*, Nvidia’s general counsel Tim Teter approached Commerce investigators about Megaspeed in August 2025, invoking permission from their superior, Jeffrey Kessler. The agents declined the meeting. An official disputes that Kessler had instructed them to share information. The Justice Department subsequently asked Commerce to suspend its work to protect a criminal investigation that remained active, the newspaper reports. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">S04</a></sup>

Bloomberg describes another part of the sequence: after a call from Teter to Kessler, instructions reportedly required Kessler’s approval for requests to Nvidia and limited references to Megaspeed in external communications. Nvidia describes its contacts as cooperation against diversion. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source S05">S05</a></sup>

For an agent trying to establish where a shipment went, access to the seller’s records is basic investigative work. Requiring senior approval adds a dependency: the agent needs the superior’s consent to ask questions of a company that can speak directly to that same superior. The company has a commercial stake in its customer’s future. The government is supposed to assess the risks independently.

Lutnick also received a public warning. On **13 October 2025**, Tom Cotton, the Republican chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee, wrote to him demanding an investigation into Megaspeed’s ownership, Chinese connections and relationships with American manufacturers. The allegations drew on press reporting. The letter itself establishes that the issue had been put publicly before the secretary more than a year before the current revelations. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s25" aria-label="Source S25">S25</a></sup>

The *Financial Times*, in reporting carried by *Asia Business Daily*, also describes a delay in adding Megaspeed to a restricted list despite requests from other departments. Commerce reportedly sought additional information about some of the companies under consideration; whether that explanation applied to Megaspeed remains unclear. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s21" aria-label="Source S21">S21</a></sup>

A restricted listing can tighten access to equipment while a risk is being assessed. Criminal proceedings take time to establish offences and responsibility. Orders can continue moving in the meantime, which makes administrative decisions commercially important well before a court reaches a verdict.

Lutnick is politically accountable for his department’s decisions. The available documents leave open whether he personally ordered any intervention. They do establish why his ministry’s handling of the case belongs at the heart of this investigation.

## Two H200 chips on a university purchase notice

While Washington argues over enforcement, a Chinese university with military links finds a supplier. On 11 November, Reuters reports the purchase of two H200 processors, an advanced Nvidia model, by Northwestern Polytechnical University in Xi’an. It also identifies five contracts to rent computing capacity. Chinese suppliers handled the purchases; the original supply route remains unknown. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source S01">S01</a></sup>

The university’s indexed notice, dated 21 October, names its management school and a total award of **874,700 yuan**. The live page returns an error. The notice records the contract award, leaving actual delivery to be confirmed. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source S02">S02</a></sup>

The university has been on the American restricted list since 2001. In 2021, Commerce again described its military ties in a case involving the illegal export of hydrophones, devices that detect sound underwater. That earlier prosecution concerned different equipment and concealment of the real recipient. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source S03">S03</a></sup>

The institution’s name should therefore have attracted attention from anyone checking the destination of sensitive technology. Yet the purchase notice exists. It illustrates the difficulty of enforcement when equipment passes through intermediaries and reappears with an institution Washington has considered sensitive for a quarter of a century.

Reuters establishes neither a direct Nvidia sale to the university nor a specific military use of the two chips. The purchase nevertheless raises a concrete traceability question. We found no evidence connecting it to Megaspeed: the two cases test the same American policy through separate transactions.

## Renting chips from the other side of the world

A chip can serve a foreign customer while remaining inside the building where it was installed. The customer rents computing capacity, connecting to remote servers to run software much as any business uses an online service. The hardware stays put; access to its capabilities crosses borders.

Consider an illustrative example, rather than an established Megaspeed transaction. Servers sit in Malaysia. Their owner sells computing capacity to a company that gives its own customers access. An inspector visits and finds the machines at the declared address. The question of who can connect to them, and for which work, remains.

That distinction matters for [export controls](/en/glossary/controle-export/). The address identifies the hardware’s location. Contracts and authorised accounts identify who can use it. Effective oversight has to connect the two.

<NvidiaInfluenceFigure lang="en" kind="access" />

The American rule published on 15 January 2026 addresses this difficulty. It provides case-by-case review for certain advanced-chip sales, including H200s, with independent testing in the United States and restrictions on users and uses. It also includes safeguards concerning certain remote access. Washington is allowing transactions subject to conditions that must remain verifiable after the sale. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source S10">S10</a></sup>

Bloomberg reports that an initial inventory exercise involving Megaspeed left roughly **50,000 chips** to be reconciled against shipments, before Nvidia cited other inspected warehouses. That is a reported inventory discrepancy with contested follow-up, rather than an established count of diverted chips. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source S05">S05</a></sup>

Finding the hardware answers one question. Identifying its [end user](/en/glossary/utilisateur-final/), the organisation actually using it at the end of successive contracts, answers another. A licence issued for a particular customer would lose much of its value if that customer could freely admit a prohibited recipient.

In December 2025, Nvidia announced an optional fleet-management tool installed by customers to report processor usage, configuration and errors. The company describes read-only telemetry, with an agent intended to be open source. The announcement outlines those functions; deployment at each relevant customer remains unverified. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s39" aria-label="Source S39">S39</a></sup>

Such information can help establish how equipment operates. Contracts and access permissions reveal another part of the picture. Technical monitoring offers useful evidence, but trust between the supplier, its customers and the authorities also depends on people being able to challenge the explanations they receive.

## Dummy servers and staged inspections

An American criminal case shows how far appearances can diverge from reality. On **19 March 2026**, federal prosecutors in New York announced charges against Wally Liaw, Steven Chang and Willy Sun. They described a Southeast Asian buyer presented as the end user, servers sent on to China and dummy machines prepared for inspections. Purchases allegedly reached roughly **$2.5 billion in 2024–2025**. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s28" aria-label="Source S28">S28</a></sup>

On 8 October, Sun pleaded guilty to four counts, including conspiracies involving export controls and obstruction of justice, Reuters reports from a court document. The other defendants’ liability follows its own proceedings. Supermicro says the company itself is outside the list of defendants. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s29" aria-label="Source S29">S29</a></sup>

The prosecutors’ account is easy to picture: inspectors see machines supposedly accounting for a delivery, while the real equipment has taken a different route. Serial numbers, shipping records and payments provide ways to test that appearance. A false account becomes harder to maintain when independent records must agree.

Supermicro published the findings of its independent internal investigation on 20 August. It said the inquiry found no evidence that current executives knew of the alleged diversion. It also announced disciplinary measures, including dismissals, and stronger compliance controls. These are the company’s published findings alongside the public proceedings. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s30" aria-label="Source S30">S30</a></sup>

The Justice Department announcement calls the buyer “Company-1”. Culper proposes identifying it as Megaspeed, but the documents examined here leave that connection unproved. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source S27">S27</a></sup> The Supermicro case supplies a concrete account of alleged concealment without establishing that every name in the wider Nvidia story belongs to the same scheme.

American rules already require suppliers to respond to warning signs. An order may be inconsistent with the buyer’s business, for example, or a customer may unusually refuse installation and testing. Guidance from the Bureau of Industry and Security, Commerce’s export-control agency, requires investigation of suspicious circumstances. It also addresses practices designed to prevent troubling information from reaching decision-makers. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s38" aria-label="Source S38">S38</a></sup>

The practical test is familiar in any large business: an employee raises a problem with a lucrative order, and a manager decides whether to pursue it or keep the sale moving. In this affair, the handling of those warnings matters more than the supplier’s reputation.

## Aivres and the machines around the chips

Nvidia supplies a crucial part of a larger system. Its processors need memory, connections, power and cooling. Server manufacturers assemble those components into usable machines. Their customers can therefore buy American chips without dealing directly with Nvidia.

Aivres is one such manufacturer. Its catalogue includes a server fitted with eight H200 processors. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s32" aria-label="Source S32">S32</a></sup> Culper, whose work is reproduced in the International Cyber Digest thread, attributes an important role to Aivres in imports by Speedmatrix, a company linked to Megaspeed. The complete customs data needed to reproduce those estimates are unavailable to us. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source S27">S27</a></sup>

Aivres also has a documented corporate history. In an order dated 10 March 2025 in a patent lawsuit brought by Hewlett Packard Enterprise, a judge describes it as a California company formerly called Inspur Systems. The order examines contested connections with Inspur Group and permits jurisdictional discovery. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s31" aria-label="Source S31">S31</a></sup>

The court document sheds light on a change of corporate identity within the scope of that patent dispute. For chip enforcement, it points to the importance of following the teams, contracts and decisions behind company names. A change in the apparent seller may leave industrial connections intact. Equally, an old affiliation alone gives an incomplete picture of a business at the time of a particular delivery.

This manufacturing layer also explains why monetary claims need care. A complete server contains considerably more than Nvidia’s chips. Adding server orders to processor sales can count part of the same equipment twice. The industrial stakes are substantial enough without billions inflated by double counting.

## The money behind Megaspeed leads towards Alibaba

The ownership trail reaches a company called SwiftData. C4ADS’s September report, *Covert Compute*, describes the transfer of all ten million Megaspeed shares to that entity in November and December 2023. It leaves SwiftData’s ultimate owner unidentified and considers whether Alice Huang retained practical influence, citing appearances as chair. That last assessment belongs to the report’s authors. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s26" aria-label="Source S26">S26</a></sup>

Financing opens a different line of inquiry. On 13 May, Culper Research disclosed a bet against Nvidia’s shares: its short position can profit if the price falls. That financial interest belongs alongside any reading of its allegations. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s12" aria-label="Source S12">S12</a></sup> Its reproduced documents include security granted by Speedmatrix to Apex, and an accounting note naming Alibaba as Apex’s ultimate parent. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source S13">S13</a></sup>

A security interest gives a creditor recourse to specified assets if contractual obligations are breached. The document reproduced by Culper is dated 18 June 2024. It covers obligations including those arising from service prepayments and reaches Speedmatrix assets such as contractual and intellectual-property rights. Those records describe a relationship that a list of shareholders alone would miss. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source S13">S13</a></sup>

<NvidiaInfluenceFigure lang="en" kind="finance" />

A *Financial Times* investigation reported by SBS on 9 November also describes financing of the Megaspeed group by an Alibaba subsidiary in Singapore. That account supports the financing lead without identifying Apex by name. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source S15">S15</a></sup>

The commercial mechanism is straightforward. A computing operator needs to pay for equipment before it generates revenue. A large customer can advance money and reserve future capacity. It helps make the project possible while gaining considerable influence over its operation. Security over the operator’s assets adds another source of leverage.

The local intermediary may own the machines while the funder holds contractual rights over the business using them. That makes the relationship between Alibaba, Apex and Speedmatrix important. The reproduced records identify specific financial connections; the full contracts are needed to establish the precise rights held by each party.

One headline figure needs a brief qualification. The approximately **$4.15 billion** in Apex’s balance sheet at 31 March 2025 combines prepayments and other receivables. It does not identify all counterparties, so attributing the whole amount to Megaspeed would be unjustified. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source S14">S14</a></sup> The reproduced Megaspeed accounts for 2024 separately identify amounts due from subsidiaries and refundable deposits. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source S27">S27</a></sup>

An example shows the accounting trap. A customer advances money to an operator, which passes funds to a subsidiary buying the servers. Several entries record relationships around the same financing. Adding them produces more money than actually entered the arrangement. The rights attached to an advance explain the funder’s power more clearly than an inflated total.

### A replacement tenant in Malaysia

The commercial consequences reach beyond chip sales. Bloomberg reported in April that Bain Capital-owned Bridge Data Centres had replaced Megaspeed with Zenlayer at a Malaysian facility. A February memorandum to lenders cited by the publication reassigned **68.4 megawatts** of capacity previously reserved for Megaspeed, without explaining the change. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s40" aria-label="Source S40">S40</a></sup>

That power rating describes the facility’s hosting capacity, leaving the precise number of processors and their utilisation unknown. Buildings, servers and customer services can have different owners and contracts. Replacing a tenant therefore raises several commercial issues: rent, the future of installed equipment and continuity for customers.

For banks financing such projects, the right to operate essential hardware contributes to a tenant’s economic value. Losing access to that equipment could cost revenue and threaten rent payments. This is a possible risk transmission, rather than the established reason for Bridge Data Centres’ decision. It explains why export-control disputes can matter to landlords and lenders as well as chipmakers.

## Chinese demand behind Huang’s market-share claim

In an interview on 30 April covered by Yahoo Finance, Huang described Nvidia’s Chinese market share as having fallen to zero. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s35" aria-label="Source S35">S35</a></sup> Culper, meanwhile, estimates that more than **20% of fiscal 2026 compute revenue** was connected to China through alleged diversion. The calculation, relayed by International Cyber Digest, cannot be reproduced from the accessible evidence. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source S27">S27</a></sup>

The numbers answer different questions. One concerns a share of the Chinese market; the other concerns a share of worldwide revenue over a financial year. Treating them as direct opposites would manufacture a contradiction. Nvidia’s published accounts provide a firmer basis for understanding the problem.

The company classifies geographic revenue by the **headquarters of its direct customer**. Its annual report explains that this location can differ from the shipping destination and the end customer. The method changed in the third quarter of fiscal 2026, with comparative figures recast. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s33" aria-label="Source S33">S33</a></sup> For the quarter ended 26 July 2026, Nvidia attributes **$7.88 billion** of revenue to China, including Hong Kong, across the products included in that category. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s34" aria-label="Source S34">S34</a></sup>

Imagine a US-headquartered server manufacturer supplying a Malaysian data centre that then rents computing capacity to a Chinese company. This illustrative transaction involves three locations: the direct customer’s headquarters, the hardware’s location and the user’s location. A headquarters-based sales table can accurately describe the first while revealing little about the third.

The economic question is Nvidia’s underlying dependence on demand from China. How much business exists because Chinese customers want access to its computing capabilities? How much would disappear if those routes closed? Reliable answers would help both investors and regulators measure the commercial exposure attached to restrictions, rather than choosing between a chief executive’s rhetoric and a short seller’s estimates.

## A suspended rule amid mounting suspicions

Washington’s chosen rules also determine how far officials look behind a company. In September 2025, Commerce extended certain restrictions automatically to businesses owned at least **50%** by listed entities. The mechanism was intended to prevent a restricted company from continuing purchases through an unlisted majority-owned subsidiary. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s36" aria-label="Source S36">S36</a></sup>

On 9 November 2026, a rule signed by Jeffrey Kessler extended the suspension of that expansion until **10 January 2027**. Other controls remain in force. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s37" aria-label="Source S37">S37</a></sup> This is a general measure; the documents examined do not establish a particular favour to Nvidia or Megaspeed.

Its effect on the framework is nevertheless significant. Majority ownership alone triggers restrictions less broadly than under the originally announced system. In an industry where purchases pass through subsidiaries and intermediaries, Washington has narrowed an instrument designed to follow those relationships. Officials consequently have more reason to understand the companies whose purchases they approve.

The government also makes an affirmative case for continuing some sales. Commerce presents its H200 policy as protecting national security while preserving American technological leadership. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">S11</a></sup> For Nvidia, retaining customers also sustains software, skills and working habits built around its products. Excluding them may encourage adoption of competing technology. That is a serious industrial argument, and judging it requires a clear account of the associated risks.

## The ballroom and the doors of the White House

While Nvidia seeks access to the Chinese market, it is helping finance the ballroom Trump wants at the White House. Speaking to NPR, *Washington Post* reporter Sarah Blaskey described an internal record tracking contributions and pledges. Nvidia appears with **$25 million**. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s43" aria-label="Source S43">S43</a></sup> Nvidia requested anonymity; it says business interests were separate. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s16" aria-label="Source S16">S16</a></sup>

A year earlier, in November 2025, Associated Press had noted Nvidia’s absence from the public donor list even though Huang had discussed its contribution. The White House said the list was evolving and some companies wanted to delay public identification. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s44" aria-label="Source S44">S44</a></sup>

The documented amount is a commitment; full payment remains to be confirmed. Blaskey told NPR that her team had found overlapping interests but no evidence of an agreed exchange of favours. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s43" aria-label="Source S43">S43</a></sup> The political issue remains: a company awaiting sensitive government decisions is financing a project personally championed by the president.

Lawmakers raised it well before this week. In January, Elizabeth Warren and Dave Min published questions to corporate donors. Their statement criticised Nvidia in particular for sidestepping the question of a quid pro quo. That is the lawmakers’ assessment; the original company response, linked as a PDF, remained inaccessible to us. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s17" aria-label="Source S17">S17</a></sup>

The institutional problem begins before proof of corruption. Security decisions require distance from private interests. Funding the presidential project creates an additional relationship between the applicant and the power deciding its case. The public is then asked to trust assurances of independence from officials who maintain that proximity.

A separate financial interest belongs to the government itself. The proclamation of 14 January establishes a **25% tariff** on specified advanced-chip imports, with exclusions tied to their use in the United States. It accompanies the revised H200 policy and applies to defined transactions, rather than Nvidia’s entire revenue. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s18" aria-label="Source S18">S18</a></sup>

The government can therefore collect revenue from trade it also scrutinises for security reasons. The income enters public accounts; the consequences of access to sensitive capabilities could extend far beyond the seller and customer. That tension calls for an explicit policy judgment. A tariff receipt measures money collected. It says little about the risk accepted alongside it.

## The board investigates its own chief executive

Inside Nvidia, the crisis reaches those responsible for overseeing Huang. On 8 November, the board maintained its confidence in him and said it had then found no evidence of compromise to company interests or national security. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s23" aria-label="Source S23">S23</a></sup> Two days later, independent directors announced that Joon H. Kim and Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz would conduct the investigation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s20" aria-label="Source S20">S20</a></sup>

Their findings will matter. Huang runs the company whose employees must supply evidence about his decisions and relationships. Directors need genuine independence to obtain communications and hear staff accounts. Their work will be judged by their ability to follow those records up to the chief executive, including if the findings challenge their initial statement of confidence.

A shareholder has also brought a case, Associated Press reports. Those are allegations filed in court; the full complaint was unavailable to us. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s42" aria-label="Source S42">S42</a></sup> In Congress, three Republican committee chairs requested a closed briefing with Huang for **18 November**. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s41" aria-label="Source S41">S41</a></sup> Brian Mast subsequently raised the prospect of a subpoena and set an 11 November response deadline, according to ABC. At our verification cutoff, that threat remained distinct from an issued subpoena. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s22" aria-label="Source S22">S22</a></sup> Democrats Gregory Meeks, Gabe Amo and Sydney Kamlager-Dove are also seeking answers from Lutnick. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s19" aria-label="Source S19">S19</a></sup>

These proceedings could move the affair beyond corporate statements and presidential assurances. They should illuminate the handling of warnings, instructions given to investigators and the conditions under which sales continued. The answers will establish responsibility more effectively than expressions of loyalty to Huang.

## The danger of an untouchable national champion

The gravity of the affair rests on an accumulation of public evidence: a chief executive acknowledges an affair as his board investigates security concerns; a customer faces scrutiny; journalists describe interventions with enforcement officials; and the supplier funds a presidential project while seeking sensitive approvals. Espionage, corruption and organised concealment remain allegations to be established. The clash between commercial interests, political proximity and independent oversight is already visible.

Nvidia possesses technology that the United States considers essential to its power. That importance makes scrutiny more necessary. Every argument for exceptional treatment, every obstacle put in an investigator’s way and every additional financial relationship with political power increases the risk that officials confuse protecting the country with protecting the company.

National security involves the public as a whole. Decisions about access to such sensitive capabilities must remain with institutions able to withstand pressure from the supplier, the customer and the president. **An industrial champion the state ceases to scrutinise becomes a national vulnerability.**
