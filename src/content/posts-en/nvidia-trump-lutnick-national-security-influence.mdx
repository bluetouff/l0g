**A Chinese military-linked university buys advanced AI chips. A customer of Nvidia faces investigation. Reporting describes interventions at the top of the department overseeing its purchases. The affair now reaches the Trump administration itself: how far can a government protect its industrial champion when its own officials identify national-security risks?**

*Documentary investigation current to 11 November 2026. This follow-up develops our [earlier Nvidia investigation](/en/analysis/nvidia-trump-warning-at-the-top/) with subsequent reporting. Reported allegations remain attributed; the assessment of institutional risk is our analysis.*

The starting point is a procurement notice for two H200s, processors designed for artificial-intelligence computing. Reuters reports their acquisition by Northwestern Polytechnical University in Xi’an, alongside five contracts for rented computing capacity. The chips’ original supply route remains unknown. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source S01">S01</a></sup>

The notice, dated 21 October and retrieved through its indexed text, identifies the management school, a Chinese supplier and a total price of **CNY 874,700**. The live page now returns an error. It records an award, with the evidential limits that entails. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source S02">S02</a></sup>

For Washington, the institution is a familiar concern. It has been listed for US trade restrictions since 2001. Commerce described its military connections in a separate hydrophone-export case that resulted in a conviction in 2021. That case involved concealment of the end user, with different equipment and defendants. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source S03">S03</a></sup>

The management school’s procurement leaves the cards’ precise journey, actual delivery and use unresolved. It establishes neither a direct Nvidia sale nor a particular military application. It nevertheless presents officials with a concrete case to explain, just as their own decisions face mounting scrutiny.

## Nvidia approaches the investigators

The *New York Times* reports that Nvidia lawyer Tim Teter contacted Commerce investigators about Megaspeed in August 2025, invoking permission from their superior Jeffrey Kessler. The agents declined a meeting. An official disputes that Kessler ordered information to be disclosed. Justice subsequently reportedly asked Commerce to pause its work to protect a criminal investigation that remained active. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">S04</a></sup>

Megaspeed is a Singapore-based data-centre operator, a Nvidia customer and the subject of suspected chip-diversion investigations. The supplier has a direct commercial stake in its customer’s future. A chief legal officer invoking the investigators’ superior to approach them creates a clear conflict of roles: the company under scrutiny enjoys access to the person overseeing that scrutiny.

Bloomberg separately describes a late-summer 2025 call from Teter to Kessler, followed by instructions requiring Kessler’s approval for requests to Nvidia and restricting external references to Megaspeed. Nvidia describes its government contacts as cooperation against diversion. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source S05">S05</a></sup>

The most alarming feature of these accounts is the shift of power up the hierarchy: investigators’ requests become dependent on approval from above. The reported transfer to Justice would have allowed the criminal inquiry to continue. It makes Commerce’s account of the obstacles encountered inside its own department all the more urgent.

Howard Lutnick leads that department. The accessible evidence does not establish that he personally ordered an investigation stopped. His political responsibility is nevertheless clear: protect investigators’ independence, explain instructions and account for export decisions. The secretary must answer for the department he runs.

The *Financial Times*, as reported by *Asia Business Daily*, also describes a delay in adding Megaspeed to the restricted-entity list despite other departments’ requests. Commerce reportedly sought further information about some companies under consideration; whether that explanation applied to Megaspeed remains unclear. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s21" aria-label="Source S21">S21</a></sup>

A restricted-entity listing can tighten access while risks are assessed. A criminal investigation seeks to establish an offence. Waiting for the latter can allow transactions to continue that the former is intended to constrain. That is the practical significance of the interagency dispute reported by the FT, whose original decision records remain unavailable.

## A written warning to Lutnick in October 2025

On 13 October 2025, Republican Senate Intelligence Committee chair Tom Cotton wrote to Lutnick about Megaspeed. He requested a thorough investigation of its ownership, China links and relationships with American manufacturers. The letter draws on press reporting and advances a political allegation; its contents need to be distinguished from established findings. Its date, addressee and request are public. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s25" aria-label="Source S25">S25</a></sup>

That document changes the chronology. Megaspeed’s risks were publicly put before the secretary more than a year before the current revelations about Huang’s private life. Commercial routes, buyer identity and enforcement were already at issue. Reducing the affair to an executive’s romantic gullibility would erase that history.

On 10 November, the *New York Times* reported that Marco Rubio had orally warned Trump of an espionage risk targeting Huang aboard Air Force One before the May trip to China. Trump disputes receiving that warning then; the White House disputes the account. A written memorandum discussed separately was intended for other officials. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s06" aria-label="Source S06">S06</a></sup>

These accounts place the affair at two levels of government: the White House and the department granting export approvals. Receipt records, reading dates and subsequent decisions are needed to establish each official’s precise knowledge. Cotton’s public letter already gives Lutnick a specific question to answer: which checks did his department initiate after that request?

On 8 November, Huang acknowledged an affair and pledged cooperation with the board’s investigation into national-security concerns. His message leaves his partner unnamed. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">S08</a></sup> Singapore has denied conducting the spying investigation into Alice Huang attributed to it by the *Wall Street Journal*, while confirming an investigation into Megaspeed. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source S09">S09</a></sup>

The security risk concerns information available to an executive, potential pressure on him and his professional decisions. Huang’s admission establishes personal misconduct. Espionage allegations require their own evidence. Access, exchanges and commercial choices should therefore anchor the investigation, rather than the spectacle of the affair.

Trump continues to defend Huang while accounts of warnings he received are disputed. That political protection calls for oversight outside the executive branch. Describing it as a *cover-up* would require documented concealment or obstruction. The political problem is already apparent: the president publicly supports the executive whose risks his officials must examine. His personal judgement can weigh on the entire hierarchy.

## On 9 November, Washington extends a regulatory gap

A *Federal Register* notice adds a directly verifiable decision to the record. Effective **9 November 2026**, Commerce extends until **10 January 2027** the suspension of its rule covering affiliates of certain restricted entities. Jeffrey Kessler signed it. This is a general policy measure extending beyond Nvidia; its existence and scope are public. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s37" aria-label="Source S37">S37</a></sup>

The original rule, published on 30 September 2025, extended certain licensing requirements to foreign entities owned **50% or more**, directly or indirectly, individually or in aggregate, by designated entities. Its stated purpose included preventing circumvention through subsidiaries operating under different names. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s36" aria-label="Source S36">S36</a></sup>

The practical difference matters. A list of names requires officials to identify and designate each relevant business. An ownership rule extends restrictions to subsidiaries defined by their shareholders. Suspending that extension increases the importance of officials’ ability to follow corporate structures and make the necessary individual designations. The FT’s account of disagreement over listing Megaspeed makes that distinction politically significant.

The suspension concerns this particular extension. Other controls on products, destinations and uses continue to apply, while Treasury financial sanctions operate under their own rules. Describing a business as an affiliate in a news report also leaves a legal question unresolved: its ownership at the relevant date must be tested against the applicable rule.

**The administration has several enforcement tools and chooses which to activate.** It determines licensing policy, participates in individual designations and postpones an automatic extension of controls. Any assessment of its leniency must include those written decisions. Their justification should withstand scrutiny independent of Nvidia’s commercial assurances and the president’s personal preferences.

## The border also runs through a login

The rule published on 15 January 2026 allows case-by-case consideration of applications involving certain advanced chips, including H200s. It imposes commercial and technical conditions, independent testing in the United States and restrictions on prohibited users and military uses. It also addresses remote access and the identification of certain computing-service users. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source S10">S10</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">S11</a></sup>

Consider a server located abroad. Its address identifies the hardware’s location. A rental contract identifies a buyer of computing capacity. Accounts and permissions show who can actually run a workload. Each answers a different question; connecting them gives [export controls](/en/glossary/controle-export/) practical meaning.

## Red flags require suppliers to look beyond the customer’s assurances

US guidance gives suppliers a concrete responsibility. The BIS, Commerce’s export-control agency, identifies warning signs including orders inconsistent with a buyer’s business and unusual refusals of installation or testing. When such information reaches a company, the guidance requires inquiry into the suspicious circumstances and actual destination. It also addresses deliberate self-blinding, including arrangements that prevent unwelcome information from reaching decision-makers. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s38" aria-label="Source S38">S38</a></sup>

For this investigation, the practical issue is which anomalies reached sales teams, lawyers and management, and how they were handled. A customer’s registration document may satisfy one administrative request while leaving a mismatch between orders, available facilities and users unresolved. A successful site visit establishes something about a place at a particular time. Compliance requires reconciling those observations when they conflict.

Potential liability depends on the facts known and the obligations applying to each transaction. Investigators need visit reports, destination changes, internal reservations and the responses they prompted. Management’s message to staff matters too: can employees suspend a lucrative shipment and escalate a concern to the board? That autonomy is a governance issue even before a criminal conclusion is reached.

### Telemetry measures equipment; investigation identifies beneficiaries

On 10 December 2025, Nvidia announced an optional, customer-installed fleet-management service reporting processor use, configuration and errors. It described read-only telemetry and a planned open-source agent. The announcement establishes the proposed functions, while deployment at each relevant installation requires separate evidence. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s39" aria-label="Source S39">S39</a></sup>

An operational record can help confirm that equipment is running in its declared environment. Identifying the beneficiary of its computing power also requires contracts, authorised accounts and service logs. The operator hosting a server and the organisation training a model can be different parties. Effective oversight must follow that relationship throughout the use of the capacity.

An audit should therefore reconcile serial numbers, shipments, installations and usage rights, with proportionate access to sensitive information. This is our analytical recommendation from the documented mechanisms. Claims of a magical ability to locate every chip would distract from the ordinary work on which enforcement depends: obtaining reliable records, identifying inconsistencies and enabling investigators to act.

Aivres illustrates that layer: its catalogue offers the KR6288 with eight Nvidia H200 processors. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s32" aria-label="Source S32">S32</a></sup> A system buyer can acquire American chips without appearing as Nvidia’s direct customer.

Aivres also has a documented corporate history. In an order dated **10 March 2025**, Judge Jon Tigar describes it as a California corporation formerly called Inspur Systems. The record warrants examination of continuing commercial relationships through name changes. That means establishing ownership, management and contracts at the time of relevant shipments. Assigning the order a criminal export-control finding would turn a useful lead into an unsupported accusation. The industrial supply chain deserves an investigation strong enough to withstand that objection.

## Supermicro: a criminal case exposes a diversion mechanism

A separate case provides a concrete example. On **19 March 2026**, federal prosecutors in New York announced charges against Wally Liaw, Steven Chang and Willy Sun. Their account describes a Southeast Asian buyer represented as the end user, servers shipped to China and dummy equipment presented for inspections. Prosecutors put purchases at approximately **$2.5 billion during 2024–2025**. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s28" aria-label="Source S28">S28</a></sup>

On **8 October**, Sun pleaded guilty to four counts, including conspiracies involving export controls and obstruction of justice, Reuters reported from a court filing. Supermicro reiterated that the company was not a named defendant. Sun’s plea concerns his own responsibility; the proceedings determine those of the other individuals separately. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s29" aria-label="Source S29">S29</a></sup>

The gap between an apparent inventory and actual use is crucial. An inspection confined to equipment presented on site can be defeated by a staged display. Oversight becomes more robust when physical identifiers are reconciled with shipping documents, payments and operating histories. Those comparisons allow investigators to seek contradictions across records held by different organisations.

In its **20 August** statement, Supermicro said its independent investigation found no evidence that current management knew of the alleged diversion. It also announced internal disciplinary action, including dismissals, and compliance improvements adopted by the board. These are the company’s published conclusions, alongside continuing public proceedings. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s30" aria-label="Source S30">S30</a></sup>

The prosecutors’ statement calls the buyer “Company-1”. Culper’s suggested connection to Megaspeed remains unproven. The judicial material consulted leaves that identity anonymous. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source S27">S27</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s28" aria-label="Source S28">S28</a></sup> The documented lesson concerns concealment and the need for cross-checks. Formally joining the two cases requires identification absent from our evidence.

The case nevertheless makes the issue tangible. A diversion network can involve employees, intermediaries and customers, each holding part of the information. A policy built around the customer’s declared honesty leaves room for participants to support one another’s accounts. Independent oversight must break that reliance on the commercial chain’s own explanations.

## The balance sheet suggests a circuit; contracts define the rights

The thread reproduces 2024 Megaspeed accounts separating subsidiary receivables from refundable deposits received. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source S27">S27</a></sup> That makes the financing lead worth pursuing while requiring accounting discipline: entries on different sides of a balance sheet may represent successive stages of one funding arrangement. Adding them would create a fictitious total of fresh money.

Consider an illustrative arrangement, rather than an established description of these companies. A customer advances money to reserve computing capacity. The operator passes funds to a subsidiary, which orders servers. The same financing can appear as an obligation to the customer, a receivable from the subsidiary, the subsidiary’s liability and a supplier payment. Each entry describes a relationship. Their mechanical sum cannot measure the project’s total cost.

To understand the financier’s power, terms matter more than a stack of billion-dollar figures. An advance can reserve capacity. A refundable deposit can create a cash repayment obligation. Security can provide recourse against assets following a contractual breach. These rights may combine to make an operator heavily dependent on a counterparty with little or no equity ownership.

Here, the reproduced documents identify relationships requiring verification; complete accounts and contracts remain missing. The decisive step would reconcile the financier, security holder, capacity customer and authorised users. That would turn a concern about dependence into an evidenced account of economic control.

## “Zero in China” and the revenue geography problem

The thread relays Culper’s estimate of more than **20% of fiscal 2026 compute revenue** linked to China through alleged diversion. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source S27">S27</a></sup> That remains an interested party’s estimate whose calculation we could not reproduce. Presenting it as an established fraud rate would mislead readers.

In a 30 April interview reported by Yahoo Finance, Huang described Nvidia’s market share in China as having fallen to zero. He was arguing for commercial access in the face of US restrictions. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s35" aria-label="Source S35">S35</a></sup> A share of the Chinese market at a particular moment and a share of worldwide annual revenue answer different questions. Comparing them requires matching product scope, period and the definition of the final customer.

Nvidia’s own accounts identify a genuine difficulty. Its annual report assigns geographic revenue according to the **headquarters of direct customers**, explaining that final customers and shipping destinations may differ. The method changed in the third quarter of fiscal 2026, with comparative periods recast. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s33" aria-label="Source S33">S33</a></sup> The subsequent quarterly filing reports **$7.88 billion** for China, including Hong Kong, in the quarter ended 26 July 2026, across the products included in that geographic category. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s34" aria-label="Source S34">S34</a></sup>

That is a published accounting category. It leaves open the use of chips elsewhere by Chinese customers, as well as lawful sales of products to China-headquartered buyers. Headquarters-based revenue is therefore a starting point for analysis with a limitation explicitly recognised in the accounts.

Imagine a sale to a US-headquartered system builder, delivered to a Malaysian data centre and used by a Chinese tenant. This illustrative transaction has three geographies: statistical revenue attribution, hardware location and final demand. Following only the first can obscure economic concentration in the last, even when the financial disclosure follows its stated definition correctly.

A useful transparency request would address exposure to final customers, required authorisations and revenue vulnerable to their withdrawal. It would also distinguish complete-server import values, orders, recognised revenue and advances already received. For investors and authorities alike, that breakdown would be more revealing than a contest between “zero” and “twenty per cent”.

The state can receive revenue from transactions it subjects to security review. The tension is clear: commercial and fiscal gains are visible, while the potential costs of transferring sensitive capabilities are collectively borne. The ballroom contribution and customs revenue follow separate channels. Both make an independently reasoned security decision essential, with criteria that withstand immediate financial interests.

## A national champion can become a blind spot

Protecting a strategic industry can require difficult choices. Restricting sales costs the supplier business and can encourage customers to develop competing products. Permitting sales sustains commercial relationships and spreads a technological ecosystem. Commerce presents its framework as a balance between American leadership and security. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">S11</a></sup>

The danger emerges when a company’s success becomes a reason to relax scrutiny of that same company. The more a government identifies its strategy with a champion, the more politically costly an investigation into that champion becomes. Officials may come to treat a warning as an obstacle to their own programme.

This is the risk of regulatory capture: the overseer gradually adopts the priorities of the business it oversees. The reported interventions, export approvals and financial relationships give that analysis concrete foundations here. Establishing individual intentions requires more evidence; examining institutional safeguards is already urgent.

A company’s nationality identifies its legal home. It offers no automatic guarantee about every commercial decision or distribution channel. Security depends on obligations that apply as rigorously to the champion as to its competitors.

## The public cost of a politically protected sale

There is a coherent commercial argument for exports. Customers using American technology may buy subsequent generations, train staff on its software and support the supplier’s ecosystem. Restrictions can accelerate the search for alternatives. That strategic hypothesis deserves serious consideration, including the conditions required for it to work. It depends in part on preserving a technological advantage while limiting access to the most sensitive uses.

Interests diverge at the point of sale. A supplier knows the contract’s revenue, costs and contribution to its targets. The commercial benefit is immediate and identifiable. Potential harm from transferring sensitive capabilities may emerge later, affect other institutions and be difficult to quantify. Economists call this an externality: a private decision can impose some of its consequences on the wider public.

Government revenue complicates the calculation further. An authorised transaction that also produces tax receipts gives the decision-maker both a fiscal interest and an oversight duty. A defensible decision should explain the risk accepted, the checks required and the circumstances triggering review. Invoking American industrial strength leaves those questions unresolved.

The problem becomes acute when monitoring a customer depends on information supplied by the equipment vendor. That vendor has essential expertise and an interest in continued sales. Independent checks exist to test its explanations, compare records and resolve disagreements. The reported interventions with Commerce leadership concern precisely that point: investigators’ freedom to act when commercial cooperation reaches its limits.

The strength of a policy is tested by a costly decision: delaying a sale, reconsidering a customer or disclosing a failure involving a national champion. If rules become more negotiable as the business grows, commercial success itself supplies additional protection. The security risk and the risk of regulatory capture reinforce each other.

This analysis reaches beyond a dispute between Nvidia’s supporters and detractors. It tests the coherence of the stated policy. **Washington cannot promise credible strategic oversight while treating proximity to its champion as a substitute for verification.** The available record makes institutional independence and the traceability of decisions after warnings the urgent issues.

## The inquiry must reach the decision-makers

On 9 November, Democratic lawmakers Gregory Meeks, Gabe Amo and Sydney Kamlager-Dove asked Lutnick to explain the warnings received and the rationale for continued export approvals. Kim and Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz would conduct their investigation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s20" aria-label="Source S20">S20</a></sup> That mandate can examine executive conduct and company procedures. Scrutiny of federal decisions belongs to the relevant public authorities.

The board had maintained its confidence in Huang on 8 November and said it then had no reason to believe Nvidia secrets or national security had been compromised. That is its initial position, to be tested against the investigation’s findings. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s23" aria-label="Source S23">S23</a></sup>

The decisive records are now identifiable. Nvidia’s exchanges with Commerce could clarify the interventions. Warning notifications and licensing decisions could establish who knew what and when. Contracts, beneficiaries and technical access records could connect financing with the use of computing capacity. Such scrutiny can protect classified information while making responsibilities, procedures and the essential reasoning public.

The Republican request also has an official record. A 5 November China committee statement announces a letter from John Moolenaar, Brian Mast and Rick Crawford seeking Huang’s participation in a closed-door briefing on **18 November**, concerning his contacts and company security procedures. At our reporting cutoff, that remains a requested meeting. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s41" aria-label="Source S41">S41</a></sup>

AP also reports a shareholder action against Nvidia and Huang alleging that investors were misled. The complaint creates another legal front; its allegations await adjudication. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s42" aria-label="Source S42">S42</a></sup>

For the board, the investigation’s quality will depend on effective access to information: preserving communications, examining commercial decisions, interviewing employees and addressing any conflicts among directors themselves. Its conclusions should distinguish established facts, procedural failures and remedial action. A private inquiry may obtain useful internal records; government oversight and criminal investigation have different powers and address different responsibilities.

Congress can demand a timeline of warnings and decisions, question departments separately and compare their accounts. Protecting intelligence can justify confidential proceedings. It still leaves room for a public account of the procedures followed, responsible authorities and reasons for maintaining or postponing restrictions. That is the minimum needed to assess whether actual decisions match the national-security rhetoric.

**Nvidia’s prestige becomes dangerous if it places the company’s decisions beyond effective scrutiny.** Washington has made the power of its chips a national-security priority. That ambition requires equally rigorous examination of foreign buyers, supplier interventions and decisions inside the administration. An industrial champion powerful enough to unsettle those checks becomes a strategic risk itself.

## Method and limitations

This investigation compares official rules, statements from the parties, attributed reporting by major news organisations and the reproduced records in the Culper thread and the C4ADS report. It also examines Nvidia’s SEC filings, prosecutors’ statements, an order in the HPE/Inspur litigation and the new affiliate-control rules. The International Cyber Digest thread was read on X and treated as leads requiring corroboration. Sun’s plea is reported by Reuters and the shareholder complaint by AP; their full court records were not independently reviewed. The Chinese notice and Cotton letter were read through their indexed texts. Classified memoranda, original emails, licensing files, complete Apex and Speedmatrix filings and the complete Megaspeed criminal case file remain outside our source material. The full Culper report remained inaccessible. The Chinese award notice was read through its indexed text after the live page failed.

Responses quoted or summarised come from the publications consulted. l0g conducted no interviews or direct requests for comment for this update. The diagrams explain documented relationships or regulatory requirements; arrows imply neither traffic volumes nor payment amounts. The opening artwork is a generated editorial illustration symbolising the relationship between technology and political power.
