The European Union could meet all its gas demand in an ordinary winter and still finish March with storage just **13% full**. That is one case modelled by ENTSOG, the European network of gas transmission operators. It assumes reference winter demand, reduced Russian pipeline supply and tight availability of liquefied natural gas (LNG). Demand is met through the winter. By spring buyers are back in the market to refill their stores. [S02](#source-S02)

On **17 November 2026**, EU underground storage was **70% full**, holding about **792.1 TWh of [working gas](/en/glossary/gaz-utile/)**, the portion that facilities can withdraw in normal operation. The snapshot comes from Gas Infrastructure Europe (GIE) data published by the Swiss Federal Office of Energy. The International Energy Agency (IEA) says EU inventories entered the heating season at their lowest since 2013. [S01](#source-S01) · [S03](#source-S03) · [S07](#source-S07)

## Gas inventories vary across the EU

On 17 November, Germany’s stores were **57.1%** full (141.3 TWh), France’s **75.5%** (93.5 TWh), Italy’s **86.5%** (175.9 TWh) and Austria’s **62.7%** (62.9 TWh). Italy held more working gas than Germany in absolute terms. How much of that gas could help customers elsewhere during a demand spike depends on the rate at which it can be withdrawn and the available capacity between networks. [S02](#source-S02) · [S03](#source-S03)

ACER, the EU energy regulator, reported stocks at **75%** on 21 October. The five-point fall by 17 November spans the transition into withdrawal season. ENTSOG’s model starts earlier, from **72%** on 1 October. The latest November reading and the model’s starting inventory describe separate dates. [S02](#source-S02) · [S03](#source-S03) · [S04](#source-S04)

## Hormuz raises the price of a replacement cargo

Qatar and the United Arab Emirates loaded **over 60 billion cubic metres less LNG** from March to October 2026 than a year earlier, the IEA reports. Output elsewhere grew by **over 40 billion cubic metres**. Global LNG production still fell by around **20 billion cubic metres** over the period. In its outlook published on **19 November**, the IEA says disruption to flows through Hormuz is continuing. [S01](#source-S01) · [S10](#source-S10)

Qatar supplied about **8%** of EU LNG imports in 2025, according to ENTSOG. ACER says the EU received very little Qatari LNG in summer 2026. The United States supplied **64%** of LNG imports that summer, equivalent to about **30%** of total gas imports when pipelines are included. A European buyer looking to replace a cargo bids against Asian buyers for the deliveries still available. That is how a disruption can raise European prices even when few Qatari tankers were headed to EU ports. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02) · [S04](#source-S04)

Europe has technical capacity to regasify around **145 billion cubic metres over a winter season**, according to ENTSOG. That is the capacity of terminals across the system to turn LNG back into gas and send it into the grid. New capacity and more routes for imports help explain why the European Commission considers the system capable of operating with lower stocks. The gas actually delivered still depends on purchases and the network’s ability to get it to customers. [S02](#source-S02) · [S06](#source-S06)

## Partly depleted stores deliver gas more slowly

In a cold spell, households use more heat and gas-fired power stations can be called on at the same time. The electricity network operators’ winter assessment of 13 November highlights the role of those plants when other generation is less available. Storage [withdrawal capacity](/en/glossary/capacite-de-soutirage/) is the amount of gas a site can supply each day. Pressure, the geology of the reservoir and its compressors all matter. [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09)

The IEA says withdrawal rates tend to fall as storage drops below **30%**. This is a point of reference for Europe. The rate of decline varies between facilities. A market facing a sharp rise in demand can still have gas underground and struggle to deliver enough of it that day. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02) · [S09](#source-S09)

## LNG availability reshapes the winter balance

In ENTSOG’s reference winter cases with minimised Russian pipeline supply, demand is met with **29%** left in storage at the end of March under optimal LNG availability and **13%** under tight LNG. In the model, tight supply means **20%** less LNG is available than in the optimal case. An additional disruption of Russian pipeline supplies leaves **11%** in storage in the tight-LNG case and creates a **2%** need for demand response or a potential shortfall. [S02](#source-S02)

In a cold winter with minimised Russian flows, the need reaches **7%** under optimal LNG and **12%** under tight LNG. In both cases, the **11%** left represents strategic reserves governed by national release rules. The demand figures cover the model’s wider European area, including countries outside the EU. They measure demand that would have to adjust or could go unmet. With high LNG availability, the model meets cold-winter demand and leaves storage **20% full**. [S02](#source-S02)

A separate IEA exercise puts end-March 2027 inventories **below 10%** if the winter is colder than average and disruption through Hormuz continues. Both exercises show how simultaneous shocks can affect the gas balance, with their own assumptions. Our [October analysis of Europe’s LNG cargoes](/en/analysis/european-gas-bridging-gap-between-cargoes/) traces the supply position at the start of the season. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02)

## Prices can bite before physical restrictions

ACER reported spot prices approaching **€90/MWh in mid-October 2026** at major European gas hubs. It estimated the Middle East conflict could add **€18–33 billion** to the summer import bill at unchanged volumes. The estimate gives an indicative upper-bound range against pre-conflict prices, depending on whether the calculation uses day-ahead or monthly [TTF](/en/glossary/ttf/) prices. Contract dates and hedges influence when a higher wholesale price reaches a particular customer. [S04](#source-S04)

A manufacturer whose furnaces burn gas can shorten production runs once fuel consumes its margin. Gas-fired generation can pass through some of that increase to wholesale electricity prices when it sets the marginal price. Firms and customers can thus bear part of the cost before any formal restriction on use. [S01](#source-S01) · [S04](#source-S04) · [S08](#source-S08)

## Low spring stocks can still matter in September

ENTSOG extends its model through summer 2027 with reference demand, tight LNG and minimised Russian pipeline flows. Starting at **30% on 1 April** leaves the EU **82% full at the end of September**. Starting at **13%** leaves it at **65%**. The **17-point** starting gap persists through the summer under those assumptions, while European buyers still have to compete for LNG with Asia. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02)

EU rules set a **90%** filling target to be reached at some point between **1 October and 1 December**, with flexibility for conditions including difficult markets and technical constraints. ENTSOG’s September results show how much work might remain ahead of that window. The obligations apply at national level, with provisions for the circumstances of individual countries. [S02](#source-S02) · [S05](#source-S05)

Steady arrivals can make up for some of the shortfall in stocks. If a series of cold spells draws inventories down early, the flow available during the next peak matters more than the headline fill rate. In spring, the inventory left behind determines how much gas buyers still have to find before winter returns. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02) · [S03](#source-S03)
