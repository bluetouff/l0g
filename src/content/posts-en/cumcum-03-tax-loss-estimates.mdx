**The cum-cum investigation, part 3**

The number fits on a single line: **€33.27 billion for France**. Its origins are easier to find than its frequent repetition might suggest. Open a five-page report dated 14 October 2021 and look for the French entry in the table. The figure covers 2000 through 2020. It comes from a tax-loss model developed at the University of Mannheim with the investigative journalism consortium CORRECTIV. The calculation is publicly described. The individual transactions needed to account for every euro are outside the scope of that table. [S01, pp.2-5](#source-S01)

Five years later, another figure appears in a ministerial response to the French Senate. On 15 October 2026, the government reported **€4.5 billion in additional tax notified** to paying institutions. It also referred to fines paid under judicial settlements, without providing an itemised total of cash receipts. [S04, ministerial response](#source-S04)

The two numbers practically invite a calculation. Subtract one from the other, or divide them, and a recovery rate seems to emerge. The answer would be appealing. It would also combine figures built to answer different questions, with reporting periods that have yet to be reconciled.

The cost of cum-cum is scattered across several bodies of evidence. Researchers estimate the wider revenue shortfall. Tax authorities pursue claims arising from the cases they investigate. Judicial settlements require further payments under a different framework. Following those records helps establish the scale of the problem and identify the information still needed for a national accounting.

## Valuing the tax that should have been withheld

The [mechanism explained in the first instalment](/en/analysis/cum-cum-01-dividend-mechanism/) takes advantage of differences in the tax treatment of shareholders. A non-resident investor temporarily transfers shares to an intermediary whose position allows the dividend to escape [withholding tax](/en/glossary/retenue-a-la-source/). That intermediary may be resident in France or benefit from a different treaty arrangement. [S09, description of the referral](#source-S09)

Estimating the loss requires a [counterfactual](/en/glossary/contrefactuel/): how much tax would have been payable without the arrangement being challenged? Consider a **wholly illustrative example**. A €100 dividend would have incurred €15 of withholding tax. An arrangement keeps those €15 within the private transaction chain. The investor and intermediary may split the benefit between them, but that contractual allocation does not by itself change the initial withholding shortfall.

The example assumes that the €15 was genuinely due. In an actual case, establishing the liability requires identifying the recipient, their residence, the relevant treaty and the rules in force when the dividend was paid. An investor may legitimately qualify for a reduced rate. That lawful entitlement belongs in the counterfactual; otherwise, the calculation attributes to fraud revenue the state was never entitled to collect. ESMA's description of dividend arbitrage illustrates the importance of the counterparties' tax positions. [S08, paragraphs 21-27](#source-S08)

The object being measured also matters. French withholding tax avoided, the participants' gains and the eventual effect on several countries' public finances are different quantities. Moving between them requires information on fees, expenses, tax paid elsewhere and available credits. Without it, the defensible approach is to retain a narrow, intelligible definition: an estimate of withholding-tax revenue forgone in the country under examination.

For the same reason, a tax refund cannot simply be entered as a fraudulent loss. What matters is the entitlement to that refund and the transactions that created it. A payment leaving the public treasury tells us little, on its own, about its legal basis.

## Rebuilding France's €33.27 billion estimate

The model starts with dividends attributed to foreign shareholders: about **€443.60 billion** in its French sample over 2000–2020. It assumes that half were affected by cum-cum and applies a **15%** tax rate. The result is **€33.27 billion**, with the full calculation set out below the figure. [S01, p.3; l0g calculation](#source-S01)

The arithmetic is reproducible. The harder questions concern the construction of the dividend base and the choice of parameters. Assuming that half the dividends attributable to foreign shareholders were affected is an assumption about incidence. It determines how much of that base is exposed to the tax loss in the model.

CORRECTIV says the assumption draws on discussions with tax authorities and market participants, alongside plausibility checks. The consortium describes its approach as conservative. That characterisation belongs to the authors: it is an assessment of their assumptions, rather than a statistical guarantee about the direction in which the estimate may be wrong. [S02, Cum-Cum section](#source-S02)

The displayed precision comes from multiplication; the uncertainty lies chiefly in the assumptions. An estimate can be calculated to the nearest euro even when participation remains poorly measured. The **€33.27 billion** figure represents a modelled loss, whose assumptions and inputs require scrutiny.

The model spans **21 calendar years**, from 2000 through 2020. The average shown in the figure’s method helps convey its historical scale. Estimating current losses would require evidence for recent years.

## Where the assumptions carry the weight

The report uses the ownership structure observed in summer 2021 as a proxy across the full period, and does not revise index constituents year by year. Holders whose residence cannot be established are treated as foreign. The underlying commercial data come from Eikon and Bloomberg. [S01, p.5](#source-S01)

The model also assumes that the transactions were feasible in every country and year covered. It does not incorporate period-specific statutory restrictions, relying on CORRECTIV’s reporting to support that assumption. [S01, p.5, assumption 4](#source-S01)

These choices have different implications. A late snapshot of ownership may overstate or understate the foreign share in earlier years. Classifying unidentified residents as foreign may enlarge the assumed base. Holding an index's composition fixed changes which companies represent the past. Quantifying the effects would require the historical series and the files used to construct the model.

We did not obtain those detailed commercial datasets. This review therefore checks the published tables, the arithmetic and the stated methodological limitations. A complete independent replication would require rebuilding dividends, ownership and tax residence at a much finer level.

The banking industry disputes the estimate. At a parliamentary hearing on 8 July 2025, French Banking Federation (FBF) chief executive Maya Atig argued that its assumptions overstated the loss, challenging the foreign-investor share, the 50% participation assumption and the treatment of exempt investors. The federation represents the banks. Resolving these points requires comparable company samples, dates, weights and tax entitlements. [S11, hearing](#source-S11)

There is a serious argument in the authors' favour. Concealed activity rarely generates a comprehensive, ready-made dataset. Waiting for every case to be adjudicated before estimating the scale of the problem would limit measurement to what has already been discovered. Journalism and modelling help reveal a fiscal risk extending beyond the transactions described in public proceedings. That contribution is entirely compatible with close scrutiny of the assumptions.

Restricting the sample to major indices leaves other shares outside the analysis. This can understate the economic universe in which relevant transactions might occur. Conversely, applying a uniform participation assumption can overstate activity in particular segments. Those effects do not automatically cancel out. Their coexistence is enough to prevent a model result from becoming a demonstrated minimum.

Market data can test parts of the account, but have limitations of their own. ESMA's September 2020 report notes that higher trading volumes around dividend dates may reflect ordinary market reactions. Its securities-lending observations provide evidence relevant to dividend arbitrage; they do not independently establish a monetary tax loss. [S08, paragraphs 167-170](#source-S08)

A volume spike can tell investigators where to look. Turning that signal into an amount of tax owed requires identifying holders, contracts, withholding payments and refunds. This evidential chain helps explain why a market-wide estimate and an administrative case file operate at different scales.

## Participation assumptions change the estimate

France's model sensitivity is immediately visible if the dividend base and the 15% rate are held constant. A hypothetical 25% participation rate yields **€16.64 billion**; 50% produces **€33.27 billion**; 75% gives **€49.91 billion**. These are l0g calculations using the published base, not three fresh measurements of historical losses. [S01, p.3; l0g calculations](#source-S01)

## Putting Europe's borders back into the total

Mannheim's published benchmark total is **€140.99 billion**. It covers ten countries, including the United States. Adding only the European country rows produces **€136.13 billion for nine countries**. Removing Switzerland as well leaves **€131.30 billion for the eight EU member states covered**. [S01, p.3; l0g aggregation](#source-S01)

Those eight countries are Austria, Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Spain. The subtotal reflects their inclusion in this particular model. Other EU members fall outside that sample. Their absence cannot be interpreted as a measured loss of zero.

Time coverage differs too. Switzerland and the United States are included only for 2000-2008, while the other eight country rows extend through 2020. The nine-country European subtotal therefore combines different periods even after its geography has been clarified. We retain the euro amounts as published, without revaluing them into 2026 euros. [S01, table note, p.3](#source-S01)

The difference between €131.30 billion and €136.13 billion measures Switzerland's inclusion in this exercise. It says nothing about a recent change in fraud. Likewise, calling the €140.99 billion figure an EU loss would add two countries outside the Union while concealing the incomplete coverage of its own members.

The **€150 billion** headline belongs to a broader exercise. CORRECTIV's October 2021 publication combines cum-cum with cum-ex and related arrangements involving American Depositary Receipts, or ADRs. The consortium says it covers twelve countries. The shared “CumEx Files” label brings the investigation together; it does not make the underlying mechanisms equivalent. [S02, overview and methodology sections](#source-S02)

An international estimate needs a clear identity: countries, period, mechanism and method. An aggregate can be useful in describing the scale of a cross-border problem. Comparing two national tax systems, or assessing a European reform, requires a much tighter match between the quantities being measured.

## From tax assessments to cash collected

The first detailed administrative snapshot used here comes from the Senate report of **25 October 2022**. It records **€804 million in tax principal** and **€409 million in penalties** notified, alongside **€277 million already recovered**. The first two components add to €1.213 billion. The passage does not split the collected amount between principal and penalties. [S03, IV.A.2](#source-S03)

The recovery figure establishes a concrete outcome of enforcement at that reporting point. It does not establish the share of France's estimated 2000-2020 losses that had been repaired. Calculating that would require identifying the tax years behind the receipts, isolating principal and matching the cases to the estimate's scope.

The October 2026 response provides a more recent reference point: **€4.5 billion notified**. It leaves the exact coverage period, components and corresponding collections unspecified. Its wording on fines establishes a government statement that payments occurred, without a named schedule or itemised amounts. [S04, ministerial response](#source-S04)

<CumCumTaxLossFigure lang="en" kind="evidence" number="04" sources="S03 S04" caption="The 2022 snapshot distinguishes principal, penalties and collections; the 2026 response reports notifications. The tax periods still need to be reconciled. S03; S04.">
  <details><summary>Method and scope</summary><p>S03, Senate report dated 25 October 2022, IV.A.2: €804m in tax principal plus €409m in penalties, or €1,213m claimed; €277m collected, without a split of collections between components. Notification makes a claim visible in the enforcement record. Collection is a separate event. To follow the results over time, a reader would also need to know which amounts survived review and litigation, which were reduced or abandoned, and how much was actually paid or subsequently refunded.

The consulted publications do not provide that complete chain for French cum-cum cases. We found no public consolidated total from which to establish a **national net balance as of 27 October 2026**. This gap does not diminish the recoveries that can be documented. It prevents them from being divided by a matching national denominator.

A clearer public record could group anonymised cases by tax year and audit year. Each cohort would show principal, penalties, disputed amounts, collections and refunds. This is a proposal for statistical transparency, with implementation that would need to protect tax confidentiality. It would allow outcomes to be compared on a consistent basis instead of placing successive enforcement announcements side by side.

## Crédit Agricole CIB and HSBC: payments with distinct status

The Crédit Agricole CIB and HSBC Bank plc agreements provide documented examples of the accounting problem. The PNF's **8 September 2025** statement on Crédit Agricole CIB gives a **€88.25 million fine** and an aggregate **€134.43 million** covering tax-related amounts already paid plus the fine due. Subtraction puts the tax component at **€46.18 million**, including **€37.45 million** paid as early as 2021. [S05, single page; l0g calculation](#source-S05)

For HSBC Bank plc, the statement of **8 January 2026** distinguishes a **€267.53 million agreed fine** from **€34.46 million in tax, interest and penalties already paid**, relating to the 2019 financial year covered. The described proceedings concern transactions from 2014 to 2019 involving the Paris branch. [S06, single page](#source-S06)

The two publications report roughly **€80.65 million in tax-related payments already made**. Agreed fines are additional obligations with their own schedules. The figure’s method details the components and mixed total, whose interpretation depends on preserving those payment dates. [S05](#source-S05) [S06; l0g calculations](#source-S06)

The Crédit Agricole CIB agreement distinguishes a restitution component, based on assessed benefits within the prosecutors’ stated scope, from a punitive component. The fine therefore serves a broader purpose than returning tax to the treasury. [S07, paragraphs 45-51](#source-S07)

The agreement provided for instalments, with the last scheduled payment due on **8 July 2026**. That date had passed by our source-review cutoff. We did not obtain an itemised receipt confirming each payment. The October ministerial response refers to fines paid by several institutions, without enabling that particular schedule to be reconstructed. The missing document provides no basis for suggesting a late payment. [S07, paragraphs 57-59](#source-S07) [S04](#source-S04)

Potential overlaps create another constraint. Adding the €80.65 million to the €277 million reported in 2022 would require checking whether earlier payments were already included. Crédit Agricole CIB's 2021 payment makes the risk of double counting tangible. The public material used here does not supply the required reconciliation.

These instruments are [conventions judiciaires d’intérêt public, or CJIPs](/en/glossary/cjip/), the French public-interest judicial settlement procedure. Validation does not amount to a declaration of guilt and does not have the effect of a criminal conviction, as the Crédit Agricole CIB agreement expressly states. The Justice Ministry describes the procedure as an alternative to prosecution. [S07, paragraph 61](#source-S07) [S10](#source-S10)

## The balance still to be established

France has a large historical estimate, substantial notified claims and documented payments. Reconciling their scope remains essential to measuring recovery of the loss. [S01](#source-S01) [S03](#source-S03) [S04](#source-S04) [S05](#source-S05) [S06](#source-S06)

Measuring the budgetary burden that France still bears would require another explicit choice of definition. A balance of lost tax principal subtracts the corresponding principal recovered. A broader fiscal account can include fines and interest actually received, the cost of collection and the time elapsed. Both exercises can be useful, but their outputs require different labels.

A reform can improve future receipts without repairing every past loss. Conversely, a large collection can settle old cases while revealing little about current trading practices. Prevention and the recovery of historical losses require complementary measures of performance.

A coherent public record would let citizens distinguish the tax claimed, the penalty and the money returned to the treasury. Each figure could then mark a verifiable step towards repairing the loss.

### Method and scope

This documentary investigation uses public sources reviewed on 27 October 2026. The Mannheim tables were visually checked and their main calculations reproduced. The underlying commercial datasets, individual tax files and complete payment receipts were not obtained. No interviews or direct requests for comment were conducted. Explanations by the authors, the administration and prosecutors are drawn from, and attributed to, their publications.

Modelled amounts and payments retain their respective periods. Sensitivity scenarios are educational, not an updated estimate or a confidence interval. The figure methods provide the formulas, numerical inputs and rounding conventions. The two settlements illustrate different categories of money; this instalment does not purport to provide a comprehensive register of proceedings.
