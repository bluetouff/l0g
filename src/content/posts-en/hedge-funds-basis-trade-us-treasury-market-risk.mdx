For decades, US Treasury securities have served as the asset investors expect to sell when they need cash. That expectation remains fundamental to the global financial system. Yet the market's resilience increasingly depends on something less familiar to the public: hedge funds that buy Treasury bonds using short-term borrowing while taking offsetting positions in derivatives. Their trades help make the market work. They also create a point at which stress in funding can spill straight into the price of government debt.

A Federal Reserve study released on **22 June 2026** estimates large hedge funds’ Treasury **cash–futures basis trades at about $830 billion in September 2025**. Another **$305 billion** of long Treasury exposure is attributed to swap-spread arbitrage. The positions are reconstructed from regulatory filings. Their dates, scope and measurement method are essential to interpreting the numbers. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The risk emerges at a specific point: **the investor supporting Treasury-market liquidity needs to raise cash for itself**. The International Monetary Fund’s **October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report** supplies the framework. Funds can narrow price gaps in normal conditions; leverage and concentrated positions can amplify dislocations when intermediaries lose capacity. This conditional mechanism needs to be followed through the financing chain. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## The buyer of Treasury futures creates a buyer of Treasury bonds

Start with an asset manager that wants exposure to US interest rates. Instead of buying a portfolio of bonds outright, it may buy **Treasury futures**. A future is a contract whose value tracks specified deliverable Treasury securities. The investor can obtain sizeable duration exposure, meaning sensitivity to interest-rate changes, by posting margin rather than paying the full cash price of the underlying bonds.

Demand can push futures above their fair value relative to deliverable bonds. A hedge fund sells the future and buys a Treasury eligible for delivery, seeking the spread after funding and delivery costs. A [repurchase agreement](/en/glossary/repo/), or repo, commonly funds the purchase: the fund receives cash against securities and agrees to buy them back. The price difference represents the financing charge. A dealer often links the fund to cash lenders such as money-market funds.

That division of labour performs a valuable service. Asset managers get a liquid and capital-efficient instrument. Hedge funds arbitrage the pricing discrepancy, buying Treasury securities along the way. Dealers and cash lenders finance the bridge between the futures and bond markets. The strategy has helped absorb Treasury supply and keep futures prices aligned with cash-market prices, including through episodes of quantitative tightening. The Federal Reserve documented the contribution and its measurement difficulties in March 2024. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

<TreasuryBasisFigure lang="en" kind="mechanism" />

A narrow pricing advantage can still support an enormous trade because the cash bond is usually bought with borrowed money. Repo [haircuts](/en/glossary/decote-de-garantie/), the margin of collateral value withheld by the lender, can be very small, sometimes zero for particular arrangements. Futures also carry a margin requirement much lower than their total notional exposure. The opportunity is therefore magnified by leverage. So is the dependency on uninterrupted repo financing and the ability to meet changes in futures margin.

The two legs can generate cash demands at different times and counterparties. Hedge ratios, conversion factors and the [cheapest-to-deliver bond](/en/glossary/cheapest-to-deliver/) affect the position. The US futures regulator’s leveraged-fund short category includes other strategies. The Fed therefore treats that public proxy as capable of overstating the [basis trade](/en/glossary/basis-trade/) itself. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

## Reconstructing the $830 billion estimate

The Fed's June 2026 study goes beyond publicly available futures positions. Phillip Monin uses the monthly observations contained in quarterly **SEC Form PF** filings from large qualifying hedge funds. The method combines long Treasury securities, short Treasury derivatives and net repo borrowing at the individual fund level. After classifying likely basis traders, the estimated position is bounded by the smallest of those three exposures. The approach identifies a plausible economic structure in regulatory data. It does not reconstruct every executed transaction or reveal the positions of individual funds to the public. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

For **September 2025**, the Fed allocates about **$2.4 trillion of long Treasury exposure** among seven uses. Basis trades at **$830 billion** and swap-spread arbitrage at **$305 billion** represent slightly less than half. Other uses include curve positions and maturity-matched trades. **$245 billion** represents Treasury securities held as liquidity buffers. Rounded categories sum to **$2.395 trillion**. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

<TreasuryBasisFigure lang="en" kind="exposures" />

Concentration deserves as much attention as the headline number. By September 2025, the **top 50 funds ranked by gross Treasury exposure held approximately 90%** of the exposures in the population analysed, up from **84% at the start of 2023**. Gross Treasury exposure among the large funds reached **$4 trillion**, with $2.4 trillion long and $1.6 trillion short. Gross exposure is a notional risk measure, not an assertion that $4 trillion of bonds are held outright and financed with the funds' own capital. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Another widely quoted estimate, **$631 billion**, comes from the Bank for International Settlements in December 2025. It measures an **upper bound for the swap-spread trade**, inferred from long Treasury exposures left unmatched by reported short positions in **the second quarter of 2025**. The Fed's **$305 billion**, by contrast, is a strategy-level estimate for **September 2025** derived from a different classification method. Treating the two numbers as interchangeable would be poor analysis. Their gap is a useful warning about the information that public aggregates conceal. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The Office of Financial Research offers another reconstruction. Its **19 August 2026** analysis estimates **$2 trillion in cash Treasury holdings at year-end 2025**, roughly **7%** of the **$28.9 trillion** of marketable debt used as the denominator. It starts from Form PF exposures and subtracts futures and options positions. The Fed’s approximately **8.5%** for September 2025 uses privately held Treasuries as its denominator. The ratios retain their separate dates and methods. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## Repo financing has become part of the rates story

A more revealing transformation sits behind Treasury prices. In May 2026, economists at the Dallas Fed analysed how leveraged Treasury strategies affect the plumbing of US monetary policy. Hedge funds' **net repo borrowing**, defined as repo cash borrowed less cash lent through reverse repo, stood at roughly **$1.8 trillion at year-end 2025**. The study equates that to about **6% of the marketable Treasury notes and bonds** in its reference universe. The figure must be kept separate from the Fed's approximately **$3 trillion in gross repo cash borrowing in September 2025**, and from the $830 billion basis estimate. They refer to different measurements and dates. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

To finance these trades, dealers pull in liquidity from money-market funds and other lenders, then advance it against Treasury collateral. Intermediation consumes bank balance-sheet capacity. When multiple hedge funds want to borrow large sums against similar assets, secured funding prices can move higher relative to administered central-bank rates. The Dallas Fed estimates that the expansion of leveraged Treasury relative-value activity over the past decade is **associated with a widening of repo funding spreads of roughly 10–20 basis points**. The authors explicitly warn against interpreting those coefficients as clean causal estimates: Treasury issuance, asset managers' futures demand and reserve conditions affect both the funding market and trade incentives. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

<TreasuryBasisFigure lang="en" kind="funding" />

The change matters well beyond hedge fund performance. Money-market rates feed into financing costs across the economy. [SOFR](/en/glossary/sofr/), based on secured overnight Treasury repo transactions, offers a view of one part of the system's cost of cash. Yet an isolated rise in SOFR cannot tell an observer that basis trades are breaking down. Quarter-end balance-sheet management, shifts in bank reserves and Treasury settlement flows can influence the same rates. The mechanism has to be diagnosed across several markets at once.

The funding trade-off is subtle. Hedge funds make Treasury markets more flexible by buying securities that other investors do not immediately want to hold. In return, they often need fresh cash every day or at the next repo maturity. The US government gains a source of demand for bonds whose financing structure may become fragile precisely when buyers are scarce.

## March 2020 and April 2025 tell different stories

The market has already supplied two valuable stress tests. In **March 2020**, the pandemic triggered a dash for cash across global markets, including an extraordinary sell-off in Treasuries. Subsequent research identified the rapid unwind of basis positions as one contributor. Funds selling their cash bonds while repurchasing short futures to close trades could add to Treasury selling at a time when dealer inventories and risk tolerance were constrained. That channel was part of a wider liquidity crisis; it would be inaccurate to attribute the whole episode to hedge funds. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

In **April 2025**, tariff announcements were followed by higher long Treasury yields and reduced market depth. The Dallas Fed found basis-trade positions relatively stable, with pricing indicators more resilient than in 2020. New York Fed official Roberto Perli instead reported swap-spread unwinds that likely amplified the rise in longer-term yields. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

The Fed's June 2026 reconstruction suggests around **$60 billion of swap-spread positions were unwound in April 2025**, followed by another **$40 billion in May**. Estimated exposures subsequently recovered towards **$305 billion by September**. These figures measure inferred reductions in a strategy's exposure, not directly observed net forced sales of Treasury bonds. That qualification matters when explaining price moves: dealers' market-making conditions, investor flows and other strategies also influence yields. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

<TreasuryBasisFigure lang="en" kind="history" />

The comparison directs attention to position structure, lenders and dealer capacity. The **July 2025** Dallas Fed analysis finds that the basis trade can be more sensitive to reduced intermediation capacity than to a funding-rate increase alone. Its sensitivity exercise excludes margin risks, a central boundary when interpreting the resilience observed. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

## A liquidity call can arrive before the economic hedge pays out

Consider a hypothetical fund with **$100 million** in Treasury bonds. A repo lender applies a **1% haircut**, making **$99 million** of repo financing available against that collateral. The fund needs to supply the remaining **$1 million**. If the lender lifts the haircut to **3%**, holding the bond's value constant and changing no other conditions, available financing falls to **$97 million**. The fund must find **another $2 million** to maintain the position. The example leaves out futures margin, changes in bond prices, other portfolio cash and the fund's ability to obtain replacement financing. It is arithmetic, not a market observation or forecast.

<TreasuryBasisFigure lang="en" kind="haircut" />

Real positions can face several pressures at once. Futures clearing may demand variation margin. Bond prices can move. The relative spread being arbitraged may widen instead of converging. Repo creditors can increase haircuts or refuse to roll overnight loans. The hedge fund then has to use cash buffers, raise financing elsewhere, sell unrelated assets or reduce the trade. Across many funds, similar defensive actions can generate a loop: Treasury sales worsen market liquidity, falling collateral values constrain repo and additional liquidity calls prompt further asset sales.

The IMF’s **October 2026** report examines the asymmetry. When dealer capacity is constrained, leveraged positioning is associated with greater pricing dislocations. One estimate associates a **one-standard-deviation increase in positioning** with a **1.5-basis-point increase at the 90th percentile** of the cash–futures basis over **eight weeks**, leaving the median unchanged. This describes a conditional distribution and depends on intermediation capacity. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

That distinction also explains the risk of relying exclusively on a fund's risk model. A portfolio can appear well hedged for ordinary interest-rate changes. A combined shock to funding access, collateral values and dealer balance sheets creates a different form of exposure: one in which the investor must survive the cash demands long enough for the relative-value thesis to work.

## The bank is still in the middle

Hedge funds are commonly described as part of the nonbank financial system. Their financing nevertheless runs through major banks and securities dealers. These intermediaries provide repo lending, execute derivatives, manage collateral and offer prime-brokerage services. If a large hedge fund loses access to cash, the immediate consequences may be felt by the brokers financing it. They can respond by changing terms for other customers, amplifying the shock across portfolios that initially appeared unrelated.

The IMF estimates about **$13 trillion of global hedge-fund gross assets in early 2026**, against **$4 trillion in 2013**, and **more than $40 trillion of gross notional exposure**, including derivatives. Those measures overlap. A separate measure describes prime-broker concentration: in **December 2025**, the five leading providers served funds representing roughly **two-thirds of covered assets under management**. Assets of funds with several brokers are divided equally among those providers for the calculation. This is a client-asset measure, distinct from a share of lending. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The balance-sheet constraints facing dealers create a policy dilemma. Tight restrictions may constrain intermediation when investors most need it, while very loose financing terms can encourage ever-larger positions relying on uninterrupted short-term credit. The answer involves the design of margin and collateral requirements, the transparency of exposures and banks' ability to intermediate stress without jeopardising their own liquidity.

Cross-border structures further complicate monitoring. Funds may be domiciled in the Cayman Islands, Ireland or Luxembourg while their trading desks, investors and lenders operate in other jurisdictions. Authorities can see part of the exposure through national data collections without necessarily having a consolidated, timely view of all the links. The IMF explicitly identifies these gaps as a barrier to assessing systemic risk. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Central clearing reshapes the funding chain

The SEC’s schedule sets **31 December 2026** as the compliance date for eligible cash Treasury transactions and **30 June 2027** for eligible repo transactions. Scope depends on defined transactions and participants. A central counterparty stands between the parties and can net offsetting transactions, potentially reducing bilateral exposures and dealer balance-sheet needs. It brings its own margin and operational requirements. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

On **30 October 2026**, the SEC granted conditional exemptions for certain inter-affiliate repos and transactions involving qualifying non-US counterparties. Limits and attestations constrain that relief. The reform’s coverage must therefore be read against the updated framework. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

In **January 2026**, the OFR simulated the framework it was studying on **January–August 2025** repos, holding transactions and behaviour fixed. **45%** of average daily outstanding was already centrally cleared; coverage would have reached **77%** in that exercise. Exceptions left **23%** outside scope. This is a historical counterfactual under a framework preceding the October relief. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

<TreasuryBasisFigure lang="en" kind="clearing" />

An OFR brief published in **March 2026** cites an estimate that about **75% of hedge-fund Treasury repo activity was outside central clearing**. Its population and reference period differ from the whole-market 45% figure. The sponsored-repo analysis covers, among other evidence, **October 2019–October 2024** and describes dealers’ incentives to clear repos when managing balance-sheet constraints. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup>

A further SEC order dated **4 November 2026** conditionally facilitates the customer-reserve treatment of Treasury margin delivered by a broker on a net omnibus basis for multiple customers. It affects resources the intermediary must mobilise. Outcomes for individual funds depend on contracts, margin requirements and implementation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

Central clearing also concentrates default management and can generate substantial margin-liquidity demands in volatile markets. Dealer balance-sheet gains and funds’ cash needs must be assessed together. The Financial Stability Board’s **9 July 2025** recommendations call for stronger leverage data and oversight, building also on its work on collateral-call preparedness. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

## The Federal Reserve has a circuit breaker, with important limits

The Fed's **Standing Repo Facility** is designed to support monetary policy implementation and orderly market functioning by providing collateralised funding to eligible counterparties. Those counterparties include primary dealers and qualifying depository institutions. **A hedge fund does not receive a direct facility line simply because it holds Treasury collateral.** A fund may benefit indirectly if the dealer that finances it has more reliable access to liquidity, but that transmission depends on credit decisions, collateral terms and the type of disruption. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

A liquidity facility can help market functioning and absorb a cash-flow shock. Fund solvency, the profitability of an arbitrage and investor withdrawals remain separate issues. Transmission runs through eligible intermediaries, their collateral and their willingness to pass funding onwards.

The IMF's case for credible backstops is consequently compatible with its recommendation for tighter monitoring and better calibrated leverage controls. Liquidity support and sound collateral discipline tackle different sides of the problem. The first concerns the functioning of core markets under stress. The second concerns how much stress market participants can create before an emergency begins. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Refinancing conditions determine the stress test

The evidence supports a structural vulnerability. Hedge funds have become material Treasury buyers. The basis trade embeds dependence on short-term borrowing and futures margin. Dealers connect the funds to cash lenders while operating under capital and liquidity constraints. Concentration means a relatively small number of large participants can matter at market scale. The Fed, OFR, BIS and IMF reach related conclusions using distinct but partly overlapping data. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

The adverse scenario requires several conditions to reinforce each other: a major Treasury rate shock, disrupted repo access, rising collateral or derivatives margin calls and insufficient dealer capacity to absorb selling. In that setting, forced deleveraging could deepen price swings and spill into other funding markets. The mechanism has empirical and historical support. **Its probability over any particular horizon in November 2026 cannot be estimated credibly from the public evidence reviewed here.**

The resilience of basis positions in **April 2025** supplies a useful counterweight. The Federal Reserve’s **May 2026 Financial Stability Report** described sound banks and robust dealer intermediation on the information then available. In October, the IMF noted a more recent moderation in its indirect basis-trade footprint indicator. Those observations help calibrate monitoring. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

A more useful risk dashboard would put the **leveraged-fund Treasury futures short**, **net repo borrowing**, **delivery-option-adjusted cash–futures basis**, **repo haircuts and financing spreads**, and **cash Treasury market depth** side by side. Persistent joint deterioration would strengthen the forced-unwind hypothesis. Stable positioning, accessible repo finance and resilient market depth would weaken it. Public data cannot presently connect all those variables at high frequency for every large fund and counterparty.

For the Treasury, leveraged buyers make it easier to distribute an enormous volume of debt. For the financial system, those buyers introduce funding requirements that may have to be renewed just when the appetite to lend weakens. That is the fault line to watch: **who has the balance-sheet capacity and spare cash to hold the bonds after the investors financing them decide to step back?**

## Method and limitations

Documentary review closed on **5 November 2026**. Position sizes are institutional reconstructions with specified periods and definitions. The investigation compares Fed, BIS, OFR and IMF research and updates the legal framework using the SEC orders of 30 October and 4 November. Several institutions reuse Form PF, so their publications are not independent datasets. Fund and counterparty information remains lagged and incomplete.

The detailed Fed decomposition primarily covers September 2025. End-2025 measures from the OFR and Dallas use other reconstructions and denominators. Econometric associations retain their conditions and causal limitations. These amounts do not provide a crisis probability on 5 November or losses for a named bank. No real-time market quotation or yield forecast is used.

The haircut example assumes securities worth $100 million at a fixed price: (1 − 1%) × 100 = $99 million of financing, then (1 − 3%) × 100 = $97 million, requiring $2 million of additional cash. It excludes futures margins, price movements and the fund’s own buffers. The opening artwork is conceptual; figures distinguish estimates, mechanisms and illustrative assumptions.
