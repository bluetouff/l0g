The ECB could cut the Eurosystem’s interest bill to banks by **€4.4 billion a year**, at a fixed rate and reserve base. Raising the [minimum reserve ratio](/en/glossary/reserves-obligatoires/) from 1% to 2% would leave more of the money banks hold at their central banks earning zero. The initial loss of income can be calculated. Who ultimately bears it depends on how banks change their prices and how customers respond. [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09) · [S06](#source-S06) · [S03](#source-S03)

On 18 November, Reuters reported, citing four sources, that an increase in the minimum reserve ratio from 1% to 2% would be discussed on 25 November. As of 19 November 2026, the official rules reviewed for this article still specify 1%. The ECB calendar lists a non-monetary-policy meeting on the 25th without a detailed agenda. Reuters says a decision could come on 17 December. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02) · [S26](#source-S26)

To understand the idea, start with the bond purchases made during the years of low interest rates. They left central banks holding long-dated securities and commercial banks holding central bank money. The interest earned and paid on those two sides of the balance sheet now moves at different speeds. [S12](#source-S12) · [S14](#source-S14)

## The legacy of bond purchases

When a central bank buys a bond from a bank, it pays by crediting that bank’s account. The commercial bank swaps a security for reserves: balances in central bank money, used among other things to settle payments between banks. The Eurosystem records a bond as an asset and reserves as a liability. A purchase from a non-bank investor is settled through the investor’s bank and also creates a deposit for the investor. [S12](#source-S12) · [S13](#source-S13)

Those reserves can circulate. When a customer pays a supplier who uses another bank, a deposit moves across banks and reserves are transferred to settle the payment. This changes the distribution of reserves across the banking system. Their aggregate volume depends on central bank operations and other flows, including demand for banknotes and changes in government deposits. [S12](#source-S12) · [S07](#source-S07)

Asset purchases therefore created a lasting financial relationship between banks and the Eurosystem. If the bonds earn little while interest on reserves rises, central bank earnings come under pressure. A fixed-rate bond pays its contractual coupon. The cost of reserves can change with each rate decision. [S14](#source-S14) · [S16](#source-S16)

This exposure has already shown up in the accounts. The ECB itself, a legal entity separate from the national central banks, reported a €1.254 billion loss in 2025, compared with €7.944 billion in 2024. Lower net interest expense explains much of the improvement. Both figures are historical annual results for that particular institution. The proposed saving on bank reserves concerns the wider central banking system of the euro area. [S15](#source-S15)

## A requirement linked to banks’ funding

The minimum is calculated from particular bank liabilities, including deposits and short-term debt securities that fall within the reserve base. Some liabilities are excluded, notably those to other institutions subject to the requirement and not exempt from it. Others carry a zero ratio, including repos, deposits with maturities or notice periods exceeding two years, and debt securities with original maturities exceeding two years. The 1% ratio applies to this defined regulatory base. [S03](#source-S03)

A bank then deducts a €100,000 allowance from its requirement, subject to special rules for groups. Compliance is assessed using average holdings over a maintenance period. Daily balances can fluctuate as payments arrive and leave: higher holdings on one day can offset lower holdings on another. This flexibility helps banks manage money market liquidity. [S03](#source-S03) · [S26](#source-S26)

Required reserves have earned zero since 20 September 2023. Other holdings are treated differently. For eligible counterparties, excess balances in reserve accounts and funds placed in the deposit facility earn the deposit facility rate. That rate has been 2.50% since 16 September 2026. The ECB left it unchanged on 29 October. [S04](#source-S04) · [S27](#source-S27) · [S06](#source-S06) · [S28](#source-S28)

A recent rule change matters here. The alignment of remuneration on excess reserve-account balances took effect on 17 June 2026. Some older explanatory pages still describe the previous arrangements. Under the current rules, moving funds between the reserve account and the deposit facility preserves their remuneration, provided the bank meets the access conditions and the funds remain above its reserve requirement. [S05](#source-S05) · [S27](#source-S27)

A move to 2% would shift that dividing line. Some balances that currently earn interest would become required reserves earning zero. The central bank’s saving would arise at the same point as the commercial bank’s loss of interest income. [S03](#source-S03) · [S04](#source-S04) · [S27](#source-S27)

## Reconstructing the €4.4 billion

The latest daily snapshot available during the research is for 18 November. The ECB reports €177.314 billion of required reserves for the maintenance period running from 4 November to 22 December, €334.644 billion in current accounts and €1,929.436 billion in the deposit facility. Those last two items add up to €2,264.080 billion of banks’ balances with the Eurosystem. [S07](#source-S07)

To calculate the additional requirement, the most useful starting point is the reserve base itself. The ECB assigns September 2026 liability data to this maintenance period: about €17,762.1 billion of deposits and debt securities subject to the positive ratio. One percentage point of that base is approximately **€177.6 billion**. At 2.50%, the simple annualised interest amounts to **€4.44 billion**, assuming the balances move from the deposit rate to zero and both the base and the rate remain constant. [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09) · [S06](#source-S06)

<EcbReservesFigure lang="en" kind="reclassification" number={1} sourceHref="#source-S07" secondarySourceHref="#source-S08" />

A small calculation trap needs attention. Doubling the current net requirement overlooks the €100,000 allowance, which would be deducted once under each ratio. The additional amount depends on the gross base. Very small institutions require separate treatment because of the allowance thresholds and aggregation rules. Our estimate retains that qualification, along with the provisional status of the published requirement for the current period. [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09) · [S10](#source-S10) · [S03](#source-S03)

With balance sheets otherwise unchanged, total holdings at the Eurosystem would remain the same. A bank could move some money from the deposit facility into its reserve account to meet the higher requirement. The ECB’s measure of [excess liquidity](/en/glossary/liquidite-excedentaire/) subtracts the required amount and use of the marginal lending facility from current-account and deposit-facility balances. If the minimum rises, the published figure can fall while those holdings stay the same. [S07](#source-S07) · [S25](#source-S25) · [S03](#source-S03)

Annualisation also needs a definition. The €4.44 billion figure is simply the balance multiplied by the annual interest rate. The deposit facility uses an actual/360 day-count convention: for an unchanged placement lasting 365 days, the corresponding amount would be about €4.50 billion. The eventual implementation date, interest rate and reserve base would determine the income actually forgone. [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09) · [S11](#source-S11)

Holding the base constant, simple annualisation produces a saving of about €3.55 billion at a 2% rate and €5.33 billion at 3%. These are sensitivity scenarios. The chart uses the same simple convention throughout to isolate the effect of the rate. [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09) · [S06](#source-S06)

## How the cost could reach customers

Consider a hypothetical bank with €10 billion of liabilities subject to the positive reserve ratio. After the allowance, its current requirement would be €99.9 million. At 2%, that would rise to €199.9 million. Funding the additional €100 million from balances earning 2.50% would cost €2.5 million in interest income on a simple annualised basis. [S03](#source-S03) · [S06](#source-S06)

Relative to the €10 billion base, the charge is 0.025%, or **2.5 basis points**, on the same simple annualised basis. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. This gives a sense of scale before additional funding costs, tax and commercial responses. A bank facing deposit outflows or thin margins could experience the same charge more acutely. [S03](#source-S03) · [S06](#source-S06) · [S23](#source-S23)

The first effect would be lower interest income. Management could absorb the cost in its margin, offer less attractive deposit rates or seek higher returns on new loans. Fees and changes to the funding mix offer other possible adjustments. The response would depend on competition, existing contracts, customers’ alternatives and the profitability of each business. Deposit rates and the pricing of new loans would show how much of the cost banks pass on. [S20](#source-S20) · [S21](#source-S21) · [S23](#source-S23)

Empirical research nevertheless gives a reason to watch lending. A Bundesbank working paper published in 2024 examines the tightening cycle that began in 2022. Credit supply at banks with greater excess reserve holdings was less sensitive to monetary tightening. Reserve income can therefore matter for monetary transmission. The paper studies a different policy experience. It provides a mechanism to investigate, with no coefficient directly applicable to the November 2026 proposal. [S20](#source-S20)

A study published in 2025 examines the two-tier remuneration system introduced in 2019. It finds that reallocating reserves towards banks with high liquidity needs supported lending. Distribution matters alongside the aggregate quantity: which banks hold the available balances, and which would need to obtain more? [S21](#source-S21)

This distinction is essential when reading the €2,086.727 billion of excess liquidity reported for 18 November. The aggregate looks ample relative to an additional requirement of about €177.6 billion. Individual banks still have to manage their own payments, market access and available collateral. Making a loan creates a claim on the borrower and a deposit. The bank must then be able to settle the resulting payments. Funding conditions and risk constraints connect reserves to lending decisions. [S07](#source-S07) · [S23](#source-S23) · [S13](#source-S13)

## The route to government budgets

On the public side, each euro of interest saved first improves a central bank’s financial result. Reaching a government budget takes further steps. Part of the national central banks’ monetary income and expenses is pooled and redistributed under Eurosystem rules. The ECB keeps separate accounts and does not participate in that pool. Where banks hold their reserves is only one part of the fiscal picture. [S18](#source-S18) · [S19](#source-S19)

Accumulated losses also matter. The Bundesbank lost €8.6 billion in 2025, taking its accumulated loss to €27.8 billion. It says future profits will be used to absorb those losses and rebuild provisions, delaying the resumption of distributions to the federal budget. Lower interest expense would help repair the balance sheet, all else equal. The saving and a government’s cash dividend in the same year would still be different accounting outcomes. [S16](#source-S16)

The French accounts show a different path. The Banque de France reported an €8.1 billion profit for 2025, allocated to clearing the €7.7 billion loss carried over from 2024 and adding €0.4 billion to a specific reserve. An exceptional €11 billion foreign exchange gain made a substantial contribution to the result. Those accounts show how strongly returns can depend on the assets a central bank holds. [S17](#source-S17)

A central bank’s public purpose remains the fulfilment of its mandate. BIS researchers point out that losses, and even negative equity, can coexist with effective monetary policy. Financial results have fiscal and political consequences. Taken alone, they provide little evidence of success in controlling inflation. [S14](#source-S14)

## The money market test

The strongest argument for raising the requirement concerns the cost of implementing monetary policy. When the ECB set required-reserve remuneration to zero in 2023, it argued that it could reduce interest expense while retaining control over money market rates. A larger required portion could pursue that logic while reserves remain abundant. The outcome would depend on liquidity conditions and banks’ behaviour. [S04](#source-S04)

The main risk emerges as that abundance recedes. The APP and PEPP bond portfolios are shrinking as maturing principal is no longer reinvested. A higher requirement would simultaneously increase demand for reserves. The operational framework provides refinancing against eligible collateral. Assessing the full cost of the change would therefore require monitoring the price and distribution of funding, alongside money market rates. [S06](#source-S06) · [S22](#source-S22) · [S23](#source-S23)

An ECB analysis published on 2 April 2026 found that reserves were being redistributed smoothly across banks and euro area countries, even though some banks were closer to their preferred level of holdings than others. If the requirement is raised, changes in liquidity trading and refinancing use will show how banks adapt beyond the initial loss of interest income. [S24](#source-S24)

Calling the measure a “tax” captures its initial economic burden: a compulsory holding with a zero return entails forgone income. Its legal form is a reserve requirement serving monetary policy objectives. Judgement would turn on the ECB’s rationale, the chosen calibration and the effects on transmission. Treating the entire €4.4 billion as a charge borne by bank shareholders would go beyond the evidence. [S03](#source-S03) · [S04](#source-S04) · [S20](#source-S20)

If the Council acts, the ratio, start date and maintenance rules will let us calculate the interest at stake again. Bank earnings, refinancing demand and changes in customer rates will show where the cost actually falls.
