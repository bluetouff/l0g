On Friday, November 13, 2026, New Zealand property lawyers ran into a practical obstacle. To complete a sale, one firm needed to give another an undertaking and receive instructions from the bank about releasing funds. Outlook was down. The New Zealand Law Society’s Property Law Section advised solicitors to phone each other, agree on a different channel and confirm receipt. The email mattered because it let someone complete the next step of the sale. [S01](#source-S01)

The cost depends on what happens next. If the sale closes that afternoon, there may be extra work, fees and a few anxious hours for two families. A missed deadline or a sale that falls through has different consequences. The Law Society documented the obstacle. Its notice leaves open how many sales were saved and what the interruption eventually cost. [S01](#source-S01)

Three days later, Rebecca Paul, a member of Britain’s House of Commons, asked what would have happened if the outage had lasted a week. Microsoft’s report describes the first day. Notices to customers show which tasks had to wait or move to another channel. To price seven days for a business, we need assumptions, starting with how much work people can recover once the service returns. [S02](#source-S02) [S03](#source-S03) [S07](#source-S07)

## Tracking the November outage

The trouble began on Thursday, November 12 at 16:20 UTC, or 5:20 p.m. in Paris. Microsoft’s final report, dated November 20, traces it to network gateways serving Exchange Online, its hosted email service. Maintenance had taken capacity offline. Teams traffic had shifted onto those gateways fifteen hours earlier. As North American demand approached its peak, the combined load exposed a software defect specific to that configuration. Some machines stopped responding. A Teams user could find it difficult to schedule a meeting even as colleagues were generally able to join an existing call. [S02](#source-S02) [S03](#source-S03)

European gateways returned to a healthy level at **11:35 UTC** on Friday. Microsoft put the end of active impact at **16:10 UTC**, **23 hours and 50 minutes** after the start. Monitoring and backlog recovery continued until **04:15 UTC** on Saturday. Microsoft says it delivered all queued enterprise messages. A message that arrives after a deadline can still have missed its purpose. On November 24, Reuters reported that Microsoft had prioritised the fixes under an internal programme called “Code Red”. ## Two ways an invoice can get stuck

At Cecurity, email carried the code some users needed to sign in. The operator recorded disruption from 21:30 UTC on November 12 to 12:40 the next day. At Medius, email carried the invoice itself. At 19:10 UTC on Friday, the company said processing had stabilised in Europe and the US while a backlog was still clearing in Europe. It closed the incident at 20:12. A supplier awaiting payment still needs the customer to receive the invoice, approve it and instruct the bank. [S09](#source-S09) [S10](#source-S10)

That Friday the Derivatives Service Bureau, which assigns identifiers to derivatives, reported that its identifier services were operating. Yet emailed codes needed to access the user interface could arrive late. At Eurex the consequence had a date attached. At 13:43 Central European Time the exchange said it was moving three procedures for handling late securities deliveries to Monday, November 16. A market process had changed its timetable because of a problem with correspondence. [S11](#source-S11) [S12](#source-S12)

The university hospital in Tours, France, asked patients to phone about matters requiring a prompt response and to wait before emailing about non-urgent appointments. In Britain, NHS Supply Chain directed suppliers to a portal to collect purchase orders. Its warehouses and transport kept working. By Monday, the organisation expected deliveries to continue as planned. A ministerial correction on November 17 confirmed that two NHS trusts had declared critical incidents and stood them down relatively quickly. ## A seven-day scenario that includes sign-in

Our seven-day exercise has a defined boundary. Suppose that from Monday through Sunday a company loses hosted email and the Microsoft 365 functions on which it relies. Suppose also that Microsoft Entra ID, the service that applications use to verify identities, is severely disrupted. Local Windows PCs stay on. We assume telecoms networks, Azure virtual machines and Azure storage continue operating, even though some users may lose access to their applications. Adding a failure of Azure compute or storage would require identifying further affected processes, then revising the exposed sales share and fallback capacity. Adding identity to the exercise is an l0g assumption. November’s observed failure concerned the gateways used by Exchange and the functions that depended on them. [S02](#source-S02) [S13](#source-S13) [S14](#source-S14)

An application may already hold an access token, temporary permission to use a specific resource. Microsoft gives an ordinary lifetime of **60 to 90 minutes** from when the token was issued, with exceptions. Two tokens each issued for **75 minutes** can expire at different times in an outage. One issued **65 minutes** earlier has **ten minutes** left. One issued **ten minutes** earlier has **65**. The target service must remain available and accept the token. Microsoft’s backup authentication can take over in certain configurations, and one of its conditions is a successful sign-in to the same app on the same device within **three days**. [S13](#source-S13) [S14](#source-S14)

This matters when costing a [service continuity](/en/glossary/continuite-de-service/) plan. A second application running on another cloud may ask for the very same Microsoft identity as soon as someone opens it. A portal already in use, an authorized telephone procedure or an independent account tested in a drill can produce quite different results. One revealing exercise is to give the duty team a low-value order and follow it all the way through to receipt by the customer. In the European financial sector, [DORA](/en/glossary/dora/) also requires continuity testing and exit plans for ICT services supporting critical or important functions. ## Delta counts the cost of cancelled flights

On **July 19, 2024**, a faulty CrowdStrike update reached Windows systems. CrowdStrike withdrew it after **78 minutes** of distribution. Delta Air Lines cancelled **about 7,000 flights over five days**. The technical cause differed from the Exchange disruption. The example shows how long operations can remain disrupted after a faulty update has been withdrawn. [S15](#source-S15) [S16](#source-S16) [S17](#source-S17)

Delta estimated a **$380 million reduction in September-quarter revenue**, driven mainly by refunds and compensation, and **$170 million in additional non-fuel operating expenses**. On the other side of the ledger, the airline saved **$50 million on fuel** as flights were cancelled. The estimated operating profit hit comes to roughly **$500 million**. The numbers belong to one airline and one event. Other businesses can use the logic: identify the sales actually lost, deduct the costs avoided and add the expense of recovery. [S16](#source-S16) [S17](#source-S17)

<MicrosoftWeekFigure lang="en" kind="delta" />

US domestic flight records provide an independent measure of the recovery. On Monday, July 22, Delta cancelled 1,209 of its own 3,161 scheduled domestic flights, or 38.2%. United’s rate that day was 2.6%. By Thursday, July 25, Delta cancelled just one of 3,162. These figures cover cancellations from all causes. International flights and regional partners flying under their own operating codes fall outside the series. The comparison shows how differently the recovery unfolded at two airlines exposed to the same disruption. [S17](#source-S17) [S26](#source-S26)

## A business with a million euros of weekly sales

Consider a hypothetical company expecting **€1 million in sales, excluding VAT**, over the seven days. Half goes through the process exposed to the outage. A fallback handles 40% of that €500,000 while the service is down, leaving €300,000 of orders waiting when it returns. Suppose staff recover three quarters in the days that follow. By the end of recovery, €225,000 of sales have been delayed and €75,000 have been lost for good. Each percentage is an input the reader can change.

On every euro of sales lost, the company would have kept 35 cents after variable costs. The €75,000 reduction in sales therefore costs **€26,250 in contribution margin**. Add €40,000 of exceptional expense to operate the fallback and clear the backlog. The operating cost of the incident comes to **€66,250**, before insurance, service credits, financing and tax. Regular pay already appears in the accounts for a normal week. Adding it again would overstate the loss.

The result turns on the patience of customers and spare capacity when people return the following Monday. Keep every other assumption and recover just 25% of the backlog, and the cost rises to **€118,750**. Recover 90% and it falls to **€50,500**. Cash follows its own timetable. A company paid on delivery might have bills to meet while €225,000 in recoverable sales are waiting their turn. Measuring that funding need requires dates for receipts and payments. ## Insurers are pricing a different loss

On November 13, while recovery was still under way, Shalom Bublil, co-founder of risk modeler Kovrr, told Reuters that **lost productivity could amount to about $16 billion**, with roughly **$700 million in insured losses**. He described the calculation as a rough estimate. It assumed resolution by 19:00 UTC that Friday. Microsoft’s final report would put the end of active impact at 16:10 UTC. Kovrr was assigning a value to interrupted work. As of November 25 we found no worldwide accounting of actual lost sales and additional expenses reported by affected businesses. [S02](#source-S02) [S24](#source-S24) [S25](#source-S25)

Even for insured losses alone, estimates differ. On November 16, CyberCube said the global total would most likely be **under $100 million**, with a conservative upper bound of **$350 million**. Morningstar DBRS’s November 17 note put the figure at **$300 million to $500 million**. A policy can cover interruption at a supplier, subject to a deductible, waiting period and exclusions. A service credit from Microsoft follows another rule: it is usually calculated against the fee for the affected service. None of these amounts tells us the cost of a postponed consultation in Tours. ## The limits of a worldwide bill

In 2018 Lloyd’s and AIR Worldwide modeled the failure of a cloud provider in the United States. Table 9 covers a **5.5- to 11-day** disruption. For two anonymous providers, numbered 1 and 3, the central estimates for losses before insurance were **$23.80 billion and $11.16 billion**, respectively. The study used the customer base and assumptions of its time, factoring in industry, fallbacks and the pace of recovery. The figures are for separate providers and a range of durations. The table supplies no estimate specific to day seven or to Microsoft. [S20](#source-S20)

The economics depend on what happens to customers’ work. A Medius invoice can still be paid on time. A call can help a New Zealand law firm complete a sale. A seat on a cancelled Delta flight can no longer be sold for that departure. A business can tally the orders it processed during the outage, the ones it recovered later and the ones it lost, then add the expense of keeping the business running and clearing the backlog. Across an economy, delays and losses are harder to disentangle. [S01](#source-S01) [S10](#source-S10) [S16](#source-S16) [S17](#source-S17) [S20](#source-S20)
