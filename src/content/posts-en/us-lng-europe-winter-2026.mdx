Europe needs American gas to help heat homes this winter. It also needs the buyers of flexible cargoes to choose its ports over competing destinations in Asia. That contest matters more when reserves are thin. The Gas Infrastructure Europe dashboard displayed on **4 November 2026** shows **841.63 terawatt-hours** in EU storage, or **74.33%** of working capacity. Germany stands at **60.81%**, Italy at **90.73%**. Each percentage measures how full that country’s storage facilities are; its supply cushion also depends on demand and incoming deliveries. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

US exports of **liquefied natural gas (LNG)** have been growing. Chilling gas into liquid allows it to travel by ship, and the American route to Europe avoids the Strait of Hormuz. For cargoes with flexible destinations, buyers compare the contract, the voyage and the selling price at each port. With European reserves depleted and Gulf trade disrupted, that commercial choice becomes part of the continent’s energy security. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The International Energy Agency’s (IEA) **Global Gas Security Review 2026**, published on **3 November**, describes the EU’s lowest opening heating-season stocks since 2013. Its stress case combines colder-than-average weather with prolonged disruption through Hormuz: storage could finish March 2027 **below 10%**. That outcome remains conditional. For buyers, the more immediate pressure comes from the price needed to secure deliveries. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## A smaller cushion before the cold arrives

The problem was inherited from last winter. EU underground storage finished winter 2025-26 at **28%**, compared with **34%** one year earlier and a nine-year average of roughly **40%**, according to ACER, the European energy regulator. A deeper winter drawdown creates a larger refill requirement. Summer 2026 then brought higher LNG prices and a constrained global market. The margin available from buying in summer and selling in winter was not consistently attractive enough to encourage the usual commercial pace of injections. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

Valuing each day’s injected volumes at that day’s Dutch wholesale gas price (TTF), the Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators, **ACER**, estimates the **summer 2026 refill cost at around €27 billion**. This indicative upper estimate may differ from operators’ actual costs, depending on their contracts. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The European Network of Transmission System Operators for Gas, **ENTSOG**, recorded **72% on 1 October**, and ACER cited **75% on 21 October**. GIE’s dashboard displayed **74.33% on 4 November**. These dated observations describe a relatively thin reserve at the start of the heating season. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

<USLNGWinterFigure lang="en" kind="stocks" />

Storage is the seasonal battery of the gas system. It shifts supply bought during relatively quiet months into periods when households need heat and electricity demand can surge. The European Commission says underground storage typically supplies **25-30% of winter gas consumption**; ENTSOG estimates the share was about **26% in winter 2025-26**. Imports through pipelines and LNG terminals, together with domestic production, provide the rest. As inventories fall, the system needs either more steady incoming gas or lower consumption. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

A low reserve also becomes harder to deliver quickly. The IEA notes that [**withdrawal capacity**](/en/glossary/capacite-de-soutirage/) tends to decline **below about 30%** of working storage capacity. Gas still underground may then reach customers more slowly just as a cold snap increases daily needs. Volume and delivery speed must be assessed together. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## American supply cushions the Hormuz shock

Repeated interruptions to traffic through Hormuz have removed a large amount of LNG from world trade since the war began in late February 2026. According to the IEA, loadings from **Qatar and the United Arab Emirates fell by more than 60 billion cubic metres** between **March and October**, compared with the same months in 2025. Producers outside the Gulf expanded output by **more than 40 billion cubic metres**. Overall global production was still down by roughly **20 billion cubic metres**, or about **6%**, across that eight-month period. The first two figures are rounded bounds. The worldwide net loss is a separate IEA estimate; the three measures should be read independently. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

<USLNGWinterFigure lang="en" kind="supply" />

The extra supply has come from new and expanded liquefaction facilities in North America and Africa, alongside greater output from several established producers. But a new gas terminal cannot be summoned into operation because a spot price has jumped. Plants have operating limits, maintenance schedules and feedgas requirements. Vessels and berthing capacity matter too. Projects that will arrive later in the decade offer little immediate help to a January 2027 buyer. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The **Energy Information Administration (EIA)**, the US government’s energy statistics agency, reported average LNG exports of **17.4 billion cubic feet per day (Bcf/d)** in the **first half of 2026**, **23% above** a year earlier. Its **6 October** outlook, completed on **1 October**, estimated **17.6 Bcf/d for 2026**, compared with **15.1 in 2025**, and projected **18.6 in 2027**. Both **2026 and 2027 are forecasts**. The daily measure remains separate from the eight-month global losses expressed in billion cubic metres. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

<USLNGWinterFigure lang="en" kind="exports" />

The IEA expects global LNG supply to **fall by 3% over 2026**, assuming Hormuz flows remain largely disrupted through year-end. That annual projection and the **6% decline observed from March to October** cover different time windows. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Europe and Asia compete for flexible cargoes

Europe’s gas benchmark is the Dutch [**TTF**](/en/glossary/ttf/) trading hub. Asian buyers follow delivered LNG cargo prices, particularly in Northeast Asia. A seller with destination flexibility compares the revenue left after shipping and other delivery costs, known as the *netback*. A longer voyage can absorb the premium offered by an Asian buyer. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

The IEA reports an average European TTF spot price of **almost $20 per million British thermal units (MMBtu)** between March and October 2026. Asian spot LNG averaged **$21.5/MMBtu** over the same months. Those prices were approximately **70%** and **80%** higher than a year earlier, respectively. Asia's spot LNG premium over European hub prices was around **$2/MMBtu from March to July**, narrowing to roughly **$1/MMBtu from August to October**. Those are broad market signals, not the landed selling prices, freight bills or realised profits of any individual vessel. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

<USLNGWinterFigure lang="en" kind="arbitrage" />

The scale of that decision has changed dramatically. The IEA calculates that contracts allowing **destination flexibility** accounted for **29% of LNG contracting in 2016** and **more than 50% in 2025**, largely reflecting the rise of American supply. Flexible cargoes make the global system better able to redirect gas when an export hub fails. They also expose buyers to the price required to attract those cargoes. The IEA says Asian imports of Atlantic Basin LNG, primarily from the United States, **doubled year on year between March and October 2026**. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Flexible supply helped Europe absorb the Gulf shock. It also exposes European buyers to competing bids: if temperatures fall in both Europe and Asia, the two regions can bid up the same cargo. The IEA forecasts a **3% decline in European gas demand in 2026**, partly reflecting high prices and reduced industrial consumption. Factories using less gas may relieve the physical balance while sacrificing production and margins. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Contracts distribute the gains from a cargo

The gap between American gas prices and European selling prices raises an obvious question about profits. Contracts determine how they are shared. In its **2025 annual report**, **Cheniere** describes agreements that generally charge a **fixed liquefaction fee per MMBtu**, plus around **115% of the Henry Hub price**, the US gas benchmark. Some cancellations leave the fixed fee payable. Under [**free-on-board delivery (FOB)**](/en/glossary/fob/), the buyer takes the LNG at the export terminal and arranges its onward voyage. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

A cargo distributes income and risk between the liquefaction operator, contract holder, charterer, trader and final importer. A buyer that secured gas in advance may benefit from a higher European selling price. Freight may also become dearer, or the purchase contract may track prices at the destination. Cheniere’s disclosures document one particular structure. Establishing each participant’s final profit requires its contracts, hedges and actual deliveries. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

ACER estimates an **indicative €18–33 billion extra cost** on summer 2026 gas imports, holding volumes constant. Both calculations compare observed prices with estimated prices in the absence of the crisis. The lower estimate uses second- and third-quarter day-ahead TTF prices and a macroeconomic model; the upper estimate compares monthly 2026 prices with a path based on 2025. ACER describes the range as an indicative upper-bound estimate: indexation, hedging and timing can reduce or delay the cost actually borne. It measures European exposure to the price shock, while the profits of individual counterparties remain a separate question. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The shock reaches the economy at different speeds. Gas-fired generators pay for fuel; fertiliser producers face changing production costs; utilities’ hedges and contracts influence when consumers see higher tariffs. ACER reports roughly **9% more gas-fired power generation during gas summer 2026** than a year earlier, as heatwaves and constraints on other generators increased demand. The eventual household bill depends on national tariffs, regulation and supply contracts. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

## A growing reliance on American LNG

ACER records **146 billion cubic metres of EU LNG imports in 2025**, **58%** of them supplied by the United States. That American volume was equivalent to about **one-quarter of total EU gas demand**. These are different denominators: 58% refers to LNG imports, while a quarter refers to overall gas consumption. Europe still uses pipeline gas, notably from Norway and Algeria, as well as domestic supply. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

Eurostat’s trade statistics provide another view. In **Q2 2026**, the United States supplied **63.2% of the value of EU LNG imports**, Russia **17.3%**, and Algeria **8.1%**. The remaining **11.4%** is calculated from the rounded published shares. These are **value shares** in customs statistics. ACER’s **58%** measures **physical volumes during 2025**. Both identify the scale of the US role, using distinct periods and denominators. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

<USLNGWinterFigure lang="en" kind="origins" />

Concentrated purchasing exposes buyers to a supplier’s energy and trade policies even when deliveries remain reliable. Long-term agreements can stabilize parts of that relationship. Globally, damage to Gulf infrastructure has delayed some of the expected easing of supply: the IEA estimates **150 billion cubic metres in cumulative LNG supply losses over 2026–30** from the conflict. That projection depends on disrupted capacity and its return to service. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Terminals and imports provide a supply cushion

Infrastructure gives Europe useful room to manoeuvre. In its **8 October** outlook, **ENTSOG** assessed the gas system as sufficiently flexible to meet demand under various supply conditions. Terminals could process around **1,600 TWh**, or **145 billion cubic metres over a winter season**. That capacity still needs cargoes available at suitable ports and prices. The **146 bcm imported in 2025** covers a full year, a different time horizon. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

On **3 September**, the European Commission’s Gas Coordination Group saw no immediate risk to EU supply security, citing diversification, terminals and lower demand since 2022. A more recent assessment adds perspective: on **4 November**, Germany’s regulator, **Bundesnetzagentur**, still rated the **risk of supply stress in Germany as low**, with stable imports, while expecting relatively low stocks at winter’s end. Storage remains one source of vulnerability alongside weather, incoming flows and buyers’ responses. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

The EU retains a **90% storage target**, to be reached between **1 October and 1 December**, with flexibility for difficult market conditions and technical constraints. Legal compliance depends on that window and the provisions applicable to each country. An aggregate fill rate displayed on 4 November measures a physical position; assessing compliance requires the full legal context. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

## Weather and deliveries determine the winter outcome

A **mild winter**, sustained US deliveries and improving Gulf availability could preserve Europe’s reserve. This first pathway is qualitative: a numerical end-March estimate would require a weather model and the complete portfolio of contracted supply.

A winter with **normal temperatures** but continued Gulf disruptions would leave Europe competing with Asian buyers for flexible LNG. Physical deliveries could remain sufficient while high wholesale prices work through industrial costs and power markets. That mechanism follows the evidence assembled by ACER and the IEA. Its size and geographical distribution are unknown. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The **IEA stress case** combines **colder-than-average weather** with **continued interruption of LNG flows through Hormuz**. Under those conditions, EU storage **could end March 2027 below 10%** of working capacity. The operational warning starts earlier, with withdrawal rates tending to weaken **below 30%**. ENTSOG separately says sustained limits on LNG availability could leave storage **well below 30%** by winter's end. These are findings from different assessments, not points on a single modelled path. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

<USLNGWinterFigure lang="en" kind="thresholds" />

The winter’s financial consequences can extend into the refill season. Lower inventories in March mean larger summer injections are required to prepare for autumn 2027. If the global LNG market remains constrained, the next refill season creates further demand for flexible cargoes and can keep prices elevated. The IEA flags precisely this transmission from one winter to the next. It is an important reason to look past the traditional end-of-March finish line. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Security negotiated cargo by cargo

American LNG has helped absorb the Gulf shock and provides Europe with a route that avoids Hormuz. Access to flexible cargoes is negotiated against buyers worldwide. A cold winter in both Europe and Asia could therefore raise costs well before it threatens the continuity of deliveries. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The useful winter dashboard therefore contains more than a single storage number. It tracks **US loadings, actual European LNG arrivals, Asia-Europe netback spreads after freight, the daily withdrawal capacity of storage sites, industrial gas demand and physical flows through Hormuz**. Each indicator carries information about a different link in the supply chain. A deterioration across several of them would make the IEA's stress case materially more concerning.

Europe has reduced its exposure to Russian pipelines, expanded reception capacity and consumed less gas since the 2022 crisis. These changes give it more physical options. The cost of exercising those options increasingly depends on a global market in which Europe must compete for marginal supply. **US gas can help Europe get through winter. The price of that security is negotiated cargo by cargo.**

## Methodology and limitations

**Sources checked on 4 November 2026.** GIE figures reproduce the dashboard displayed that day, labelled “Status on 04/11/2026 at 6AM CEST”. That supplier label is retained without converting it into a gas day or local time. The dynamic page may change after publication.

Global LNG changes use **billion cubic metres (bcm)** over the stated period; EIA exports use **billion cubic feet per day (Bcf/d)**. Prices remain in **dollars per million BTU**, with no conversion into EUR/MWh. ACER’s **€18–33 billion** estimate is an indicative counterfactual with explicit qualifications about costs actually incurred. Eurostat shares measure **value**, while ACER shares measure **volume**. Storage percentages relate [working gas](/en/glossary/gaz-utile/) to capacity; cross-country comparisons also require demand and withdrawal rates.

The analysis uses institutional publications and Cheniere’s general contracting disclosures. Individual sales, shipping and hedging positions fall outside the evidence reviewed; allocating company profits would require those records. Figures are static inline SVGs. The opening image is a generated conceptual illustration, separate from documentary photography.
