**When selling a failed bank, the FDIC compares bids with the cost of liquidation. Its inspector general finds incomplete data, insufficiently supported assumptions and late explanations in the 2023 resolutions. Those weaknesses affect the benchmark used to decide whether a sale offers good value.** [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

*Investigation as of 16 November 2026. Monetary amounts are in nominal US dollars unless stated otherwise.*

On 1 May 2023, First Republic moved under JPMorgan Chase. Customers were meant to keep using their bank through the transition. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation presented the deal as the least costly outcome for its insurance fund. A memorandum explaining the choice of loss assumptions was created on 14 December: **227 days after the takeover**. That interval concerns the formalisation of the reasoning in this particular document. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 31–32; [S04](#source-S04)

Report EVAL-27-02 from the Office of Inspector General (OIG), dated **10 November 2026** and announced on **13 November**, examines the valuations of Silicon Valley Bank, Signature and First Republic in spring 2023. The findings weaken confidence in the liquidation estimates used to assess bids. Crucial rates and amounts remain redacted in the public version. The report lets readers follow decisions and shortcomings in their supporting records, with a specific limit on the financial verdict: recalculating an avoidable loss would require those data and a feasible alternative available at the same time. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 1, 13–25 and 35–37; [S02](#source-S02)

## Liquidation sets the benchmark for evaluating a bid

A bank can run out of liquidity before losing all its economic value. Deposit flight forces it to find funding or a buyer very quickly, as in the [US regional-bank crisis](/en/analysis/us-regional-banks-liquidity-lcr/). The distinction between liquidity and solvency, explained in our [guide to reading a bank’s soundness](/en/guides/read-bank-health/), becomes critical to preserving services and realising asset value.

In a resolution, the FDIC values loans and other assets, the deposits to be assumed and the expenses it would incur if it liquidated the bank. The [least-cost test](/en/glossary/test-de-moindre-cout/) compares that option with acceptable bids. The law focuses on total cost to the Deposit Insurance Fund, or DIF. Future and contingent obligations enter the comparison. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 2–6; [S03](#source-S03), § 1823(c)(4)(A)

Cash flows must be discounted: a payment several years away is translated into a comparable value at the reference date. The statute requires realistic discount rates, documented assumptions and retention of supporting records for at least five years. It also specifies dates for determining assistance and liquidation costs. A headline sale price therefore captures only part of a transaction that may include financing or guarantees. [S03](#source-S03), § 1823(c)(4)(B)–(D)

The three cases have different legal frameworks. **First Republic remained subject to the ordinary least-cost requirement.** For SVB and Signature, the systemic risk exception invoked on **12 March 2023** allowed all depositors to be protected and permitted departure from that statutory constraint. The FDIC nevertheless used its comparison model to assess best value in the sales. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 4 and 9–10; [S05](#source-S05) [S08](#source-S08)

<FDICResolutionValuation2026 figure={1} lang="en" />

Assumptions can change the preference between selling and liquidating. If assets retained by the FDIC are expected to recover less, liquidation costs rise. They also rise when anticipated expenses increase. An unchanged bid can then cross the comparison threshold. Ranking competing bids against one another depends on their respective assets, liabilities and commitments. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 35–36

Take an **entirely hypothetical example**, in billions of dollars already stated in present-value terms. A fund must repay 100 of insured deposits. With 96 recovered from assets and 4 of expenses, liquidation costs 100 − 96 + 4 = **8**. A sale costing 12 would be more expensive. With recoveries of 90 and expenses of 6, liquidation costs **16**: the unchanged sale at 12 becomes preferable. The example isolates this mechanism, setting aside other creditors and guarantees.

## Incomplete data make the choice of assumptions more consequential

The first difficulties come before the calculation. Detailed asset information must be reconciled with the general ledger, the bank’s central accounting record. FDIC officials said they had only one day’s notice before SVB closed. Its ledger was incomplete and unreliable. Accountants then needed weeks to understand what the accounts represented at failure. At Signature, data from some loan servicers arrived too late for the usual pre-closing reconciliation. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 13–14

The agency worked with models, internal specialists and outside advisers. The OIG found that full asset valuation reviews expected for SVB and Signature had not been obtained. The FDIC used what it called *blended* loss rates, selected from several approaches for individual portfolios. The report makes clear that these were selections, **without a weighted average or statistical method**. Explaining those choices therefore became particularly important. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 16–21 and p. 30, footnote 76

For SVB, the FDIC used bid information on Signature portfolios to revise its estimates after SVB’s bidding had closed. For Signature, it also increased estimated losses after receiving bids. Weak bids can provide information about a disrupted market and prompt correction of an optimistic assumption. The control question is whether the new information, the reason for the change and its approval are preserved. The OIG identifies inadequate justification for some revisions. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 17–21 and 29–31

The expense model created another weakness. The Receivership Expense Model, or REM, was poorly suited to large, complex banks, according to the inspector general. It may have overstated expenses and made liquidation appear more costly in the comparison. FDIC officials described it as the best tool available at the time. Its technical documentation nevertheless allowed expert judgement to replace model output in certain circumstances. The FDIC has since developed a new model, REEM, designed to produce more reliable estimates. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 35–36

## First Republic: 57 files received out of 355 selected

First Republic provides a particularly instructive number. The valuation contractor selected **355 loans** for review, but received only **57 loan files** to derive its estimates. The report links the shortfall to the compressed timetable. The initial selection methodology prioritised the three largest loans by balance in each sub-asset category. [S01](#source-S01), p. 23, footnote 55

The calculation 57 / 355 × 100 gives **16.1%** after rounding. Its denominator is the contractor’s requested selection. Assessing economic coverage would also require the balances and distribution of the files received. A small number of large loans can represent substantial money, with different risks from smaller credits.

<FDICResolutionValuation2026 figure={2} lang="en" />

Standard procedures directed analysts towards the midpoint of the contractor’s loss range. The FDIC chose a high-loss scenario for First Republic. Its explanation included banking-system stress, exceptional expenses associated with borrowing lines and the volume of assets it might have had to manage across several receiverships. The incomplete sample also complicated customary reliance on the midpoint. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 22–23

**The inspector general explicitly acknowledges that the high-loss scenario may have been appropriate.** Its criticism concerns departure from established practices and the quality of the supporting evidence. The December memorandum contained inaccurate information and relied on an outdated version of the asset valuation review, according to the OIG. Urgency may warrant a cautious judgement while still requiring a sound record of its basis. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 24 and 31–32

## The memoranda follow the takeovers

For Signature, the OIG records a takeover on **20 March 2023** and a memorandum finalised on **27 March**, seven days later. The agreement had been announced on 19 March. These dates concern different milestones: agreement, transfer of the business and finalisation of the document. For First Republic, 1 May to 14 December is an interval of 227 calendar days. The memoranda examined reveal the retained documentary record of the decisions. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 31–32; [S10](#source-S10) [S11](#source-S11)

<FDICResolutionValuation2026 figure={3} lang="en" />

Retention of final versions also fell short. Signature’s final least-cost analysis was absent from both official repositories examined. A version retained by one division preceded revisions by the other. The OIG identifies inconsistency with the agency’s internal requirements and difficulty for its board, auditors and Congress in reconstructing the decision. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 32–33, footnote 80

Initial DIF cost estimates show the scale of the transactions: **$20 billion for SVB, $2.5 billion for Signature and $13 billion for First Republic** in 2023. They describe the expected costs of the failures at the time, after losses and deposit flight. Measuring a loss caused by valuation weaknesses would require comparison with a credible alternative, available on the same date and covering the same commitments. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 8–9; [S04](#source-S04) [S12](#source-S12)

## Signet required a valuation of its own

Signet allowed real-time payments between Signature’s commercial customers. During a November 2022 readiness exercise, the FDIC had already identified potential franchise value and the need to examine whether the activity could be separated. During resolution, Signet remained within a general “other assets” category, without a separate valuation. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 14–16

The platform was difficult to isolate in the accounts. Officials interviewed by the OIG had not discussed its accounting treatment with Signature’s management. The inspector general consequently left its book value and estimated liquidation loss undetermined. A payment technology may derive value from customers, contracts and service continuity beyond its accounting treatment. This connects with the distinctions between charters, deposits and services examined in our investigation of [crypto and the meaning of bank](/en/analysis/crypto-banks-occ-icba-trust-charters-deposits/). [S01](#source-S01), pp. 15–16

Official testimony reports **$152,000** for marketed trademarks and preferred stock associated with Signet. The master servicing contract received no bids and was subsequently repudiated by the FDIC. The amount describes the elements sold. The value of the whole business before failure, and how much different preparation could have preserved, remain open questions. [S09](#source-S09), p. 8

## Financing and guarantees keep producing effects after a sale

JPMorgan announced **$50 billion in five-year, fixed-rate FDIC financing** for First Republic, alongside loss-sharing agreements on certain loans. The financing is repayable. The agreements allocate future risks between the buyer and the FDIC. Their economic cost depends on financial terms, repayments and losses actually covered. [S06](#source-S06) [S04](#source-S04)

The buyer also expected an initial post-tax gain of approximately **$2.6 billion**, excluding anticipated **$2 billion in post-tax restructuring costs**. Those expectations date from 1 May 2023. The loan, expected accounting gain and initial DIF cost estimate measure different things, to be assessed alongside the detailed commitments when judging the transaction’s terms. [S06](#source-S06)

For SVB and Signature, exceptional protection of uninsured deposits is to be funded through a special assessment on banks. Shareholders and certain unsecured creditors remained outside that protection. The law provides for recovery of the relevant losses; the FDIC later adjusted collection arrangements in a December 2025 decision. How the burden ultimately falls also depends on the pricing and other choices of the banks that pay it. [S03](#source-S03), § 1823(c)(4)(G); [S05](#source-S05) [S16](#source-S16)

Speed could preserve considerable value by limiting contagion. The GAO concluded in its January 2025 review that the measures likely helped stabilise the system. Several interventions, including the Federal Reserve’s BTFP emergency lending, operated together. The absence of an observable path without intervention prevents the effect of the systemic risk exception from being isolated. Stabilisation benefits and the quality of individual valuations therefore need to be assessed at their respective levels. [S08](#source-S08)

## The next decision must remain verifiable after the emergency

The FDIC accepts all five recommendations. At the report’s issuance, action on valuation contracts had been completed and that recommendation was closed. The other four remained open. The new contractual framework provides for expedited valuations deliverable in as little as three calendar days, with scope defined at engagement. Remaining work concerns specialised assets, indicative bids, documentation and shared records. The latest announced deadline is **30 September 2027**. [S01](#source-S01), pp. 37 and 44–46

An earlier OIG report, published on **10 December 2024**, had already described insufficiently mature readiness for large regional bank failures. In June 2026, the FDIC presented new resolution-readiness proposals. The Comptroller of the Currency argued for strengthening the agency’s own capabilities. In the regulatory publications consulted as of 16 November, the June texts remained classified as proposals. [S07](#source-S07) [S13](#source-S13) [S14](#source-S14)

The operational test begins with the files: obtaining usable data, reconciling them with the accounts and being able to explain an assumption changed over a weekend. The bank-franchise marketing evaluation, still listed as ongoing work as of 13 November, will need to be read alongside this report. [S15](#source-S15) **A price decided under emergency pressure becomes defensible when its assumptions, successive versions and alternatives remain open to scrutiny after the panic passes.** [S01](#source-S01), pp. 1 and 33–37

## Limits

Redactions in the public report prevent reconstruction of loss rates, decisive portfolio values and estimated expenses. The findings concern procedures, data and supporting records, with identified financial risks. The sensitivity example is hypothetical and simplified. Initial costs announced in 2023 remain dated estimates, separate from final costs or damage attributable to the weaknesses. The seven-day and 227-day intervals date the documents examined, rather than the start of all analytical work. Discrepancies between the catalogue and report on recommendation status, and between the OIG report and testimony on the year of Signet-related sales, are set out in S15 and S09.
