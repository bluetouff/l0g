<p class="series-kicker">After the cyberattack, who pays? · Part 2/8</p>

<p class="standfirst">Restarting a factory takes suppliers able to finance their work. The JLR crisis reveals how that capacity came under strain and how lenders tried to restore it.</p>

In the accounts Autins published on 25 November 2025, its UK invoice-financing facility with HSBC carries a ceiling of £3.5 million. A qualification determines how much of that credit the company can actually use: availability depends on its outstanding customer receivables. Autins makes acoustic and thermal insulation, including for JLR. When the carmaker’s production stopped, fewer new invoices followed, reducing the assets on which part of Autins’ borrowing depended. The supplier identifies the fall in invoicing as one of the reasons its liquidity headroom contracted. [1](#source-01) [2](#source-02)

[The first instalment followed JLR’s industrial shutdown](/en/analysis/after-cyberattack-1-jlr-factories-cash-flow/). For a supplier, spending on a component comes before the proceeds from its sale. The work has to be financed throughout that interval. The crisis therefore raises a practical question: what can a bank lend against when the business that generates the invoices comes to a halt?

Autins’ disclosures, Lear’s results and the financing arrangements introduced for JLR allow us to follow that change. They also explain why announcing a support facility and establishing what it delivered to factories require different stages of investigation.

## The missing orders travel upstream

On 17 September 2025, Autins described its initial response in a regulatory announcement. It was using its banked-hours system, delaying or cancelling purchases of raw materials and suspending discretionary spending. The company identified JLR as a major customer whose production stoppage was affecting its UK operations. These decisions brought the cyber incident into another business’s day-to-day organisation. [2](#source-02)

Following the purchases makes the transmission easier to understand. The carmaker needs fewer components; its supplier revises the quantities of material it had planned to buy. The producer or distributor of that material then receives less demand, even if its own equipment and systems remain available. Commercial dependence carries an industrial interruption beyond the business where it began. Autins’ published decisions provide a concrete example, although the documents reviewed do not identify the suppliers or values of the cancelled purchases. [2](#source-02)

Payroll presents another commitment to manage. Autins subsequently described its banked-hours scheme as a way of providing certainty over workers’ pay while customer demand fluctuated. The individual terms are outside the public record examined here. The announcement documents an adjustment to working time; individual hours and effects on earnings would require payroll records. [1](#source-01)

A regional survey captures some of the pressure at the time. In findings published on 26 September by the West Midlands chambers of commerce, 35% of the 84 responding businesses said they had reduced working hours or temporarily stood down staff; 14% said they were making redundancies. These proportions describe the businesses that responded, 79% of which reported being in JLR’s supply chain. They cannot be read as the proportion of workers made redundant or as a representative measure of the entire industry. They do, however, record adjustments already under way before the new supplier-financing programme was announced in October. [3](#source-03)

That sequence matters when production resumes. A business that has slowed its purchasing must secure materials again as its customer starts requesting components. One that has reorganised its workforce must be able to put working hours back into production. A new order offers the prospect of revenue, while the spending needed to fulfil it comes earlier. Financing has to cover that interval.

## Credit that depends on the next invoice

Invoice financing uses a trade receivable: money a customer owes its supplier. Under the terms of the financing agreement, a bank can advance part of that money before the customer’s payment falls due. An invoiced sale thereby becomes a source of funding for continued operations. At Autins, the £3.5 million HSBC limit remains subject to available receivables. The headline ceiling has to be read alongside the assets that make it usable. [1](#source-01)

A business interruption can disturb this arrangement in two ways. Existing invoices may await payment, while slower deliveries generate fewer new receivables. As older invoices are settled, the financing attached to them unwinds. If subsequent sales are missing, the usual pool of invoices available for financing is replenished more slowly. A credit facility can retain its contractual ceiling while providing less usable funding, because its availability is tied to trading activity.

Autins’ figures make the distinction visible. Its consolidated liquidity headroom fell from £3.47 million on 30 September 2024 to £1.52 million on 30 September 2025. The measure combines net cash at bank, overdrafts and unused invoice-financing capacity across the group. Comparing it directly with the ceiling on the UK facility would therefore mix two different scopes. Autins attributes the decline mainly to lower invoicing after the cyber incident and repayments of existing loans. The respective contribution of those causes is not disclosed. [1](#source-01)

The temporary £250,000 overdraft HSBC granted in October provided another funding option. Autins reported that it remained undrawn when the interim results were published on 25 November. It was additional borrowing capacity, whose availability could offer flexibility in managing payments as they fell due. The disclosure establishes the authorisation and its status on that date; tracing any subsequent use would require further documents. [1](#source-01)

These distinctions change how a business under pressure should be described. A bank balance, an authorised credit line and a liquidity-headroom measure each answer a different question. The balance records money held on a particular date; the credit line sets out a contractual funding option; published headroom brings together several resources still available. Establishing how long Autins could keep operating would also require its expected receipts, payment schedule and detailed borrowing terms. The public accounts reveal the strain without supplying that daily cash calendar.

## The cost of keeping capacity ready

Autins’ annual results, published on 29 June 2026 for the twelve months ended 31 March, identify £534,000 of costs classified as non-underlying items connected with the cyber incident at its major customer. This is a defined expense category identified by the company. Lost sales and the wider economic consequences would require a broader assessment; the timing of cash payments would require another set of information. [4](#source-04)

Lear provides a view at a different scale. On its 31 October 2025 earnings call, the chief financial officer explained that the incident’s effect on quarterly earnings included labour costs incurred while production was interrupted. No separate payroll breakdown was provided. The disclosure nevertheless describes the industrial problem: keeping capacity available while volumes are missing weighs on the period’s earnings. [5](#source-05)

In annual results published on 4 February 2026, Lear estimated that the JLR incident had reduced its 2025 sales by $217 million and adjusted operating earnings by $54 million. The latter measure, which Lear calls *core operating earnings*, excludes certain accounting items. The attribution to the cyber incident is the company’s estimate. It is distinct from a cash payment, a compensation claim or net income. [6](#source-06)

Those estimates changed as more information became available. In October, Lear was still projecting an annual effect of roughly $255 million on sales and $71 million on operating earnings. This article uses the retrospective figures published in February. The difference between the two disclosures records a revised estimate; treating it as money recovered or compensation received would assign a meaning the documents do not support. [7](#source-07) [6](#source-06)

For the same year, 2025, Lear reported positive consolidated [free cash flow (FCF)](/en/glossary/fcf/) of $527.2 million. The figure covers the whole group, after deducting capital expenditure under Lear’s definition. It places the JLR disruption within a business whose operations, taken together, continued to generate cash. Consolidated accounts describe that overall capacity; understanding how a particular site or team experienced the shutdown would require evidence at that level. [6](#source-06)

The useful comparison with Autins concerns how the interruption is absorbed. One operation can incur an identifiable cost while drawing on the resources of a wider group. Another can see the assets supporting a sales-linked credit facility diminish. Adding their figures together would obscure that distinction, mixing currencies, measures and scopes that describe different constraints.

## Bringing the advance forward to the order

On 7 October 2025, JLR announced a phased restart of manufacturing and a new early-payment programme. It would serve eligible suppliers, beginning with those considered critical to the restart. The main advance was to follow shortly after the purchase order, with a final adjustment when the invoice arrived. Subsequent accounts specified a facility of up to £500 million and advances of up to 90% of the order value. [8](#source-08) [9](#source-09)

This form of [supply chain finance](/en/glossary/scf/) brings funding forward within the production cycle. Invoice financing starts once the sale has created a receivable. An advance against an order can help fund production itself. It brings the cash receipt closer to the point at which the supplier commits resources, shortening the interval it must cover through its usual sources of funding.

JLR described its ordinary terms as payment 60 days after invoice and said the new programme could accelerate payment by as much as 120 days. That maximum depends on the sequence of ordering, manufacturing and invoicing; it establishes neither an observed average nor a common timetable for every supplier. The announcement also committed JLR to reimbursing participants’ financing costs during the recovery phase. [8](#source-08)

Publications by Crédit Agricole CIB and its legal adviser, Norton Rose Fulbright, identify the bank behind the arrangement. Both describe financing that advances cash to suppliers while preserving JLR’s payment terms. The carmaker’s accounts state that it reimburses the finance provider for each advance according to the supplier’s usual payment terms or by a backstop date. The bank therefore supplies the interim funding, while the manufacturer retains a repayment obligation. [10](#source-10) [11](#source-11) [9](#source-09)

That description explains the movement of money. Reconstructing the full allocation of risk would require the agreement itself: what happens to an advance if a component is disputed, the quantity changes or delivery fails? Which party has recourse against which, and what security applies? The public announcements and accounting notes reviewed leave those clauses unresolved. The bank’s and law firm’s accounts confirm their participation in the transaction; their interest in it also matters when assessing their descriptions of its benefits.

Access to the programme is another practical boundary. The announcement envisaged a phased opening, with expansion to other suppliers, including some outside production. The evidence reviewed does not establish whether Autins or Lear used it. Their disclosures help explain suppliers’ exposure; their individual participation in this financing arrangement remains to be documented. [8](#source-08)

## The bank guarantee and supplier advances

Another figure had already entered the public debate on 28 September: £1.5 billion for a credit facility to JLR supported by UK Export Finance, the UK’s export credit agency. That transaction combines commercial lenders with a public guarantee. It provides the manufacturer with a reserve of available funding. The £500 million programme arranges advances to suppliers. The documents describe two routes, with different immediate recipients and obligations. [12](#source-12) [9](#source-09)

In his letter dated 13 October 2025, Business and Trade Secretary Peter Kyle stated that the banks would retain £300 million of uncovered risk on the £1.5 billion facility. Relative to the principal, those amounts correspond to a 20% bank share and, by subtraction, 80% coverage. The split describes exposure in the event of default. Actual disbursements and any eventual loss to the public finances would then depend on use of the loan, its repayment and the guarantee’s terms. [13](#source-13) The calculation concerns principal; the documents reviewed do not establish the full terms for covering interest and fees. [13](#source-13)

The £1.5 billion facility remained fully undrawn on 30 June 2026, according to the quarterly accounts published on 13 August. This is the latest specific accounting observation verified for this article. On 9 September 2026, minister Blair McDougall told Parliament that the guarantee remained in place and JLR had not drawn on the facility. That statement is more recent than the June accounts; it does not establish the balance on 18 October. [14](#source-14) [20](#source-20)

In his reply dated 7 November 2025, Kyle argued that an accessible reserve had value: knowing it was there could give JLR greater confidence to commit its existing cash. The reasoning is consistent with the function of a liquidity reserve. Measuring its actual benefit to suppliers, however, would require linking that reassurance to JLR’s payment decisions and the amounts received further down the chain. The letter does not quantify that link. [15](#source-15)

The decision to work through JLR also reflected a need for speed, according to the minister. In his October letter, he explained that lenders’ existing knowledge of the carmaker’s credit quality allowed them to act more quickly. Supporting suppliers individually would have required separate assessments and banking arrangements and, in some cases, overcoming difficulties with eligibility for UKEF’s instruments. The approach therefore concentrated the intervention on a business able to organise payments within its supply chain. That makes tracing the subsequent distribution of funding all the more important. [13](#source-13)

## How far down the chain did the money reach?

The parliamentary Business and Trade Committee’s hearing on 11 November 2025 brought this question into focus. MP Antonia Bance relayed the concerns of second- and third-tier suppliers in her constituency. Kyle set out the government’s expectation that money reaching direct suppliers should pass through to their own suppliers. When MP Justin Madders asked how many businesses in those lower tiers had actually benefited from the financing, the minister could not provide a number. [16](#source-16)

The exchange exposes a precise measurement problem. Trading receipts and financial support can arrive in a business’s cash flow at the same time. During the hearing, the minister said he found it difficult to distinguish their respective contributions to the improvement observed among suppliers further removed from the carmaker. The transcript establishes that limit to visibility on 11 November. A complete later assessment would require further data on recipients, amounts and dates. [16](#source-16)

For a second-tier business, several routes are possible. Its direct customer might settle an old invoice, order more material, make an advance payment or temporarily retain cash to meet other commitments. Those transactions have different effects on the business’s ability to restart work. The existence of financing at the top of the chain therefore cannot establish its timing or form at each subsequent stage. Payments would have to be traced from one commercial relationship to the next.

JLR’s annual report subsequently offered the company’s own assessment of the advance-payment programme. It said uptake had been low and the arrangement was being wound down, which it attributed to the speed of the recovery. The notes report accounting positions at particular period ends. They show the scheme within JLR’s accounts, while leaving unanswered the cumulative amount received by each supplier and the needs that fell outside its scope. [9](#source-09)

Limited use is compatible with several situations: needs may have receded quickly, other financing may have been available, participation may have been restricted, or the offer may not have matched some suppliers’ requirements. Distinguishing those explanations requires records of access and payments. JLR’s published assessment belongs in the account, with its attribution; the available data still provide only a partial picture of the experience of recipients and businesses that remained outside the programme.

## Who finances the time needed to restart?

The thread connecting these documents is the interval between an order and its payment. Purchasing and labour commit resources before the product brings in revenue. Credit helps organise that interval, but its terms become decisive when sales are interrupted. At Autins, fewer invoices contributed to a reduction in available funding. In JLR’s programme, the advance moved back to the order stage to support production still to come. [1](#source-01) [8](#source-08)

An early payment can buy a supplier time. Depending on its needs and the terms it obtains, the supplier can fund spending before the ordinary commercial payment date. The cost of the lost weeks raises a further question: expenses remain in the accounts of those who incurred them until a contractual payment, compensation or another resource potentially offsets them. Autins’ identified charges and Lear’s estimates show why the inquiry needs to continue beyond the restart of the factories. [4](#source-04) [6](#source-06)

The next decisive document would be a record connecting the announced funding to identified businesses, then to the payment timing of their own suppliers. It would show where credit restored the ability to work and where the wait continued to be absorbed through cash resources, purchasing decisions or the organisation of employees’ time. That is the level at which support for an industrial supply chain becomes verifiable.

## Method and limits

The research for this documentary investigation has a cut-off date of 18 October 2026. It draws on regulatory disclosures by Autins and Lear, JLR’s accounts and announcements, documents describing the banking transaction, parliamentary correspondence and hearings, and a chambers of commerce survey. The notes below provide source locations, reporting periods and limits for each document. Later publications identified in the research, including Autins’ trading update of 23 September 2026, were reviewed to follow developments; historical figures retain the date and scope of the reference publication used for them. [19](#source-19)

The work uses public documents, without interviews or access to individual contracts, bank statements or payroll data. Estimates of causal effects remain attributed to the companies publishing them. The diagrams explain documented mechanisms: spacing represents stages, while the lengths of explicitly graduated bars represent amounts. Sterling and dollar figures retain their original currencies.

JLR’s interim accounts and annual report allow the existence and accounting treatment of the financing programme to be checked. The balances at each reporting date also have to be read according to their accounting category. At 31 March 2026, JLR reports £37 million of advances against undelivered orders, representing rights to receive inventory, and £27 million owed to the finance provider. These positions describe rights and obligations at the reporting date; adding them together does not measure cumulative payments to suppliers. Questions about recourse, transmission to subsequent tiers and the precise effect on employment remain identified lines of further enquiry. [17](#source-17) [18](#source-18) [9](#source-09)
