## A $26 billion private placement alongside multiyear compute contracts

*Documentary investigation · l0g · 11 November 2026. Sources checked through this date; the reference prospectus was filed on 10 November. All amounts are in US dollars. The initial public offering and concurrent private placement remain proposed transactions, subject to their closing conditions.*

A ten-billion-dollar payment can replenish a company’s cash without increasing the total funding already promised. A forty-two-billion-dollar facility can cover rent without leaving the tenant a single dollar to spend elsewhere. Both arrangements appear in the documents for Anthropic’s planned stock market debut: the first involves Google; the second, Broadcom. Examining them together reveals far more about AI financing than adding up fundraising announcements. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02) [S04](#source-S04)

On 10 November, Anthropic announced the launch of its IPO roadshow. Alongside the public offering, seven partners in the computing supply chain are due to subscribe for $26 billion of shares. They sell services, chips, memory or manufacturing capacity on which the laboratory’s expansion depends. Their investments give them a stake in the customer’s future value; its orders can also support their own businesses. [S01](#source-S01) [S21](#source-S21)

This investigation establishes contractual links between financing and purchasing. It also identifies substantial commercial demand and protections for the parties involved. The central question concerns timing: **can customer receipts take over from the capital raised to reserve computing capacity?** The documents reveal several financial routes and the points at which they could become vulnerable. They cannot tell us how much demand would have existed without that support.

## The $26 billion is due to arrive. Some was already promised

Google and Nvidia are each contributing $10 billion. AMD plans to invest $2.5 billion; Broadcom, Microsoft and Micron, $1 billion each; and TSMC, $500 million. Google and Nvidia therefore account for 76.9% of the placement. The Class N shares are to be purchased at the IPO price. They have the same economic rights as Class A shares, with different voting and conversion provisions: Class N shares generally carry no voting rights. [S01](#source-S01) [S22](#source-S22)

The placement is scheduled to close immediately after the IPO, subject to its other contractual conditions. The dependency runs in one direction: the placement requires the IPO to close, while the IPO can proceed without the placement. The published agreements provide for transfers of immediately available funds. Treating the entire $26 billion as a conversion of money already received would therefore be wrong. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02) [S03](#source-S03)

The double-counting problem lies elsewhere. Google has had a financing agreement in place since March 2026. Its $10 billion IPO investment exercises an option under that agreement and reduces the remaining facility from $20 billion to $10 billion. The cash becomes available at closing, but was already part of a broader funding commitment. AMD’s $2.5 billion subscription similarly reduces a $5 billion commitment tied to the deployment of its equipment. The remainder will depend on delivery and deployment milestones. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02) [S06](#source-S06)

**At least $12.5 billion of the $26 billion therefore overlaps with explicitly identified earlier commitments**, or 48.1%. Yet the arithmetic remainder of $13.5 billion cannot safely be called “entirely new funding”. In November 2025, Nvidia and Microsoft announced investments of up to $10 billion and $5 billion respectively. The February 2026 funding round expressly included part of those commitments, without an individual breakdown in the announcement. The documents reviewed provide no complete reconciliation establishing that every dollar in November 2026 comes on top of all earlier announcements. [S08](#source-S08) [S09](#source-S09)

The May fundraising presents the same issue: its announced $65 billion included $15 billion previously committed by hyperscalers, the largest cloud operators. Amazon accounted for $5 billion of that amount. Its own quarterly report subsequently confirmed two $5 billion investments in 2026, one of which reduced a future financing facility. A fundraising announcement, a commitment ceiling and a payment are three different events. [S10](#source-S10) [S12](#source-S12)

## A supplier can benefit at several points in the chain

Following the counterparties matters as much as following the dollars. An equity investment provides Anthropic with funding. A compute payment buys a cloud service or access to equipment. A chip purchase by a data centre operator pays the manufacturer. Finally, a customer pays Anthropic to use Claude. These transactions can form a connected economic chain, with a separate contract, timetable and accounting treatment at each stage.

Google can act as an investor, cloud supplier, partner in TPU architecture and distributor of Claude. TPUs are processors designed for AI computation. Nvidia also operates upstream: operators buy its systems and then sell Anthropic access to the machines. The joint November 2025 announcement explicitly connected Nvidia’s investment with Anthropic’s expansion on Azure using Nvidia hardware. [S08](#source-S08) [S19](#source-S19)

This arrangement aligns several interests. The shareholder wants the laboratory to grow; the seller wants orders; the equipment financier wants regular rent. Interests can diverge over prices, performance, delivery schedules or the ability to move workloads to a competitor. A group may even supply Anthropic with infrastructure while selling its own competing AI model. [S15](#source-S15)

The FTC described this kind of circular spending in January 2025, after obtaining documents relating to several partnerships. Its staff report identified cloud commitments that channelled much of the invested capital back to suppliers, alongside information rights and the costs of switching architectures. The report examines mechanisms and competition risks. Its historical scope, with some details anonymised, does not support attributing every provision to all the 2026 agreements; nor does it find that unlawful conduct occurred. [S15](#source-S15)

The value a supplier adds must therefore be assessed through the service or equipment it actually provides, the costs it bears and the risk it retains. An investment does not turn entirely into profit when an order comes back. Adding cloud revenue to chip revenue as a measure of final demand would count successive stages of the same supply chain.

## Available cash comes with existing obligations

The placement documents do not publicly earmark each contribution for a particular invoice. The prospectus describes general uses of proceeds: operating needs, investment, compute, hiring and other business spending. Management retains broad discretion. It would therefore be unwarranted to claim that Google’s entire $10 billion must return to Google, or that every Nvidia dollar pays for an Nvidia chip. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02) [S03](#source-S03)

Anthropic has, however, signed commercial commitments that constrain its economic choices. The laboratory can decide how to use fresh funding while remaining obliged to pay for services reserved earlier. Cash is fungible: new funds enter a common pool, while each obligation remains payable under its own contract. Public information does not isolate a precise percentage of the $26 billion that is free of all commercial constraints.

Three cloud agreements illustrate how far those obligations extend. The figures below reflect the contracts as described in the 10 November prospectus. For this assessment, they supersede the amounts in earlier announcements. [S01, p. 180](#source-S01)

| Supplier | Stated commitment | Contract period | Essential qualification |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Microsoft | Minimum $47.2bn; up to $49.2bn | November 2026–June 2033 | Remedies for an uncured material breach and certain undelivered tranches |
| Google | Minimum of approximately $121.3bn | April 2026–July 2033 | Spending shortfalls must be made up, subject to adjustments and exceptions; remedies for an uncured material breach |
| Amazon | Minimum $110bn | May 2026–April 2036 | Spending shortfalls must be made up, subject to adjustments and exceptions; remedies for an uncured material breach |

Their shared logic is a minimum purchase obligation independent of actual usage. When agreed capacity is available but underused, payment may still be due. This is the mechanism commonly called [*take-or-pay*](/en/glossary/take-or-pay/): paying for reserved resources even when the laboratory’s customers do not consume enough of them. Delivery, commencement conditions and the remedies in each contract remain crucial. [S01](#source-S01)

Such spending can fund genuine expansion. In April 2026, Anthropic and Amazon announced up to five gigawatts of new capacity; Google and Broadcom also announced additional capacity starting in 2027. Advance purchasing commitments allow suppliers to prepare hardware and infrastructure well before they are used. An announcement in gigawatts, however, describes electrical capacity associated with the facilities; it establishes neither immediate availability nor the eventual profitability of every machine. [S11](#source-S11) [S19](#source-S19)

For a supplier, a long contract makes an investment easier to finance. For Anthropic, it secures access to compute. Both decisions depend on future demand. Financial support can enable additional capacity to be built while also securing an order already negotiated. Public documents do not separate the contribution of each effect.

The financing facilities have deadlines of their own. After the placement, Google’s remaining facility is to stand at $10 billion: requests can continue through the end of 2030, subject to capacity and revenue conditions. Amazon had $15 billion remaining at 30 September, available upon capacity milestones, with a window ending thirty months after the IPO or earlier if certain commercial agreements terminate. These facilities cannot be treated as bank deposits available on demand. [S01](#source-S01) [S06](#source-S06) [S07](#source-S07) [S12](#source-S12)

## Broadcom: financing becomes part of the rent arrangement

The Broadcom agreements provide the most direct contractual evidence. A facility can finance up to $42 billion of quarterly rent under a 2026 equipment agreement through convertible notes: debt that may be exchanged for shares. No notes had been drawn at 30 September. The facility is a conditional financing option, with a draw period beginning when the relevant equipment becomes ready for use. [S01](#source-S01) [S04](#source-S04)

The permitted use is explicit. The amended wording distinguishes a lessor that is also the financier, or its affiliate, from a third-party lessor: [settlement by offset](/en/glossary/reglement-par-compensation/) in the first case, payment directly to the lessor in the second. Anthropic receives no transfer it can freely redirect. A rental bill is settled in exchange for a financial claim against the laboratory. The documentation retains older, general wording excluding cash payment; the 26 October amendment expressly adds payment to a third-party lessor. Reading the provisions together establishes the earmarking without overlooking that drafting nuance. [S04, § 2.1(a) and (c)](#source-S04)

The cost of this financing matters as much as its size. The facility cannot cover the first quarterly payment for each equipment group or any rent due before that group’s ready-for-service date, under section 2.1(a). Newly issued notes normally bear annual interest of 9%, added to principal each quarter through [payment-in-kind, or PIK, interest](/en/glossary/pik/). After the IPO, the holder can elect to convert them at 90% of the reference weighted average share price over thirty trading sessions. Notes issued during a defined period of revenue underperformance bear 13% interest and convert at 85% of that reference price. These terms apply to new notes issued during the underperformance period; they do not justify automatically repricing every outstanding note. [S04](#source-S04)

The mechanism can therefore become more expensive as commercial performance weakens. Capitalised interest preserves cash in the near term but increases the claim. A conversion discount increases the number of shares needed to settle a given amount. Prolonged deterioration can also close access to further drawings, subject to the contract’s conditions. The decisive revenue thresholds are redacted in the public version, making it impossible to calculate how close Anthropic actually is to those triggers. [S04](#source-S04)

Repayment also has a deadline. Depending on the tranche, the notes mature 57, 60 or 63 months after the draw period begins, rather than that many months after each subsequent issuance. Conversion is at the holder’s election; any unconverted balance must normally be repaid in cash. The arrangement postpones payment and provides a potential route to settlement in shares, without guaranteeing that the repayment obligation will disappear. [S04](#source-S04)

Certain contractual defaults, including defaults on rent or material indebtedness, can also trigger early repayment and terminate remaining commitments, subject to the applicable conditions and cure periods. [S04, § 6](#source-S04)

The start of rent payments and eligibility for financing follow separate conditions. The prospectus states that some rent may start before the data centre is ready if site preparation is delayed. Financing can subsequently avoid an immediate cash outflow for eligible payments, leaving debt or [equity dilution](/en/glossary/dilution-du-capital/). If revenue falls behind, the terms of that financing can tighten just as the company needs it more. This remains a risk scenario; no threshold breach is established here. [S01, p. 181](#source-S01) [S04, § 2.1(a)](#source-S04)

## Behind the machines sit lenders and guarantees

A supplier can also bring in other investors to finance the equipment. On 26 October, Bank of America, Blackstone, Citi and Morgan Stanley announced a $63 billion transaction through a [special-purpose vehicle, or SPV](/en/glossary/spv/). It is to buy Broadcom accelerators and networking equipment for lease to Anthropic, with deliveries expected to start in 2027. Banks are arranging first-lien debt; Blackstone is providing the committed portion of second-lien financing. Broadcom is providing [residual value support](/en/glossary/vrg/) equal to the first-lien financing. [S13](#source-S13)

This structure distributes risk. The senior lender depends on rent and its contractual protections; the subordinated lender accepts a lower place in the repayment queue; the supplier can remain exposed to the equipment’s resale value. Broadcom’s own quarterly report describes a guarantee on an earlier transaction tied to the difference between a portion of the protected outstanding balance and the value recovered from the racks. The earlier transaction’s terms cannot simply be carried over to the October deal. [S14](#source-S14)

The $63 billion measures an asset-financing transaction. The $42 billion describes potential financing for certain rents. The $161.2 billion of equipment rent, excluding interest, disclosed by Anthropic covers a group of leases. The $26 billion is an equity subscription. **Adding them together would produce an economically meaningless total**: the same equipment or rental payment can appear at several levels. [S01](#source-S01) [S04](#source-S04) [S13](#source-S13)

A renegotiation of purchases would therefore affect different parties depending on the contract. It could reduce cloud receipts, delay rent, impair equipment values or trigger a guarantee. The name on the chip alone cannot tell us who would bear the cost. That depends on the lessor and creditor, the collateral, the debt’s ranking and the recourse provisions. Public announcements do not provide enough information to calculate a consolidated loss across the entire chain.

## $900.9 billion in commitments, payments extending to 2045

The prospectus’s most striking aggregate requires careful reading. Its $900.9 billion brings together obligations recorded at 30 September, short-term commitments, signed leases on which rent has yet to begin, and agreements concluded after that date. Leases not yet commenced alone account for approximately $451.2 billion of future undiscounted payments. Adding the value of the equipment financed by those leases would count the same economic activity again. [S01, p. 178–179](#source-S01)

Timing changes the interpretation: approximately $70.7 billion is expected to fall due in the twelve months after 30 September 2026; $451.6 billion before the end of 2030; and most of the remainder between 2031 and 2045. The first amount is included in the second. An aggregate of nominal future spending spread over years measures neither debt immediately payable nor revenue already earned by suppliers. [S01](#source-S01)

Approximately $186.2 billion of the total is associated with conditions or termination rights before commencement. Subtracting it does not turn the remainder into an unconditional claim: delivery provisions and other remedies still matter. The prospectus also identifies, outside the aggregate, an estimated $150 billion to $175 billion of payments under agreements with no fixed dollar commitment. Their conditions and uncertainty require separate presentation. [S01](#source-S01)

Nor is every purchase as rigid as a ten-year cloud minimum. The SpaceXAI contracts allow exits after their initial firm periods; a Lambda tranche became terminable because the facility had not been secured by the agreed date, although the prospectus reported no termination. These differences are more informative than a single sum presented as irrevocable. [S01, p. 180–181](#source-S01)

The economic challenge is the difference between the visibility available to suppliers and to the laboratory. Reserving capacity for years can give the seller greater confidence about future receipts. Much of what Anthropic sells is usage that will depend on its customers’ future choices. A compute contract commits spending; a capable model alone cannot guarantee the corresponding cash collections.

## Paying customers, and a funding dependence that needs measuring

The accounts require taking the strongest counterargument seriously: Anthropic is selling its service at substantial scale. For the nine months ended 30 September 2026, it reports $33.242 billion in revenue and $6.569 billion in net cash generated by operations. The interim accounts are unaudited. Cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments together reached $131.845 billion at that date. These figures do not describe a company surviving solely on fresh cheques from suppliers. [S01](#source-S01)

Operating cash flow also depends on when receipts are collected and bills paid. It is measured before spending classified as investing activity and does not represent cash left over after meeting future commitments. [S01](#source-S01)

They also illustrate the importance of reading the accounts correctly. The $104.039 billion net loss includes $94.324 billion of fair-value changes in convertible notes and other instruments. A rise in the company’s value can increase the accounting cost of its financing obligations without an equivalent cash outflow. Meanwhile, the $36.546 billion of investing cash outflows includes $33.418 billion of net purchases of short-term investments. Calling the net loss, or that entire cash-flow category, “cash burned” would distort the investigation. [S01, financial statements F-13](#source-S01)

*The adjustments reconcile earnings and cash flows; they do not eliminate future commitments. Purchases of investments move cash between asset categories.*

An external observation supports the existence of paying customers. In its September 2026 data, published on 14 October, Ramp reports that 43.8% of businesses in its sample paid Anthropic. That measures the presence of a payment among the businesses Ramp observes. It is neither a dollar-based market share nor a representative measure of all US businesses. It also does not trace the source of those customers’ capital. [S18](#source-S18)

Another distinction matters: sales through cloud marketplaces accounted for 44% of revenue in the first nine months of 2026. This is a distribution-channel share. It does not mean hyperscalers bought 44% of services for their own use. Anthropic says it retains the end-customer contract, responsibility for delivery and control over pricing, supporting its recognition of gross revenue in these arrangements. [S01, p. 60–61 and F-22](#source-S01)

The evidence therefore leaves two compatible facts standing: substantial paying demand, and an infrastructure expansion that still depends on vast amounts of financing. Recognised revenue does not answer the counterfactual question. Measuring demand independent of financial support would require actual prices after discounts and credits, renewals, margins by use case, the sources of payments and customers’ behaviour as support declines. Public disclosures do not isolate all those elements.

A price reduction provides a simple illustration. With the sales mix unchanged, a hypothetical 20% price cut requires 25% more volume to maintain revenue: 0.8 × 1.25 = 1. That calculation does not automatically preserve margins, because additional usage consumes compute. It is not a forecast for Anthropic; it shows why request volumes and the dollars available to pay for infrastructure can move differently.

## Contracts retain their influence without voting rights

Suppliers’ shareholdings do not imply that they run Anthropic. Under the proposed structure, the placement’s Class N shares are nonvoting. A founders’ entity is to hold 50.1% of voting power on general shareholder matters, while a special class of shares gives the Long-Term Benefit Trust the right to elect a majority of directors. Economic ownership, shareholder votes and board appointments therefore follow different routes. [S01](#source-S01) [S23](#source-S23)

In November 2024, the UK’s CMA concluded that the agreements it had examined did not give Google material influence over Anthropic. Among its reasons were the absence of a board seat or voting rights, and the availability of other funding and compute providers. That dated finding provides a useful counterweight to the assumption that any large investor automatically exercises control. The Amazon review, by contrast, ended because the UK jurisdictional thresholds were not met; the CMA did not need to determine whether Amazon had material influence. [S16](#source-S16) [S17](#source-S17)

A commercial contract can nevertheless shape decisions without conferring a vote. A costly technical migration, a purchase minimum, a facility linked to the deployment of a particular architecture or a convertible claim changes the available choices. Multiple suppliers reduce dependence on any one of them; they do not remove obligations already signed with each. Competition between infrastructures remains valuable, but takes place against an existing book of commitments. [S04](#source-S04) [S06](#source-S06) [S07](#source-S07) [S15](#source-S15)

The next stage of scrutiny should focus on observable developments: cash actually received after the IPO and reductions in earlier facilities; capacity entering service and minimums actually billed; use of rent-financing facilities; changes in customer receipts and discounts; renegotiations, guarantees called and creditor concentration. Sustained improvement in cash generation, alongside utilised capacity and less need for support, would strengthen the industrial explanation. Repeated deferrals, mounting debt incurred to pay rent or tighter financing terms would point to a different vulnerability.

The documents establish that suppliers help finance their customer’s purchasing capacity. At Broadcom, the link is built into the financing’s permitted use; at Google and AMD, part of the placement brings forward or fulfils earlier commitments tied to the computing ecosystem. The evidence supports neither calling all revenue artificial nor estimating an “organic” share with confidence. **The best-documented risk is the mismatch between compute spending committed over several years and revenues that customers will continue to reassess.** If completed, the IPO will provide resources to carry that mismatch. Closing it will depend on Claude’s underlying economics.
