**At Fortescue, shipments are running ahead of sales. Its stand-off with China Mineral Resources Group shows how an organised buyer can make time work against a supplier ; and how mines, ports and blast furnaces set limits to that power.**

*A documentary investigation with a cut-off date of 26 October 2026. All monetary amounts are in US dollars unless otherwise stated; forecasts reflect the information available when they were published.*

A loaded ship can leave Australia while its cargo remains on the producer’s books. That possibility gives the commercial dispute between Fortescue and China Mineral Resources Group, or CMRG, a tangible form. Between July and September 2026, the Australian group shipped **46.8 million [wet tonnes](/en/glossary/wmt/)** and sold **42.9 million**, with both figures reported on a 100% basis for the operations. It attributes the gap to its continuing negotiations with the Chinese buyer. Its Shanghai trading subsidiary holds inventories at Chinese ports, allowing the sea voyage and the sale to follow separate timetables. [1](#source-01)

Those figures had been public since 8 October. The report released on the 22nd adds a warning: Fortescue expects inventories to rise by a further **4–5 million tonnes in October**, and says its operations could be affected if the talks remain unresolved at year-end. The company retains its targets for the financial year ending in June 2027, subject to those negotiations. As of 26 October, the additional October inventory remains a forecast; the December deadline describes a conditional operational risk. [1](#source-01) [2](#source-02)

The contest also takes place in the interval between incurring an expense and completing a sale. Mining, processing and moving ore all require money. When selling takes longer, some of that money remains tied up in the material. The ore may be physically close to its eventual user while the producer continues to bear the financial burden of getting it there. Understanding what Beijing can obtain requires following that interval, then examining the alternatives available to each side.

## A buyer that brings orders together

CMRG is a Chinese state-owned company whose establishment was announced in July 2022 by SASAC, the body overseeing the central government’s industrial holdings. Its growing role in iron ore procurement introduces an intermediary able to combine several steelmakers’ negotiations with the major producers. Australia’s Department of Industry sees its expanding presence as a potential change in the market’s structure and the way prices are formed. [5](#source-05) [18](#source-18)

The economic attraction lies in coordination. Mills negotiating separately may accept different terms, place orders at different times or compete for an available cargo. A negotiator representing several mills can offer a larger volume of business in exchange for better terms. Bringing those decisions together changes the alternatives available across the negotiating table. It can also make a collective decision to wait more credible: the supplier then has fewer opportunities to find another nearby customer willing to step in immediately.

That organisation is still taking shape. In a report published on 6 August 2026, Reuters said CMRG had asked some steel mills to suspend their discussions with Rio Tinto. According to the sources it interviewed, the intervention was also intended to persuade mills to delegate negotiating rights they still retained. The scope of the buying group was itself part of the commercial contest. This account rests on market sources cited by the news agency. [6](#source-06)

Purchasing remains partly centralised within an industry of many buyers. For Fortescue, that concentration is already sufficient to have consequences: the company explicitly links delayed sales and rising inventories to its talks with CMRG. The public documents reviewed provide insufficient evidence to establish the precise share of volumes currently controlled by the Chinese group. [1](#source-01) [6](#source-06)

BHP’s experience shows that these negotiations can reach a conclusion. On 22 April, the producer announced that it had completed its iron ore sales contract negotiations with CMRG. Its statement confirms the outcome of the talks, while the specific concessions remain confidential. The precedent establishes the possibility of an agreement; the terms sought from Fortescue belong to a separate negotiation. Assessing the commercial value of an agreement would require knowing the terms each side accepted. [7](#source-07)

On 21 October, Reuters also reported a new supply agreement between Rio Tinto and CMRG, citing people familiar with the matter. The agency said some mills had been told about it verbally. Allocation volumes remained unclear in the report, and commercial terms remain private. [19](#source-19)

## The ore travels; the financing stays

Fortescue’s annual accounts set out a useful distinction. Under its usual seaborne sales arrangements, control and title generally pass at the date of the bill of lading, the document associated with loading the cargo. For sales from port inventories, they generally pass when the customer collects the material. Contractual arrangements determine when the company recognises a sale. The timing of cash collection depends on the agreed payment terms. Loading, delivery, recognition of revenue and payment can occur at different moments, their relationship determined by the contract governing the cargo. [4](#source-04)

Trading from Chinese ports brings inventories closer to their users. Customers can buy locally and arrange collection. In return, the producer may carry more ore within its own commercial network. Fortescue explains this beneath its product table: inventories held by its Chinese subsidiary create timing differences between shipments and sales. [1](#source-01)

The **3.9 million tonne** gap is the difference between the two quarterly flows. During the 22 October conference call, management described an inventory build of roughly **4 million tonnes** over the September quarter. That figure measures an increase; establishing the total stock would also require its opening level. It helps explain the movement during the quarter, while leaving the full quantity already in the system undisclosed. Executives also specified that inventories were held in both China and Australia. [1](#source-01) [3](#source-03)

Keeping material close to the customer may help Fortescue sell it once the dispute is resolved. The immediate cost is that mining and part of the logistics chain have already consumed resources. A longer cycle can increase the [working capital](/en/glossary/bfr/) required. Put plainly, the company may have to finance its activity for longer before recovering the proceeds. Fortescue says the increase in working capital, including inventories, weighed on operating cash flow. [1](#source-01) [3](#source-03)

This is how waiting becomes a source of bargaining power. A buyer with access to other supplies can defer an order and leave its supplier financing the delay. The supplier can offer a concession, seek other customers, move inventories or slow certain spending. Each response changes either the cost of waiting or the prospect of finding another outlet for the material. How effectively the pressure works depends on how long each side can sustain its position. This economic interpretation follows from the mechanism observed; the confidential terms proposed by CMRG remain unknown.

## Cash absorbs several pressures

Fortescue’s cash balance fell from **$5.1 billion on 30 June** to **$3.2 billion on 30 September**. Several flows contributed to that movement. During the quarter, the company paid a **$1 billion dividend** and spent **$916 million on investments and capital expenditure**. The report also identifies the adverse working capital effect, without providing a numerical reconciliation that would isolate the cash cost of the CMRG negotiation. [1](#source-01)

That distinction matters when interpreting the decline in cash. A distribution to shareholders, an equipment investment and a build-up of ore put money to different uses. Some may reflect decisions made before the commercial dispute. The inventory increase adds to those commitments and reduces the flexibility available while the company waits for an agreement.

Executives say they are reviewing the operating expenditure and investments within their control, looking at scope, timing and sequencing. By adjusting these, management can preserve cash while the timing of sales remains uncertain. The industrial consequences will depend on which projects are actually deferred and how long the impasse lasts. [3](#source-03)

The negotiation is also taking place as the business becomes more expensive to operate. Hematite **[C1](/en/glossary/c1/) operating costs reached $21.98 per wet tonne sold**, against $18.17 a year earlier, an increase of **21% year on year**. The rise from the preceding quarter was **13%**. Fortescue cites inflationary pressures and lower supply-chain volumes, and puts the year-on-year impact of higher diesel prices at approximately **$1.40 per wet tonne**. Scheduled port maintenance shutdowns also affected the quarter’s shipments. [1](#source-01)

Meanwhile, the realised hematite price fell from **$88.86 to $79.79 per [dry tonne](/en/glossary/dmt/)** over the year. The two trends help explain why the length of the dispute matters. The seller faces higher unit operating costs while receiving less revenue for each dry tonne sold. [1](#source-01)

Calculating a margin would require putting the figures on a common basis. A wet tonne includes water; a dry tonne excludes it. C1 covers an operating cost base to which freight and royalties, among other items, must be added to arrive at a delivered cost. The annual accounts set out that reconciliation. Both the quantity used as the denominator and the expenses included in the cost measure have to match the comparison being made. Rising C1 costs, a lower realised price and the financing of inventories therefore describe complementary pressures, each with its own mechanism and units. [4](#source-04)

## Burden requirements shape cargo choices

Iron ore comes with a particular composition, physical form and industrial behaviour. A buyer seeking to replace a cargo has to consider what its plant can process and the blend it is preparing. Fine ores are commonly agglomerated before entering a blast furnace; other burden materials include sized lump ore and pellets. The strength of that burden matters because gases need to pass through it. Nippon Steel describes how the breakdown of sinter can impair gas flow and productivity. [12](#source-12)

Iron content provides a starting point. To **contain one tonne of iron**, the arithmetic requires approximately **1.724 tonnes of dry ore at 58%**, **1.639 tonnes at 61%** or **1.493 tonnes at 67%**. This calculation describes the mass of material containing the same quantity of iron, before processing losses and transformations. A tonne of contained iron is therefore distinct from a tonne of steel produced. The remaining mass includes the oxygen bound to iron in oxides, alongside the ore’s other constituents.

Blending expands the steelmaker’s options. In a purely arithmetic example, two dry tonnes at 58% and one tonne at 67% produce three tonnes at 61%. A lower-grade cargo can thus have a place alongside a richer product. The calculation describes the combined iron content; it is the starting point for considering how those materials might work together. Turning it into an industrial recipe also requires examining particle size, impurities and mineralogy.

Experimental work by CSIRO and BHP researchers shows why those details matter: the mineral supplying the alumina affects the formation of binding phases during sintering. Materials with similar overall compositions can behave differently at this stage. Understanding the chemical analysis requires examining how the minerals react during processing. An ore’s value depends on its role in the blend and the result the plant is seeking. [11](#source-11)

That variety is reflected in Fortescue’s published prices. In the September quarter, hematite earned an average **$79.79 per dry tonne**, equivalent to **82%** of the group’s benchmark. Iron Bridge concentrate earned **$111.09**, or **115%** of the same index. These ratios compare different products and average revenues with a common reference. They incorporate the effects of product mix and commercial terms. Identifying a discount specifically associated with the CMRG dispute would require information beyond the public report. [1](#source-01)

The reference itself needs a date. Since **2 January 2026**, Platts IODEX has been based on **61% iron content**, following a change from its previous 62% specification and revisions to several impurity specifications. Platts explains the change by reference to the evolving quality of Australian ores informing its assessment. The index represents a market value for fines delivered to China, expressed in dollars per dry tonne and including freight to the reference delivery point, on a [CFR](/en/glossary/cfr/) Qingdao basis. [8](#source-08) [9](#source-09)

Changes in Fortescue’s portfolio provide further context. As early as October 2025, the group announced the gradual withdrawal of West Pilbara Fines and the introduction of a 55% iron product from the 2027 financial year. A year later, its quarterly table reports West Pilbara Fines shipments as 0.0 million tonnes and records the arrival of Fortune Fines. This follows a change in product mix announced before the current episode. Interpreting it requires considering the life of the mines alongside the commercial negotiation. [10](#source-10) [1](#source-01)

## Beijing has alternatives, within physical limits

China’s industrial backdrop gives buyers room to be patient. Between January and September 2026, iron ore and concentrate imports reached **954.97 million tonnes**, up **4.1% year on year**, according to customs figures reproduced by Mysteel. Over the same months, the National Bureau of Statistics recorded **724.77 million tonnes of crude steel**, down **2.9%**. Worldsteel reported the same decline in its 23 October release. [13](#source-13) [14](#source-14) [15](#source-15)

Those movements describe different stages of the industrial system. Imports feed inventories and the range of blends available, while steel production also depends on domestic ore, scrap and the processes used. The divergence in their growth rates invites closer examination of storage and the composition of supply. Separate inventory data are needed to establish how much additional material was stored and where it was held. Australia’s September report, based on information available up to 20 September, describes plentiful global supply, high Chinese port inventories and weak steelmaking margins ; conditions that give buyers room to press for better terms. [18](#source-18)

The alternatives remain concentrated. Customs figures reported by Wenhua Finance and republished by SMM attribute **759.051 million tonnes to Australia** and **277.416 million to Brazil** in 2025, out of **1,258.709 million tonnes imported**. Together, those two origins account for approximately **82.3%** of the total. That structure explains the interdependence: China is an enormous market, and much of its supply comes from two countries. [16](#source-16)

A buyer can target a product or a supplier while continuing to source from others. The exercise becomes more demanding when larger volumes are involved, or when a particular quality is needed in an industrial blend. Replacing an entire stream of supply requires those alternatives to work repeatedly and at scale. For the producer, finding other outlets requires customers able to absorb the quantities concerned on acceptable freight and quality terms. Fortescue’s executives say they are working on their marketing channels; the capacity actually available to replace delayed sales remains private. [3](#source-03)

Simandou, in Guinea, is gradually expanding supply options. Rio Tinto’s report published on 14 October helps establish the timetable. For SimFer, whose reported production volumes are measured in wet tonnes, the group says it reached an annualised production rate of around **10 million tonnes per year** during the third quarter. It retains a target of **60 million dry tonnes per year in the second half of 2028**. The latter is a ramp-up target, covering SimFer on a 100% basis within the larger Simandou development. [17](#source-17)

The new origin adds competition, with its impact dependent on the combined functioning of the mine, railway, port and commercial chain in China. Rio, too, distinguishes tonnes produced, shipped and sold. For SimFer in the third quarter, **2.2 million wet tonnes were shipped** and **1.3 million sold**. That gap, observed during a ramp-up, is a reminder that separate physical and commercial timetables are part of how this market operates. [17](#source-17)

## Production decisions before year-end

Fortescue’s annual shipment target remains **197–207 million tonnes** for the 2027 financial year, including 11–14 million tonnes from Iron Bridge. It is a shipment forecast, conditional on the CMRG negotiations. The tonnes leaving the logistics system and those actually sold will therefore need to be tracked together. Maintaining the shipment target leaves open the question of how quickly the material moving through that system will turn into recognised sales and cash. [1](#source-01)

A recovery in sales could begin by clearing inventories already close to customers. The effect on cash would then depend on the pace of customer collections and payments. A slower resolution would extend the period of inventory financing and the need to make choices about spending. Management has raised the possibility of production adjustments if talks remain unresolved at year-end, without publishing a volume of potential cuts. During the conference call, it also declined to extend October’s expected inventory build mechanically into subsequent months. [1](#source-01) [3](#source-03)

For CMRG, the quality of an eventual agreement would be measured by the terms secured, their durability and continuity of supply to the mills it represents. For Fortescue, it would be measured by revenue per product, the sale of inventories and the ability to finance its operations. Those measures would connect the outcome at the negotiating table to the performance of the commercial relationship. A signature would provide the first piece of information; the effects would then emerge in those results.

Fortescue’s case makes a usually discreet commercial power visible: deciding when a material finds a buyer. Combining purchasing decisions gives that choice greater weight. The counterweights remain concrete: the qualities required by blast furnaces, volumes available from other suppliers and the producer’s ability to finance the wait. Subsequent reports will have to show how those constraints translate into sales, inventories and cash receipts.

## Limitations

This investigation draws on public documents available up to 26 October 2026. Negotiating terms, Fortescue’s detailed inventories by location and quality, payment schedules and the amount of cash tied up specifically by the dispute remain private. The observations about bargaining power analyse mechanisms and should be distinguished from statements attributed to the companies.

The September customs page was identified in search results for the official site; its figures were read in Mysteel’s reproduction. The 2025 breakdown by origin comes from customs figures reported by Wenhua Finance and republished by SMM, whose article was read in full. This provides secondary access to the customs statistics; the original country-level table was unavailable for direct inspection. The grade ratios are illustrative calculations on a dry-mass basis. The October, financial-year 2027 and SimFer forecasts each retain their own dates and scope.
