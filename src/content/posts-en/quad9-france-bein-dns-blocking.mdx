Mirra Andreeva beat Elina Svitolina at Indian Wells to close the women’s tennis season. Nine days later, at 9 a.m. Paris time on 24 November, Quad9 scheduled a shutdown of its public DNS service for requests it identified as coming from France. The Swiss foundation made that decision after a Paris court ordered it to block names used by unauthorised streams of matches covered by beIN Sports France’s rights. For anyone whose device relied on Quad9 to find a website or a mail server, a dispute over tennis could suddenly affect an ordinary connection. [S01](#source-S01) [S04](#source-S04) [S05](#source-S05) [S18](#source-S18)

Before a browser can connect to a site, it usually needs to find the address associated with its name. A [DNS resolver](/en/glossary/#dns) looks up that address and returns it, or retrieves an answer it already has in its cache. Quad9 offers a version that also filters domains it identifies as security threats, such as those used for phishing. When the resolver refuses every request from a particular network, the sites and services are still online. A device that relies on Quad9 may simply be unable to find them. [S04](#source-S04) [S06](#source-S06)

## A tennis broadcaster takes on the internet’s address book

In January, beIN Sports France sued several DNS providers, including Google and Quad9. The broadcaster held exclusive French rights to covered matches on the WTA circuit, under a chain of licences starting with WTA Ventures Operation. It brought evidence of unauthorised streams to the Paris Judicial Court. The court found serious and repeated infringements and ordered blocking on 13 May. The list began with six domain names and associated subdomains. The order extended beyond mainland France to much of the overseas territory, with exceptions including New Caledonia, Wallis and Futuna, and French Polynesia. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

Six names were a starting point. The list could grow over the season under [dynamic blocking](/en/glossary/#dynamic-blocking). When beIN identified another service covered by the ruling, it could pass the identifiers to Arcom, the French media regulator. Arcom could notify the providers once its authorised officers had verified the requisite conditions. If legitimate services were caught in the process, providers could ask the judge to lift the measure. The judgment also required beIN to flag names it learned had gone inactive or changed purpose. The court declined to impose a daily penalty at that stage. [S01](#source-S01) [S08](#source-S08) [S17](#source-S17)

## The court orders a daily penalty

By September, beIN was back in court. It sought €10,000 per day of delay for each domain or subdomain still reachable through Quad9. BeIN submitted records which, it said, showed some of the names had been blocked. Quad9 disputed that account. Its technology chief submitted a statement saying the foundation had put none of the disputed names under a block by 25 September. [S02](#source-S02)

The judge ruled on 21 October that Quad9 had a duty to make reasonable efforts and, by its own account, had taken no steps to implement the order. The judge found that Quad9 had failed to establish that geographically limited blocking was impossible and pointed to the foundation’s existing worldwide security filtering. Quad9 had appealed the May ruling, which remained enforceable while the appeal was pending. [S02](#source-S02)

The court set a provisional penalty of €1,000 per day of delay overall from 21 October until the end of the blocking measures. The ruling cited 14 November as the expected end date. The WTA Finals actually ended on 15 November, and the May ruling required beIN to notify providers of any change in the date. The court reserved the power to determine the amount ultimately due. Quad9 put its exposure at €24,000 before legal costs and said about 20 other cases were pending against it in France. The amount finally assessed or paid remains unverified. ## Quad9 draws a border using IP addresses

Quad9 announced its departure the day after the October ruling. It had argued that it could not reliably identify which users were within the order’s reach. A public IP address can be associated with a country, but the location of a network’s exit point can differ from that of the people using it. Quad9’s privacy policy says that in normal operation it uses the source address temporarily to send the answer and retains aggregated statistics. Its public service does not require customers to register. [S03](#source-S03) [S04](#source-S04) [S07](#source-S07)

The foundation chose to use IP geolocation for the withdrawal anyway. It says the same data could be used to refuse answers selectively for the disputed domains. Its objection is to using that imperfect boundary to filter content on the orders of a court, particularly when requests from outside France could be affected. The withdrawal accepts the same kind of error with broader consequences for the queries caught by it. A request from abroad can be classified as French and refused for any name. A request from France can be classified as foreign and answered. Quad9 says it does not know how often either happens. A different version of this infrastructure problem came up in the French debate over [backdoors in encrypted messaging](/en/analysis/france-encryption-backdoor-safeguard/). A decision aimed at a particular case can change a service for users with no connection to it. [S04](#source-S04)

This is where the decision reaches the people who had nothing to do with tennis. An organisation relying on Quad9 has to find which devices and applications use it and, if it switches providers, determine whether the replacement offers comparable security filtering and privacy. Quad9 published examples of networks from which it had received traffic. That shows where it observed requests, without telling us which organisations or people actually lost service on 24 November. [S03](#source-S03) [S04](#source-S04)

## From a list of names to blocking during a match

The case landed during an expansion of sporting rights enforcement. Arcom’s annual report counts 772 sports services notified for blocking in 2022 and 6,496 in 2025, up 71% from 2024. The report says that since January 2025, 81% of blocking requests sent to internet access providers had also gone to alternative DNS providers. Those figures describe the reach of the procedure. Arcom separately estimates that the audience for illicit sports and cultural services fell from 11.7 million internet users in 2021 to 7.7 million in 2025. That broader trend cannot isolate the effect of adding DNS resolvers to the process. [S08](#source-S08) [S09](#source-S09)

<Quad9Figure lang="en" kind="arcom" />

The August 2026 sports law brought a new tool for live events. If a judge expressly authorises it, an automated system under Arcom’s control can send identifiers for newly discovered services to the intermediaries named in the order. Arcom set the rules on 4 November, including prior approval of the rights holder’s detection method and checks to protect legitimate services. On 26 November, the French professional football league said it had obtained a separate order on 20 November to use the new framework for Ligue 1 and Ligue 2, with deployment expected in the following weeks. The tennis order dates to May and followed the earlier procedure. [S01](#source-S01) [S10](#source-S10) [S11](#source-S11) [S12](#source-S12)

The European Court of Justice has held in a case concerning an internet access provider that a blocking measure can be permissible when it seriously discourages unlawful access while protecting lawful access. ICANN’s security advisory committee recommends review procedures to limit collateral damage and clear error messages when a resolver blocks a name. For a broadcaster, a block delivered after the final point can come too late. For an internet user, the cost of a mistaken block begins the moment an ordinary service stops working. The more central DNS becomes to enforcement, the more important it is to measure both outcomes. [S13](#source-S13) [S14](#source-S14)

**Reporting and limits.** This investigation uses the published anonymised May judgment, the complete October ruling released by Quad9, Arcom’s figures and regulatory texts, and statements by the foundation and the league. We did not measure the reach of Quad9’s withdrawal on French networks or identify an independent estimate of the effect of these specific blocks on piracy.
