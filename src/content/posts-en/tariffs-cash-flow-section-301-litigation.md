A shipment with a customs value of $1 million can create an extra $125,000 funding requirement before it generates any customer cash. In the fictional business we follow here, the goods are sold two months after the tariff is paid. The customer pays a month later. For ninety days, the importer must find the money elsewhere. Commercial deadlines keep running while the court examines the challenge.

On September 29, the Liberty Justice Center, counsel for some of the plaintiffs, announced oral argument on September 30, 2026 in the consolidated *In Re: Section 301 Forced Labor Cases*. The challenges concern July’s tariffs linked to trading partners’ policies on forced-labour imports. For importers, the funding clock starts with the duty payment. [Announcement by plaintiffs’ representatives](https://libertyjusticecenter.org/mediaalert/court-of-international-trade-to-hear-challenge-to-sweeping-section-301-tariffs/).

This article follows the dollars between those stages. The official documents and court positions are reviewed as of September 30; the business, its prices and its funding terms are illustrative assumptions. The litigation remains unresolved in the sources reviewed.

## The headline rate is only part of the bill

On July 23, the Office of the US Trade Representative announced action under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 concerning 60 economies. It justified the action by what it considered inadequate prohibitions on forced-labour imports or inadequate enforcement of those prohibitions. The measure addresses foreign policies, giving it a reach beyond the identification of individual shipments. [USTR](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/july/ustr-takes-action-forced-labor-section-301-investigations)

The official notice sets July 24, 2026 as the effective date, subject to exceptions. Its 10% and 12.5% rates involve different calculations. For some groups, the tariff is added to the ordinary duty. For affected goods from the European Union and Taiwan, it tops that duty up to 10%. For Japan, Korea and Switzerland, the threshold is 12.5%. Where the ordinary duty already meets the threshold, this additional component is zero. [USTR, pp.2 and 9](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Press/Releases/2026/FLIP%20301%20Investigation%20Final%20Action%20FRN%207-23-26%20FINAL.pdf)

Consider a product with a $1 million customs value and a hypothetical 4% ordinary duty, solely to illustrate the schedule. An additive 10% surcharge adds $100,000 to the existing $40,000 bill. A top-up to 10% adds $60,000. The cash difference is $40,000 on the same import value. This calculation isolates the two duties and assumes no exemption or other levy.

The product classification, origin and exclusions determine the actual application. The USTR’s fact sheet includes an exclusion for articles subject to Section 232 tariffs. An origin-based headline rate therefore cannot simply be applied across an entire commercial invoice without examining its products. [USTR](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/fact-sheets/2026/july/fact-sheet-ustr-section-301-action-response-failure-60-economies-ban-imports-produced-forced-labor)

A separate legal boundary also remains: the United States prohibits certain goods made with forced labour under Section 307. Paying a tariff does not make prohibited goods admissible. Conversely, being covered by July’s tariff action does not establish that the taxed product itself was made with forced labour. [US Customs and Border Protection](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/forced-labor/frequently-asked-questions) [USTR](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/july/ustr-takes-action-forced-labor-section-301-investigations)

## The first payer has a bank account

The *importer of record*, the party formally responsible for the import entry, remains responsible for documentation and duties even when using a customs broker. That is the legal starting point. The final economic burden will also depend on prices negotiated with the supplier and customers. That distribution is called [tax incidence](/en/glossary/incidence-fiscale/). [US Customs and Border Protection](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/basic-import-export/importer-exporter-tips)

The timing of payment needs care too. US law provides for estimated duty deposits and different payment arrangements, including periodic mechanisms. A customs payment is not necessarily a debit at the moment a vessel arrives. Our model deliberately chooses a payment date, D0, and measures the remaining intervals from that debit. [US Code / Cornell Legal Information Institute, §1505(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1505)

The fictional importer receives a shipment with a customs value of $1 million, subject to an additive 12.5% surcharge. The ordinary duty is assumed to be zero in this cash-flow model, unlike the separate 4% illustration in the first figure. The additional $125,000 is deposited at D0. The customer is invoiced at D60 and pays at D90.

The tariff creates a **working-capital requirement**: money tied up between an outgoing payment and the corresponding customer receipt. Financing the $125,000 for ninety days at an assumed annual rate of 9% costs about **$2,774** in simple interest, using a 365-day year. This is only the cost of financing the additional duty. Inventory, freight, fees and other expenses have their own funding requirements.

Adding the full $125,000 to the customer’s invoice allows the advance to be recovered at D90 in this example. Until then, the importer uses a credit line or its own cash. A customer deposit, a different payment schedule or faster inventory turnover would shorten the requirement. The selling price and the date on which it becomes cash perform different jobs.

## Three shipments before the first cash comes back

The problem changes scale when deliveries keep arriving. Retain the assumptions and introduce six shipments, thirty days apart. Each $125,000 duty payment is recovered from the customer ninety days later. Full pass-through and unchanged volumes are assumed specifically to examine a business that preserves its dollar contribution from trading.

At D0, the importer advances $125,000. By D30, the outstanding amount is $250,000. At D60, it reaches $375,000. The first customer receipt arrives at D90, alongside the duty payment on the fourth shipment: the two flows offset each other, leaving the funding requirement at $375,000. It starts falling only after new shipments stop.

An additional facility capped at $300,000 would therefore leave a **$75,000 funding gap at D60**. The simulation does not imply insolvency. The company could inject equity, negotiate a deposit, arrange another facility or change its orders. It pinpoints where a commercial plan needs a matching financial resource.

Across the six shipments, additional duty payments total $750,000. The maximum amount tied up at one time is $375,000. The first figure is a cumulative flow; the second is a stock. Adding them would produce a meaningless measure. The model nets receipts and payments falling on the same day and excludes any intraday funding needs.

If every advance is financed at the same assumed 9% rate, including the amount above the illustrated credit limit, the total cost is approximately $16,644. That [carrying cost](/en/glossary/cout-de-portage/) rises when customers pay later, inventory turns more slowly or credit becomes more expensive. The accompanying data allow these assumptions to be changed without altering the mechanism.

## How the tariff moves through the margin

Suppose the same shipment generated $1.5 million of revenue against $1.2 million of variable costs, including the goods, before the surcharge. It contributed $300,000 before fixed overhead and financing, or 20% of revenue. This is a simplified contribution calculation, not reported net income.

With the selling price unchanged, the new duty reduces that contribution to $175,000. It falls **41.7%**, although the tariff is 12.5% of customs value. Those percentages have different denominators: $1 million for the duty and $300,000 for the original contribution.

Adding exactly $125,000 to sales raises revenue to $1.625 million and restores the $300,000 contribution. At unchanged quantity, the selling price has risen **8.33%**, while contribution now equals 18.46% of sales. Preserving a 20% contribution ratio would require revenue of $1.65625 million, a 10.42% price increase in this arithmetic.

These options explain the choices rather than predict them. Each assumes the customer buys the same quantity. Tougher competition may constrain the price; higher prices may reduce sales; a supplier may agree to a discount. The burden and the resources available to finance the next shipment change with each adjustment.

Research published by New York Fed economists on February 12, 2026 estimated that almost 90% of the burden of the **2025** tariffs it studied was borne in the United States. That finding covers US firms and consumers, using data through November 2025. It measures neither retail-price pass-through alone nor the incidence of July 2026’s action. [Federal Reserve Bank of New York](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/02/who-is-paying-for-the-2025-u-s-tariffs/)

## The ruling may change where the money ends up

The litigation concerns the legal basis and implementation of the administrative action. Among other arguments, the plaintiffs challenge the justification for decisions across the economies concerned. The government maintains that the statutory conditions were satisfied and defends the USTR’s discretion. These are the parties’ positions. [Court filing / plaintiffs](https://libertyjusticecenter.org/wp-content/uploads/Plaintiffs-Motion-for-Judgement.pdf) [US Department of Justice / court filing](https://libertyjusticecenter.org/wp-content/uploads/Defendants-Response-in-Opp-to-Motion-for-Judgment.pdf)

For cash planning, the requested remedies are especially important. Plaintiffs seek vacatur and refunds with interest. The government argues, in the alternative, for *remand without vacatur*: returning the matter to the agency for reconsideration or further explanation while leaving the challenged action in force. It also disputes the reach of relief benefiting non-parties. [Court filing / plaintiffs, p.33](https://libertyjusticecenter.org/wp-content/uploads/002-Burlap-and-Barrel-v.-Greer-Compl-2026-07-24.pdf) [US Department of Justice / court filing, pp.64–69](https://libertyjusticecenter.org/wp-content/uploads/Defendants-Response-in-Opp-to-Motion-for-Judgment.pdf)

A ruling that criticises an explanation could therefore have different consequences from one that sets the action aside. For an importer, the scope of relief matters as much as the court’s reasoning. Does it halt future collection? Which past entries does it cover? Who can obtain restitution, and through what process?

As of September 30, the reviewed documents do not establish a general entitlement to refunds of July’s duties. Quantifying a potential claim and scheduling a receipt require the applicable ruling, its beneficiaries and processing arrangements. An expected litigation recovery cannot, by itself, finance a shipment.

## IEEPA provides an administrative reference point

A separate 2026 dispute illustrates the distance between a court instruction and a payment system. It concerns tariffs imposed under IEEPA, a different statutory basis. In its April 13 bulletin, US Customs and Border Protection explained that an order issued on March 4 was followed on March 6 by suspension of the requirement for immediate compliance, allowing time to develop an automated tool. [US Customs and Border Protection](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCBP/bulletins/412cc7f)

The tool’s first phase, CAPE, became available on April 20. That launch was a dated administrative event, separate from the judicial decision preceding it. [US Customs and Border Protection](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCBP/bulletins/413a9f2)

For certain eligible entries, CBP indicated **60–90 days after acceptance of a valid declaration**, including 45 days of review followed by payment processing. Some categories were outside that timetable, and further compliance review could extend it. This was operational guidance published in April, not a measurement of actual turnaround times. [US Customs and Border Protection](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCBP/bulletins/412cc7f)

The precedent helps frame the question: which event starts the clock? A judgment, a system launch, submission, acceptance, final determination of duties and a transfer can occur on different dates. No CAPE deadline is imported here into a possible Section 301 refund.

US law provides for interest on certain excess deposits that are refunded. The applicable framework governs its treatment. The 9% bank rate in our model is not a customs-refund interest rate. A refund may offset some carrying costs, but the simulation cannot determine the final net amount. [US Code / Cornell Legal Information Institute, §1505(b)–(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1505)

## The next order is placed before the case ends

The more persistent difficulty lies in decisions that must be made now. An importer can absorb the tariff, raise prices, change volumes, request a deposit or look for another supplier. Each changes a commercial relationship. Waiting may preserve options, but it may also cost a selling season or a production slot. Those trade-offs are specific to the business.

Federal Reserve research on trade policy uncertainty connects unexpected increases in uncertainty with lower investment and activity in the data and models studied. The historical result explains a mechanism; it is not a numerical forecast for this litigation. [Federal Reserve Board](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/ifdp/the-economic-effects-of-trade-policy-uncertainty.htm)

The wider economic accounting also matters. A duty payment becomes public revenue. The sum collected is therefore not, on its own, a measure of wealth destroyed across the economy. This article examines the importer’s cash: the levy, its funding, the scope to pass it on and the commercial decisions it triggers. Aggregate effects would require a different model incorporating domestic producers, public spending and demand responses.

There are practical offsets. Exempt merchandise, advance customer payment or a company with ample liquidity can greatly reduce the constraint. Even in the last case, cash committed to duties has alternative uses. Exposure varies with the balance sheet, contracts and inventory cycle.

To track the risk without inventing a future refund, the useful unit remains a documented import entry: product, origin, customs value, duty deposited, payment date and administrative status. Sales and customer receipts complete the picture. These records allow a potentially affected amount to be reconciled with cash actually tied up. The choice of legal remedy or filing requires case-specific advice.

In our example, a business that passes through the entire duty and preserves a $300,000 contribution per shipment must still advance as much as $375,000 of additional tariffs. The court case may change where that money ultimately ends up. Its bank and suppliers need to know how the trading cycle will be financed today.
