<p><strong>The war on ordinary machines · Part 7 of 7</strong></p>

<p class="standfirst">Backups, spare equipment and an on-call contract only preserve operating hours when people can put them to work. The final part of this series examines recovery capacity, shared dependencies and the economics of keeping a usable fallback ready.</p>

On 26 March 2019, a week after the cyberattack on aluminium producer Norsk Hydro, a single company update described two very different industrial situations. For three business units within Extruded Solutions, Hydro reported an aggregate production rate of 70–80%, without detailing the denominator. A fourth, Building Systems, remained almost at a standstill. Elsewhere, production was running normally, sometimes with more manual work. Fallback capacity already had a geography, a workforce and a limit. [1](#source-01)

On the day of the attack, 19 March, Hydro had announced that it was isolating its operations and switching to manual procedures wherever possible. It also reported stoppages caused by an inability to connect to production systems. The contrast between operations raises a practical question behind the broad promise of resilience: which functions remain available when the usual computing environment disappears? [2](#source-02)

This final instalment examines the resources that allow a business to keep going. The earlier articles followed machine commands, remote access, evidence, losses and regulation. The remaining question is how to fund a fallback that can actually be used. Public documents explain the mechanisms. An entirely hypothetical numerical case then shows how the number of available recovery teams changes the cost of an outage. Its inputs make the arithmetic inspectable; they are neither market prices nor an attack forecast.

## Start with the function that needs to survive

In its retrospective account, updated on 15 May 2024, Hydro puts the total cost of the attack at approximately **NOK 800 million**. It describes labour-intensive workarounds. This remains the affected company’s estimate for one event at its own scale. The figure conveys the size of the disruption; the separate contributions of architecture, staff and manual operation to avoided losses remain unknown. [3](#source-03)

The story could easily become an argument for going back to paper. A more useful investigation asks what makes temporary operation feasible. For a refrigerated warehouse, for example, that would mean examining temperature control, monitoring, decisions about stock and available staff together. This is an analytical example, not a reconstruction of an attacked installation or advice on permissible temperatures.

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) describes the target as [*minimum viable operations*](/en/glossary/mvo/): a level at which an organisation can operate safely, meet its obligations and retain the trust of partners. It is a definition built around the service being delivered. An application may be accessible again while the operation that depends on it remains too fragile to resume. [4](#source-04)

An operator’s first useful expenditure might therefore fund the work of specifying which function must continue, for how long, using which resources, and under what conditions it must be stopped. Those answers give the budget a purpose. They help distinguish the case for stronger protection, local autonomy or faster restoration.

The industrial security guide from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) follows this logic by calling for recovery objectives to be set before selecting the means, including spare inventory where those objectives justify it. This article uses the final September 2023 edition. A notice dated 21 September 2026 points to an initial Revision 4 draft, with comments due by 30 November. Its draft status is kept separate. [5](#source-05) [6](#source-06)

## Two devices can share one point of failure

[Redundancy](/en/glossary/redondance/) provides an additional resource capable of taking over a function. An isolated hardware failure and the compromise of a shared administrative route test that arrangement in different ways. NIST explicitly observes that redundancy in supervisory control systems may be insufficient against a malicious attack. [5](#source-05)

Consider a hypothetical arrangement in which two control devices are administered through the same account and environment. Duplicating the devices leaves that shared dependency in place. Assessing the fallback requires examining what would happen to both devices if that environment were lost or compromised, then identifying which functions would survive. The diagram below asks that question. It represents no particular product or victim.

Separating dependencies brings costs of its own. Distinct access routes need management, configurations need to remain consistent, and staff need to understand the recovery options. Technical diversity can make maintenance harder. The investment decision should identify which common causes of failure it addresses and how much additional work the design creates.

Guidance published by the NCSC on 1 October 2026 connects limiting the impact of compromise with network segmentation, protected backups and control of administrative privileges. These measures describe ways to contain damage. Their effectiveness in a particular installation still needs local evidence. [7](#source-07)

<MachineResilienceFigure lang="en" kind="dependencies" />

Isolation poses a related test. A plant may rely on a remote service for a function whose loss has physical consequences. The NCSC recommends an isolation plan linked to [service continuity](/en/glossary/continuite-de-service/), tested and designed for multi-site situations, with suppliers included in the planning. [8](#source-08)

The investment can buy an operational choice: suspend a compromised connection while retaining necessary functions, or arrange a controlled shutdown. The business case can specify the resources needed to preserve that choice and the conditions for using them. Its value depends on the extent of disruption it can contain and the operating conditions it preserves, rather than the number of devices installed.

## Recovery has a capacity limit

Recommendation R76 in the November 2025 industrial security guide from ANSSI, France’s cybersecurity agency, calls for recovery priorities to be agreed with management. It recognises that several sites may be affected together and may have different levels of criticality. This class C1 recommendation exposes a central constraint: restoration needs can arrive simultaneously, while available resources have to be allocated. [9](#source-09)

To make that constraint visible, take **six fictional sites**, each losing the same function. The safety conditions required for intervention are assumed to have been met. Two teams become available two hours after the interruption begins. Each restoration job, including validation, takes four hours. Parts and configurations are ready, sites have equal priority, and travel between jobs takes no time in the model. These assumptions deliberately simplify the problem.

With two teams, two sites are restored at hour six, another two at hour ten, and the last pair at hour fourteen. Adding those durations gives **60 site-hours of unavailability**. One hour without the function at one site is one site-hour. This unit measures cumulative interruption; the elapsed time until the last site is restored is fourteen hours.

A third team, available at the same time and equally productive, restores three sites at hour six and three at hour ten. The total falls to **48 site-hours**. The improvement is twelve site-hours. The first restoration still occurs at hour six, while the final restoration moves from hour fourteen to hour ten. The extra team changes waiting time, with different benefits for sites at different positions in the queue.

<MachineResilienceFigure lang="en" kind="capacity" />

The calculation exposes a straightforward contractual question. A business paying for on-call support needs to understand which resources would be available if several customers called at once. The supplier’s customer base, priority commitments and available skills become relevant evidence. The NCSC’s continuity guidance includes suppliers and asks organisations to consider an abrupt loss of their services. [10](#source-10)

The model becomes less favourable when a prerequisite is missing. A shared investigation may block every team; only one person may be authorised to validate restoration; a component or trusted recovery environment may be unavailable. Useful capacity is then constrained by that bottleneck. Funding another team should be compared with funding the prerequisite that would allow the teams already present to work.

For a real organisation, every duration would require a measurement or a documented assumption. Sites would need individual treatment, and supplier-unavailability scenarios would need examination. A priority driven by human safety or an essential service takes precedence over the convenient ordering used here.

## Saved operating hours and the break-even threshold

Continue with this **hypothetical case only**. Suppose the arrangement providing access to the third team costs €30,000 to establish and €6,000 a year to maintain. Over five years, the incremental budget is **€60,000**. These are teaching inputs, unrelated to a quotation or pay agreement. They stand for an assumed available capability whose actual procurement and staffing would need investigation.

For the exercise, each site-hour of outage causes €2,500 of permanently lost contribution margin. This is the revenue that would have contributed to continuing fixed costs and profit after deducting the variable costs avoided. Sales merely delayed and later recovered would require a different calculation. The twelve site-hours saved therefore preserve **€30,000 per incident of the specified kind**.

Two identical incidents over five years arithmetically offset the €60,000 cost. This **conditional break-even threshold** describes two comparable events; their future frequency requires a separate estimate. Physical damage, insurance recoveries, tax and effects on other firms sit outside the model. Amounts are constant, with no inflation or discounting and zero assumed residual value. Incident-time expenditure is assumed identical under both arrangements, so it has no effect on the calculated difference.

<MachineResilienceFigure lang="en" kind="threshold" />

Sensitivity is immediate. At €1,000 of lost contribution per site-hour, the improvement is worth €12,000 per incident and the threshold rises to five events over the period. At €5,000, one event is sufficient in the model. These are three economic assumptions, not a confidence interval or industry estimate. They show why identical recovery equipment can have very different value depending on the function it supports.

Management can use the threshold to ask for the missing evidence: the value of output that would genuinely be irrecoverable, contractually available capacity, tested mobilisation time and the period over which the assumptions remain credible. Any probability introduced later needs its own basis, limitations and sensitivity analysis. A convenient round number would turn an incomplete understanding into an apparently precise return.

Financial comparison operates within the set of options compatible with safety and applicable obligations. Some spending serves protective or continuity requirements whose value extends beyond the firm’s own earnings. The model helps compare resources; qualified people still have to establish safe operating conditions.

## Maintaining capacity while it waits

Initial expenditure is the easiest part to display. A replacement component occupies a place in inventory. Its future usefulness also depends on compatibility, configuration and the ability to commission it. NIST includes installation media, licence keys and configuration information among the items needed for usable backups. The conditions in which spare equipment is stored also matter to recovery. [5](#source-05)

Someone must own that continuity of knowledge. Every system change raises a question about which recovery resources and evidence need review. Reserving human capacity similarly requires retaining skills and access to useful information. ANSSI specifically addresses the transfer of knowledge when people leave or change roles. [9](#source-09)

Recurring expenditure can maintain a readiness that is largely invisible during normal operation: training time, checks on spares, exercise preparation or review of a supplier commitment. Assessing it means linking each item of spending to the capability it keeps available. A procurement list alone would provide little information about how that capability changes over time.

The NCSC recommends keeping recovery information secure and separate from primary systems, and documenting infrastructure dependencies. It calls for clarity about incident response and system reconstruction, which may be delivered under different service arrangements. [11](#source-11)

That distinction provides a useful way to read an invoice. Is the organisation buying available expertise, reconstruction work, a parts reserve or evidence that a working combination can be restored? Several may be needed, but they deliver different outcomes. Acceptance should connect the purchased service with the function being funded and the conditions under which it was tested.

## A useful rehearsal produces bounded, dated evidence

The NCSC distinguishes tabletop exercises, which examine roles and decisions, from technical testing that reveals practical restoration difficulties. They answer complementary questions. A discussion can expose missing decision-making authority; a test can reveal that a particular step takes longer than expected. [10](#source-10)

For an industrial installation, such verification requires an authorised scope, a prior risk assessment and conditions for stopping the exercise. The approach proposed here is documentary: prepare questions with the operator, control engineers and safety specialists through the authorised process, preserving its safety and continuity requirements.

Useful evidence records the version tested, the function restored, the people and resources actually present, elapsed time and the limitations encountered. It also records favourable test conditions: parts already on site, a prepared test environment or advance notice to a supplier. These details help establish what the result can reasonably imply for a different crisis.

<MachineResilienceFigure lang="en" kind="evidence" />

Human practice needs its own place. The NCSC’s staff-preparation guidance recommends exercises and alternative communications when familiar tools are unavailable. [12](#source-12) The budget question then becomes specific: has time for that preparation been allocated, and which teams have actually received it?

The strongest argument against continually adding recovery resources is the complexity they create. An unfamiliar fallback may delay decisions; a security change may disturb the process; a second operating arrangement requires maintenance of its own. ANSSI asks organisations to ensure cyber controls preserve degraded operating modes. The NCSC also notes that containment decisions can themselves cause disruption. [9](#source-09) [13](#source-13)

A test must therefore be allowed to disappoint. If an additional intervention does little for the priority function, money can be redirected towards the delay, skill or dependency that matters more. That learning loop gives testing value even when the originally proposed solution becomes less attractive.

## Fund the capability, then check that it still exists

Across an industry, the same question applies to specialists, spare parts and shared services. Pooled capacity can lower individual preparation costs; its sizing needs to be tested against simultaneous demand. This is an inference from the mechanism examined here, not a claim to have measured a national shortage of technicians or equipment.

A buyer can ask how priorities will be set, which resources are reserved and what remains available if the provider is affected too. A funder can ask which improvement has been observed and under what conditions. An operator can document the level of service it can sustain and how long its people can sustain it. These questions connect the contract to the physical function that needs protection.

Across these seven parts, a remote command connects equipment with the people, service providers and rules that shape its operation. An incident leaves evidence and losses whose consequences require their own assessment. A resilience budget funds a specific capability in a specific setting. Its quality rests on the service preserved and the recovery demonstrated, with a verification date and the resources needed to do it again.

### The numerical case, spelled out

l0g teaching model: six site functions unavailable from hour zero; teams mobilised at hour two; four hours per complete job; independent work, interchangeable teams, material resources ready and zero travel time. Two teams finish at 6, 6, 10, 10, 14 and 14 hours; three finish at 6, 6, 6, 10, 10 and 10. Totals: 60 and 48 site-hours. Five-year incremental cost: €30,000 + 5 × €6,000 = €60,000. Benefit per event: 12 × €2,500 = €30,000. Threshold: €60,000 ÷ €30,000 = two comparable events. Attack frequency remains unknown, and the threshold preserves that uncertainty.
