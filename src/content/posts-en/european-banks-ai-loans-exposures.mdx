*AI’s creditors · 2/6. [Part one: Jupiter and the claim sold by HSBC](/en/analysis/jupiter-hsbc-european-banks-ai-financing/).*

Crédit Agricole CIB, Deutsche Bank’s New York branch and Societe Generale appear on the cover of the same US credit agreement. Signed on 7 August 2026 for AI computing provider CoreWeave, it names them as joint bookrunners, a role in arranging the financing. A few lines further down, the list of lenders points to an attachment: *Schedule 2.01*. [S01](#source-S01)

That is where individual commitments should appear. The schedule is absent from the public version. The filing submitted to the US regulator explicitly states that certain schedules have been omitted and will be supplied to the SEC on request. The overall amount is clearly disclosed: **$2.6 billion of borrowing capacity**. [S02](#source-S02)

The banks’ names are public; their individual shares remain unknown. Understanding their exposure means identifying the loans they retain and the amounts they may still have to advance. The CoreWeave agreement provides a starting point.

## From the signed facility to the retained loan

The CoreWeave facility is called [DDTL](/en/glossary/ddtl/) 5.5: a delayed-draw term loan that allows funds to be borrowed progressively. The borrowing subsidiary finances GPU servers and infrastructure needed to fulfil customer contracts. The 10-Q filed on 12 November provides the position at **30 September 2026: $1.555 billion of gross debt outstanding**. [S03](#source-S03)

Capacity and outstanding debt describe different stages. A facility authorises funding subject to its terms; the outstanding balance records debt still owed on a particular date. Retained exposure introduces another question: how much belongs to an individual lender after drawdowns, repayments and any transfers?

<AiBankExposureFigure lang="en" kind="loan" number={1} sourceHref="#source-S03" />

The agreement provides for a register maintained by JPMorgan, the administrative agent, recording lenders, commitments, amounts owed and assignments. A lender selling participations to other investors maintains a separate participant register. These records would help trace legal positions and their redistribution. The public documents identify roles in arranging the financing, with individual allocations still to be established. [S01](#source-S01)

A separate document materially affects the risk: **CoreWeave Inc. guarantees the borrowing subsidiary’s obligations**. The dedicated company separates assets and contracts, while the guarantee preserves recourse to the parent. That gives creditors additional rights. The economic value of those rights still depends on the guarantor’s ability to pay. [S04](#source-S04)

This illustrates why a [special-purpose vehicle, or SPV](/en/glossary/spv/), needs careful interpretation. It is a company formed for a particular transaction. Guarantees determine creditors’ rights of recourse; consolidation rules determine how the debt appears in the group’s accounts. [S03](#source-S03)

## A measurable portfolio at Societe Generale

Societe Generale’s disclosures allow us to go further. At the end of the third quarter of 2026, the group reports approximately **€12 billion of regulatory exposure** to data centres, IT consulting and software. The measure is **exposure at default, or [EAD](/en/glossary/ead/)**, calculated under prudential rules for the event of a counterparty default. It covers positions on the balance sheet and off-balance-sheet commitments, with the applicable treatment of credit protection. [S05](#source-S05) [S07](#source-S07)

Data centres account for **60%** of that portfolio. Multiplying the two rounded figures produces an estimate of **about €7.2 billion**. [S05](#source-S05)

Since data centres can serve AI, cloud and conventional computing, isolating the AI component requires a breakdown of the financed uses. Estimating a loss also means assessing default probabilities and the amounts lenders could recover.

<AiBankExposureFigure lang="en" kind="portfolio" number={2} sourceHref="#source-S05" />

The presentation for debt investors provides the same breakdown. It also says that roughly 70% of data-centre exposure is geared towards hyperscalers. Across the wider data-centre, consulting and software portfolio, about 70% is investment grade, the higher-quality credit category. These percentages have different denominators. [S06](#source-S06)

The financial strength of these counterparties needs to be assessed, alongside their concentration: several buildings, owners and banks may depend on payments from the same technology group. Assessing that dependence requires a map of tenants and guarantees alongside the map of borrowers.

## London and Frankfurt count differently

On 10 November, Barclays finance director Anna Cross answered a question about data centres. She reported **approximately £4.5 billion of commitments**, explicitly distinguishing that measure from drawn amounts. She described the total as flat year on year and split roughly equally between corporate exposure and structured finance within Markets. For the latter, she cited margining and rapid turnover. [S08](#source-S08)

Those details give the number meaning. A bank can have promised more than it has already advanced. Margin calls can change the collateral that must be provided and the liquidity needed during the financing’s life. Comparing Barclays with Societe Generale would require reconciling contractual commitments with EAD, then aligning the activities covered and the observation dates.

At Deutsche Bank’s 28 October results presentation, finance director Raja Akram maintained that exposure described as AI-related or data-centre exposure was **below €10 billion**, with **70% in the United States**. He highlighted long leases, low loan-to-value ratios and a hedging programme. His response did not define the measure sufficiently to translate it into drawn balances or comparable EAD. [S09](#source-S09)

The geography nevertheless establishes an important point: a European bank can finance US infrastructure. Following the debt requires distinguishing the banking group’s home country, the entity making the loan and the project’s location. A count restricted to European construction sites would miss part of the activity observed here.

Measuring Europe’s exposure adds another question: which institutions and activities belong in the count? The ECB reference point published on **9 November**, approximately **€62 billion** in the second quarter of 2026, represents **1.2%** of euro-area banks’ total committed corporate exposures. Its sector selection includes activity beyond AI while excluding some property-classified data centres and indirect lending, including through SPVs. This benchmark therefore has a different scope from our investigation of European banking groups. [S14](#source-S14)

## Electricity also needs a bank’s backing

On 21 April, data-centre operator Switch announced a **$2.6 billion** performance letter-of-credit facility to support obligations associated with power generation and transmission. On **10 June**, it increased that capacity to **$3.5 billion**, explicitly describing the facility as **uncommitted**. The ceiling defines a framework for issuing letters, with utilisation and individual bank allocations still unknown. [S10](#source-S10)

The distinction becomes concrete when a **[letter of credit](/en/glossary/lc/)** is issued: the bank then undertakes to pay the beneficiary if a demand meets its terms. Issuance creates a conditional obligation, potentially before any cash is advanced. Switch’s releases disclose neither the amount of letters actually issued nor payments made following demands. The announced $3.5 billion is therefore not a firm bank commitment covering the whole facility. [S10](#source-S10) [S18](#source-S18)

<AiBankExposureFigure lang="en" kind="power" number={3} sourceHref="#source-S10" />

The June announcement identifies BBVA and Natixis as structuring and issuing banks. Natixis had also said in April that it acted as administrative agent. The April release named BNP Paribas, Societe Generale, Rabobank and Standard Chartered among the participants. Those roles explain the arrangement, with individual shares still to be established from the agreements. [S10](#source-S10) [S11](#source-S11)

Moving from the framework to an issued letter, then from a letter to payment, changes a bank’s position. A compliant demand can create a cash requirement. After payment, reimbursement rights depend on the applicable agreements. Switch presents this bank backing as support for its obligations to the power supply chain. Assessing the risk requires the issued letters, their expiry dates, payment conditions and security. [S10](#source-S10) [S18](#source-S18)

## New financing continues

Banks remain present in recent transactions. On **12 November**, Rowan Digital Infrastructure announced a new **$590 million revolving facility** and a **$355 million letter-of-credit facility**. Societe Generale appears in their lender group. Those capacities belong to the entire syndicate; the French bank’s individual share is unknown. [S12](#source-S12)

This announcement argues for examining loan sales transaction by transaction. A bank can reduce one position, finance another customer and change its portfolio mix. Establishing a sector-wide retreat would require reconciling new commitments, exits and remaining availability over a common period.

The ECB lending survey published on **27 October** points in the same direction. Several banks expected a gradual increase in demand to finance digital infrastructure, particularly data centres. They described AI-related lending as more likely to target infrastructure providers than software developers or companies adopting AI. These are banks’ assessments, rather than a measure of loans already granted. [S15](#source-S15)

## Private credit belongs on the same map

Some exposure can run through investors holding the debt. At its second-quarter results presentation on **29 July**, Deutsche Bank acknowledged a small data-centre component within its [private-credit exposure](/en/glossary/credit-prive/). Management thus described an overlap between the categories, leaving its size unquantified. [S13](#source-S13)

A Bank for International Settlements bulletin published on **27 October** describes the broader mechanism: banks can lend to project vehicles and to investors holding their debt. The authors observed limited and short-lived bond-market movements on the announcement days studied. Their method describes associations between the news and market prices, without isolating a causal effect. [S16](#source-S16)

The boundary of the calculation matters. Debt received by a project and borrowing obtained by a fund to finance its portfolio are two financial relationships. Adding both as money delivered to the construction site would inflate final funding. Keeping both links on the map, however, helps explain how trouble at the project might reach several creditors. [Insurance on AI loans](/en/analysis/ai-loan-insurance-banks-reinsurers/) introduces further conditional obligations to identify contract by contract.

On **16 November**, the ECB again highlighted the expansion of AI financing into debt and the associated concentrations, while recognising banks’ resilience. Capital and liquidity help absorb a shock, while identifying shared counterparties improves the assessment of its reach. [S17](#source-S17)

The investigation has moved from CoreWeave’s loan agreement to Switch’s announcements on power-related credit support. Bank exposure extends beyond disbursed loans to the financing of some debt investors. Societe Generale’s disclosures let us estimate one part of that activity; a European total still requires comparable data and each lender’s retained positions. The next stage lies in fund accounts: when an investor buys a loan, who supplies the money, and on what security? That is where the next instalment will begin.

## Limitations

This investigation uses public documents reviewed through **17 November 2026**. Management statements and announcements by transaction participants are attributed; they do not constitute an independent examination of each portfolio. Figures retain their original currencies, periods and definitions. The €7.2 billion estimate is derived from rounded data and covers data centres with mixed uses. No European banking total or loss estimate is calculated.

Current lender allocations, sub-participations, the particular terms of certain guarantees and AI-attributable indirect exposures remain incomplete. The diagrams explain the data or mechanisms identified in their captions; they do not depict a fully reconstructed chain of ultimate risk holders.
