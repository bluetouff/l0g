A free software download, an evening of streaming and an advertisement can travel through the same cables. Their digital weight is measured in bytes. Their usefulness, revenue and profit margins are very different things. France’s Socialist Party proposes turning that weight into a new tax base: **5.5 euro cents for every gigabyte sent to French Internet users**, payable by content suppliers. [S01](#source-s01)

On X, Laurent Lhardit, the MP sponsoring the amendment, forecasts **€10 billion in receipts** and says subscribers will have nothing to pay. The promise is attractive: collect money from large platforms, protect households and make competition fairer. Yet the bill chooses a unit that runs through the entire digital economy, from advertising groups to distributors of free software. Naming the company that remits the tax leaves its ultimate economic burden unresolved. [S01](#source-s01), [S08](#source-s08)

**The central flaw is poor targeting.** The proposal uses data volume as a stand-in for economic power, adds caps based on revenue, then promises a burden concentrated on foreign giants. Each step introduces a gap between the political claim and the mechanism. The adopted amendment, parliamentary debate, sponsor’s replies and regulators’ research help explain where those gaps appear.

## One amendment through, a tax still under construction

On 30 October 2026, during the first reading of the 2027 Finance Bill, the National Assembly adopted amendment **I-1358** by **11 votes to 10, with 14 abstentions**: 35 MPs participated in the vote. That vote adopted the amendment. As of 31 October, the budget remains under examination and the provision can still be changed or removed. Implementation would also require technical arrangements specified by decree. [S01](#source-s01), [S02](#source-s02), [S22](#source-s22)

The idea has changed along the way. The Socialist Party’s official *Vivre libres* programme proposed **€0.10 per gigabyte** to finance network infrastructure. The budget amendment uses **€0.055**; its operative provisions arrange payment to the state and contain no earmarking for network operators. An earlier version was rejected by the Finance Committee on 9 October. Mixing these stages produces the wrong rate, funding claim or legislative status. [S07](#source-s07), [S21](#source-s21)

Under the current wording, Internet access providers and other businesses offering Internet access to households and companies would record data quantities by sender. The telecoms regulator, **Arcep**, and the tax authority would supervise the process, with consolidated reporting every quarter. The sender would pay. The bill prohibits requiring records of communications content or their destination. [S01](#source-s01)

Its scope is broad. The definition of content suppliers contains no minimum traffic or revenue threshold, restriction to large groups, or express exemption for nonprofit organisations. A cap would then apply, expressed as **15% of the sender’s revenue in France or 2% of its total revenue**. The interaction between those alternatives, and the precise entity or group perimeter, need clarification. [S01](#source-s01)

For scale, a hypothetical **100 GB** transfer would generate **€5.50 of gross tax before any cap**. That multiplication establishes a starting amount; subscription prices would still depend on the caps, contracts and commercial responses. The calculation is useful because it reveals the pressure on services delivering large amounts of data for relatively little revenue. [S01](#source-s01)

## Free Facebook, paying advertisers

The parliamentary debate contains a revealing exchange. Philippe Juvin, the budget’s general rapporteur, warns that large platforms could pass the charge to **advertisers**. Lhardit replies that he struggles to see how Facebook, Instagram or TikTok could pass it to users, since those services are free. His answer switches customers halfway through the argument. An advertising-funded service has another paying customer: the business buying access to its audience. [S03](#source-s03)

That channel already exists for other taxes. Google says it charges advertisers a **2% regulatory surcharge in France before 1 January 2027**, with an announced increase to 3% taking effect on that date, to cover costs associated with the digital services tax. Amazon has also explained to French sellers a charge of **3% of its selling fees and certain fulfilment fees**, distinct from the total price of the goods. These commercial policies concern a different tax. They demonstrate how a platform can move a cost to business customers while keeping access free for its audience. [S11](#source-s11), [S12](#source-s12)

An invoice posted by Mounir Laggoune on 31 October makes that mechanism tangible. Presented as a Google Ads invoice billed to Finary, it shows **€7,566.63** in advertising charges, followed by **€151.33 in French regulatory operating costs**, bringing the total to **€7,717.96**. The surcharge equals 2% of the advertising amount, rounded to the nearest cent. The screenshot covers April, with no year visible in the excerpt. We treat it as a document published by its author, consistent with Google’s official fee schedule. [S25](#source-s25), [S11](#source-s11)

The distinction matters: **the 2%, rising to 3% in 2027, describes Google’s commercial surcharge**. France’s digital services tax has its own statutory rate of 3%, applied to a legally defined base. The invoice illustrates an additional charge to an advertiser. How much eventually falls on that advertiser’s customers, employees or margin depends on its economic circumstances. That subsequent adjustment is precisely the issue left open by the assurance to subscribers. [S11](#source-s11), [S26](#source-s26)

Several responses to the gigabyte levy are possible. A paid service could increase subscription prices. An advertising platform could change prices, commissions or creator payments. A company could absorb part of the cost in its margin, reduce quality, narrow its offer or postpone investment. Competition, customers’ sensitivity to price and contractual arrangements would determine that distribution, known as [tax incidence](/en/glossary/incidence-fiscale/).

Predicting that households will bear every cent would be as unwarranted as promising them complete protection. **The tangible risk is a dispersed burden**, some of which could return to the French businesses the policy seeks to support: retailers buying advertising, publishers paying hosting providers, and creators dependent on a platform. Treasury receipts and protection of household purchasing power require separate assessment.

## File size is a poor measure of economic strength

A publisher can charge a high price for a service that exchanges little data. Another can distribute a large file for free. The same film can be funded through subscriptions, advertising, grants or donations. A byte counter puts these activities on a common scale while saying very little about their operators’ ability to pay.

The explanatory statement explicitly chooses data as a **proxy**, an indirect indicator, for platforms’ economic activity. It argues that physical delivery to French users is harder to relocate than accounting profits. This is the proposal’s strongest argument. Serving a local audience does leave a traffic footprint. The challenge is turning that footprint into an economically meaningful measure that can be assigned to a taxable entity. [S01](#source-s01)

The burden would depend in particular on revenue earned per gigabyte. A business delivering large amounts of data for little revenue could reach its cap quickly; a highly lucrative activity with little data traffic could remain far below its own. The levy would therefore favour some ways of monetising a service over others, rather than rank companies by profitability. That selection follows from the rate structure itself.

The cap mitigates potentially excessive gross liabilities, but brings revenue and its location back into a levy promoted for its physical tax base. The administration still needs French revenue, total revenue and their legal owner. Hosting content internally or buying distribution from an intermediary could also change the entity whose cap applies, depending on the attribution rule. The legislation needs to settle that issue before contractual organisation becomes a tax-planning variable. [S01](#source-s01)

## Networks are built for peaks

Infrastructure costs introduce another mismatch. A connection has a maximum capacity, much as a road has a number of lanes. Investment needs depend partly on the periods when that capacity approaches saturation. Moving a download into a quiet period can use spare capacity; concentrating many downloads at once can require a link upgrade. Identical annual volumes can therefore create different constraints.

**BEREC**, the body bringing together European telecoms regulators, distinguishes fixed-access costs related to coverage and subscribers, capacity upgrades, and the greater traffic sensitivity of mobile networks. Its 2024 report stresses peak demand and describes a downward trend in interconnection prices and costs. Investment needs and bottlenecks remain real. The distinction explains why accumulated annual gigabytes are a crude approximation of those costs. [S05](#source-s05), [S06](#source-s06)

[Content delivery networks, or CDNs](/en/glossary/cdn/), make the difference visible. They store copies near users, avoiding repeated transport from a distant server. Arcep’s end-2025 observations show an average ratio of **11 between traffic served by internal caches and traffic needed to fill them**, with substantial variation across operators. This is a traffic-volume ratio, rather than a count of requests for one particular file. [S04](#source-s04)

The amendment expressly counts **outgoing CDN traffic**. For the same final volume, moving content closer to users could save transport while leaving the gigabyte tax base unchanged. The fiscal incentive primarily rewards reducing delivered volume; it gives much less recognition to better distribution architecture. That matters when network efficiency forms part of the justification.

## OVHcloud’s scenario puts gross tax at €70 million

OVHcloud’s chief executive puts numbers on the exposure of a French provider. On 31 October, chairman and CEO Octave Klaba reported **average traffic of 324 Gbit/s** to French Internet access providers. That figure provides an input for a volume scenario. It remains management’s own figure: the underlying measurements, observation period and precise scope of the flows require documentation. [S27](#source-s27), [S28](#source-s28)

Extending that average over **365 days**, and assuming every corresponding flow is taxable, produces roughly **1.28 billion GB** and **€70.25 million in gross tax**. The calculation uses decimal gigabytes and eight bits per byte: 324 ÷ 8 × 365 × 24 × 3,600 × 0.055. Klaba’s €70 million order of magnitude is therefore reproducible under those assumptions. An actual liability would then require measuring the relevant period’s taxable traffic and applying the caps. [S27](#source-s27), [S01](#source-s01)

Published accounts provide another benchmark. For the year ended **31 August 2026**, OVHcloud reported **€1,145.1 million in worldwide revenue**, including **€550.6 million in France**. Two per cent of the first amount is **€22.902 million**; fifteen per cent of the second is **€82.59 million**, above the gross-tax scenario. These calculations help explain Klaba’s **€23–70 million range** under different readings of the caps. They remain benchmarks drawn from a past financial year: the accounts allocate sales according to the entity managing the customer relationship, while the future tax’s territorial base and assessment period need their own rules. [S28](#source-s28)

Klaba says OVHcloud would pass the charge to customers. That is a commercial intention expressed by an affected business, with the final amount and terms still open. It gives the political problem a concrete form: a levy sold as a charge on foreign giants could also reach a French provider and the businesses buying its services. A revenue cap limits the amount collected; the ability to absorb that amount remains a separate question. [S27](#source-s27)

He also fears that foreign competitors would escape payment in practice. The amendment, however, provides a collection mechanism for suppliers without a French entity. The issue to examine is the effectiveness and cost of cross-border enforcement. Treating foreign non-payment as settled would turn a competition concern into an observed outcome. The legislature owes that concern a documented response. [S27](#source-s27), [S01](#source-s01)

## Environmental policy deserves a better gauge

The explanatory statement claims an environmental benefit: the levy would encourage platforms to compress and optimise data. A financial incentive can strengthen that effort. But the text ends its argument by asserting that the relevant businesses currently fail to do so. Documented deployments contradict that generalisation. [S01](#source-s01)

In December 2025, Netflix reported that the **AV1 video codec**, a compression format, accounted for around **30% of its viewing**, with lower bandwidth use in the relevant sessions. In June 2026, Meta described deploying AV1 for Messenger and WhatsApp calls, including trade-offs between compression, computing, battery use and device compatibility. Those are company-reported results. Arcep’s cache measurements separately provide independent evidence of distribution optimisations already in operation. [S17](#source-s17), [S18](#source-s18), [S04](#source-s04)

Environmental impact depends on the device, the network used, the computation performed and the electricity supply. Arcep’s environmental survey distinguishes the weak traffic dependence of some fixed-network and home-router consumption from the greater sensitivity of mobile networks. A uniform per-byte rate follows none of those distinctions. [S16](#source-s16)

Saving bytes can be useful. A coherent environmental tax would need a rate linked to the harm being addressed, an assessment of substitution effects and a way to measure outcomes. The promise becomes especially fragile when the revenue cap neutralises the incentive being invoked.

## The cap also cuts the incentive to save data

Consider a **fictional business** earning **€1 million in annual French revenue**. To illustrate the mechanism, assume that a **15% cap** applies: **€150,000**, with revenue held constant. At **€0.055 per GB**, gross tax reaches that cap at approximately **2.73 million GB**. Beyond that point, another gigabyte leaves capped tax unchanged. A reduction in traffic saves tax only once it brings gross liability below the cap. [S01](#source-s01)

The design pulls in two directions. It seeks to encourage major senders to reduce volume while limiting their exposure above a threshold. The cap remains useful against an excessive gross bill for data-intensive businesses; **that protection weakens the marginal environmental incentive**. A serious proposal needs to address the trade-off openly.

The denominator matters too. Fifteen percent of revenue can represent a substantial share of a low-margin company’s profit. A cap therefore offers limited reassurance on its own. It constrains a charge based on bytes by reference to sales revenue, which still has to cover wages, purchases and other costs.

## VideoLAN exposes a gap in the promise

On 30 October, VideoLAN, the nonprofit behind the VLC media player, warned on X that it estimated the proposal would cost it **more than €1 million a year**. Lhardit replied that the tax was capped and would not apply in its case. The exchange prompted numerous reactions, including entrepreneurs’ concerns about their services’ viability. These are public positions, distinct from an impact study or a representative opinion sample. [S09](#source-s09), [S10](#source-s10), [S24](#source-s24)

VideoLAN’s estimate cannot establish its eventual liability: the relevant volumes, taxable entity and application of the cap still need documentation. In particular, **a direct sender with genuinely zero relevant revenue could in principle have a zero cap**. The absence of an express nonprofit exemption therefore does not establish that VideoLAN would owe the amount it announced. [S01](#source-s01)

The issue shifts when distribution runs through a commercial host or CDN. That provider has its own revenue, contracts and customers. The amendment’s explanatory statement specifically envisages an intermediary keeping its customers’ identities confidential and choosing whether to pass the tax on. A nonprofit could thus be protected in its own tax position while remaining exposed to a higher distribution bill. This is a contractual risk to investigate, rather than an established future charge. [S01](#source-s01)

Effective protection for free software and public-interest services requires the legislation to specify which entities and services qualify, including their distribution arrangements. An assurance in an X reply has a very different reach from a written rule applying to intermediaries.

## Counting bytes and identifying a taxpayer

Traffic measurement already has standards. **IPFIX**, a protocol for exporting flow information, includes byte counters. Counting can therefore take place without reading a message’s contents. The fiscal challenge begins with assigning volumes to the correct legal entity, applying its cap and preventing double counting. [S15](#source-s15)

The amendment refers to a registration number with **IANA**, the organisation coordinating certain technical Internet resources, without specifying the registry. Among those resources, [autonomous system numbers, or ASNs](/en/glossary/asn/), identify networks with a coherent routing policy and are assigned through regional registries. That system describes traffic routing. Turning it into a tax instrument requires a link to a taxable identity, revenue and group perimeter. [S13](#source-s13)

Cloudflare, for example, explains that its IP addresses are shared across hostnames using its proxy service. The visible network can carry content for many businesses. A workable regime needs to identify the liable sender, handle hosting and distribution chains, and audit quantities while preserving users’ privacy. Metering standards solve part of the technical problem; tax attribution and control rules still require explicit design. [S14](#source-s14), [S23](#source-s23)

The implementing decree would also need to specify observation points, which bytes count, retransmissions and deduplication. The bill’s safeguards concerning content and destination matter. Delivering them requires data minimisation, robust aggregation, retention limits and suitable controls. Treating mass inspection of messages as a technical necessity would exaggerate the problem. The substantive task is reliable attribution compatible with those safeguards. [S01](#source-s01), [S15](#source-s15)

## A €10 billion forecast that needs rebuilding

The explanatory statement attributes **200 billion GB a year** delivered to French subscribers to Arcep. Multiplying that figure by the proposed rate gives **€11 billion gross**. The sponsors forecast €10 billion in receipts. The public documents examined provide no detailed bridge from those aggregate figures through emitter-level caps to collectible revenue. [S01](#source-s01)

The method matters as much as the amount. Arcep’s barometer publishes interconnection bitrates and describes pricing at the **95th percentile**, which excludes the highest 5% of observations. Multiplying a peak or billing bitrate by every second in a year gives a hypothetical annualisation. An observed volume would require a representative average bitrate. Here, the 200 billion GB remains the sponsors’ attributed estimate; its correspondence with a verifiable primary series remains to be established. [S04](#source-s04)

Traffic concentration helps identify major networks. It supplies a different piece of information from the share of tax their companies would pay. Caps, third-party content delivery, revenue allocation, behavioural changes and collection from foreign entities affect the outcome. A credible forecast requires taxpayer-level data and several scenarios with a publishable methodology.

In the debate, minister David Amiel argued that half the burden could fall on smaller suppliers, including French businesses. His **€5 billion** represents half the sponsors’ €10 billion; it is not an independent government forecast reducing total receipts to €5 billion. Lhardit, meanwhile, put TF1’s cost at **€200,000–€300,000**, without detailing the calculation in his intervention. These remain claims made during the debate. [S03](#source-s03)

## Digital sovereignty can also lose services

A tax can change decisions about serving a market as well as prices. A provider with a small French customer base might narrow its offer if compliance and tax costs exceed that market’s commercial value. A large group can spread fixed compliance costs across more business. That mechanism creates a risk for new entrants and niche services, even as the political case focuses on dominant firms.

South Korea offers a limited warning. In December 2023, Twitch announced that it would close its Korean business in February 2024, citing very high network costs despite efforts to reduce traffic. That is the company’s explanation. Network payments to operators and a French tax paid to the Treasury are different instruments. The Korean withdrawal illustrates a possible commercial response to distribution costs, without predicting the fate of individual services in France. [S19](#source-s19)

European law requires equal care. Net neutrality protects access to content and traffic treatment by Internet access providers. A general volume-based tax does not itself instruct providers to block or slow a service. Its legal compatibility needs assessment against its actual rules and implementation. Competition and service diversity already provide sufficient economic reasons to demand a proper impact study. [S20](#source-s20)

## The wrong unit makes poor policy

The Socialists raise a real issue: how can cross-border digital activity contribute fairly to public finances where its customers live? Measuring a flow delivered in France has administrative appeal. Taxation can also capture some economic rent and fund useful public services. Those aims deserve evaluation alongside the costs imposed on businesses and users.

A gigabyte nevertheless mixes software distribution, video delivery and highly profitable advertising activity. Caps change the distribution of burdens again; intermediaries shift the collection point; commercial adjustments undermine assurances that subscribers will be spared. The environmental argument adds another objective that this uniform, capped rate serves inconsistently.

A serious rewrite would begin by selecting the target: French-market revenue, profits, platform rents or environmental harm. It would publish simulations by business category, define protections for small suppliers and public-interest services, settle attribution between publishers and intermediaries, then estimate collection costs and possible pass-through. Those decisions constitute the substance of tax policy.

**As drafted, the gigabyte tax is economically misguided: it seeks the giants’ wealth by weighing Internet traffic.** The risk is a charge on distributing services, reaching businesses France would like to grow while leaving market power to determine who ultimately pays. Parliament still has time to choose a better tax base.

## Method and limits

Research cut-off: 31 October 2026. The amendment, vote, parliamentary transcript and legislative file were checked against Arcep and BEREC publications and the cited technical documentation. Lhardit’s posts, VideoLAN’s response and reactions were read directly on X. Statements by interested parties are attributed; community notes and angry replies identify questions rather than replace primary evidence. Calculations are explicitly hypothetical. This article presents no new forecast of receipts, retail price increases, business failures or employment effects. Unsettled tax rules and missing company-level data constrain such projections. This page includes no tool measuring readers’ traffic.
