## Tracing losses from banks to reinsurers, and the customers linking their risks

*Documentary investigation · l0g · 11 November 2026. Sources checked as of this date. Figures in the illustrative examples are explicit assumptions; they describe no identified bank insurance policy. Contract documents for the recent transactions remain outside the public sources reviewed.*

A bank can finance a data centre, keep the loan on its books and ask an insurer to take on part of the default risk through [loan non-payment insurance](/en/glossary/assurance-de-non-paiement-des-prets/). Repayments still flow to the bank; if a covered payment default occurs, another balance sheet must provide the money. That protection can sometimes allow the bank to lend more. The question is how many promises, at the end of the chain, depend on the same revenue. [S18](#source-S18) [S10](#source-S10)

On 11 November, the *Financial Times* reported that Barclays had recently bought cover, HSBC had explored the product and JPMorgan had considered it before deciding against it. The reporting relies on people familiar with the programmes or discussions. These are three distinct situations, and neither the contracts nor the precise risk carriers have been disclosed. [S01](#source-S01)

The implications extend beyond those banks. The previous day, the UK banking supervisor finalised a reform of its treatment of large exposures. From 1 January 2028, where eligible protection reduces an exposure, the amount deducted will have to be reassigned to the protection provider. Some reassigned exposures will be exempt from the limits while remaining subject to reporting. The rule captures the problem running through this investigation: risk can leave one line of the exposure table and accumulate in another. [S07](#source-S07)

Insurance has a real economic purpose. It brings other institutions’ loss-absorbing capacity into the financing structure and can spread losses among institutions able to absorb them. Assessing that diversification, however, means looking beyond the names of buildings and banks. One buyer of computing capacity may support several data centres; one reinsurer may protect several insurers; an insurer may also hold bonds issued by companies whose debts it guarantees. **The number of contracts gives only a partial picture of the number of independent risks.** Supervisory principles specifically call for examination of these interactions between business activities, assets and liabilities. [S10](#source-S10)

## The market predates November’s announcements

Insurance for data centre financing already has a history. In July 2023, The Hartford explained how non-payment cover could allow a bank to increase its commitment to a project. The insurer also described particular caution towards some facilities hosting AI. Although a commercial publication, the document establishes that both the product and this distinction predate the current announcements. [S18](#source-S18)

A case published by Marsh and Oliver Wyman in June 2024 provides a more concrete example. It described a US bank’s $300 million senior secured term loan in the data centre sector, supported by non-payment insurance. The report discusses the arrangement’s prudential treatment while keeping the bank and insurer anonymous. The $300 million refers to the loan; the fraction of risk actually transferred is undisclosed. The case documents the mechanism’s use according to the report’s authors, while leaving its full financial structure out of view. [S06](#source-S06)

What makes November’s development significant is its use during a rapid expansion of AI lending. In reporting published on 28 October and carried elsewhere the following day, Bloomberg said Barclays was seeking protection on more than $10 billion of debt associated with this infrastructure. The discussions were still exploratory. That figure does not establish how much is now insured: no public reconciliation connects the newly reported purchase with the earlier report. [S02](#source-S02) [S01](#source-S01)

Another Bloomberg report, on 30 October, described HSBC’s discussions with insurers. It separately mentioned a [significant risk transfer (SRT)](/en/glossary/srt/) transaction referencing more than $1 billion of data centre loans. That billion refers to the SRT’s reference portfolio, mentioned separately from the insurance discussions. SRT here describes a structured transaction transferring part of a portfolio’s risk; applying the label to every loan insurance policy would blur a material distinction. [S03](#source-S03) [S12](#source-S12)

HSBC’s own public comments provide useful context. On the 27 October analyst call, chief financial officer Pam Kaur described data centre lending as small and a limited contributor to the group’s growth. Managing concentrations can be ordinary lending discipline. The reported initiatives alone offer insufficient grounds to infer particular concern about the borrowers involved. [S04](#source-S04)

## Three contracts, three different questions

The mechanism starts with the data centre’s commercial contract. A customer reserves capacity, space or computing services, with payments expected to contribute to project revenue. Then comes the loan agreement: the operator or a dedicated vehicle borrows to build, equip or refinance the facility. Finally, the bank may buy a policy covering a defined loss from non-payment. Market publications distinguish this bank protection from insurance on trade invoices. [S18](#source-S18) [S19](#source-S19) [S21](#source-S21)

Those distinctions become consequential when a project runs into trouble. A defective server, a delayed power connection or a disputed delivery can affect the customer contract. For the bank, the first question concerns the debt: which entity still owes payment, when is it due, and what recourse is available? The insurance question follows: do that obligation, borrower and event fall within the cover purchased? Financing for the building, financing for the chips and the operator’s receivables may each carry different protections. The [Broadcom–Anthropic structure](/en/analysis/broadcom-anthropic-blackstone-63-billion-risk/) provides another example involving equipment leases and guarantees, with an architecture distinct from loan insurance. [S09](#source-S09) [S19](#source-S19)

Property insurance, which protects a building or equipment against a covered physical event, follows a further set of rules. Announced capacity to insure physical infrastructure therefore tells us little about capacity to insure its borrowings. Similarly, invoice credit insurance and a loan non-payment policy may respond differently to a dispute over performance. The insured obligation defines the relevant scope. [S18](#source-S18) [S19](#source-S19)

## More room on the balance sheet can mean several things

For a credit committee, protection can make a loan acceptable when it would otherwise exceed the bank’s internal appetite for a customer or sector. The regulatory calculation has its own criteria. Any relief depends on the applicable regime, the calculation method, the guarantor’s quality and the policy terms. The US case presented by Marsh and Oliver Wyman uses a particular methodology; its conclusions cannot be extended to all US banks. [S06](#source-S06) [S08](#source-S08) [S09](#source-S09)

Under the standardised approach, the Basel framework describes a substitution mechanism: the protected share is treated according to the protection provider, while the uncovered share remains linked to the borrower. Among its requirements are protection that is clearly identifiable, legally enforceable and available within an appropriate timeframe. Maturity or currency mismatches and residual risks must be addressed. Each jurisdiction must implement this international standard; it does not approve any particular insurance policy. [S08](#source-S08)

Capital and funding also need to be distinguished. With unfunded insurance, a bank acquires a promise of indemnification, without receiving an immediate cash reserve equal to the guaranteed amount. Some structured risk transfers, by contrast, have resources provided in advance and held as collateral. The Bank of England highlights the different counterparty exposures and payment timing risks these arrangements create. [S12](#source-S12)

The UK reform published on 10 November addresses the concentration created by that promise. The PRA notes that the current framework allows an exposure to be reduced without consistently reassigning the corresponding amount to the guarantor or collateral issuer. It considers that material concentrations can consequently accumulate without being fully recognised, monitored or constrained. The new rule takes effect in 2028. Its economic significance is already clear: when one insurer covers loans from several banks, each bank can reduce its borrower exposure while becoming more dependent on the same insurer. [S07](#source-S07)

For a fixed volume of outstanding loans, well-designed cover can improve how losses are distributed. If banks use all the resulting room to make new loans, the amount financed grows. The overall effect then depends on two developments: sharing an existing risk and creating additional exposures. Public figures do not allow us to calculate the volume of lending enabled by these new policies.

## A loss should be counted once

Consider a deliberately simple example. A $1 billion loan suffers a final loss of $400 million after $600 million is recovered from the borrower and collateral. A policy covers 50% of that loss; the insurer has reinsured 60% of its own obligation. The protections are proportional and aligned, with no deductible in this example, and all valid commitments are honoured in full.

The bank receives $200 million from the insurer and retains a $200 million loss. The insurer recovers $120 million from its reinsurer, leaving a net cost of $80 million. The reinsurer bears $120 million. **200 + 80 + 120 = 400.** The loan, the direct guarantee and the reinsurance represent successive layers of the same risk. Adding their amounts as though they were separate losses would inflate the exposure artificially.

That equality requires clarity about what is being measured. The insurer’s gross claims payment is $200 million, while its net cost after reinsurance is $80 million. For liquidity, the first figure may matter before the second becomes a reality. If the reinsurer pays late, the insurer must fund the gap. IAIS principles make clear that reinsurance leaves the cedant, the insurer transferring part of the risk, responsible to policyholders for valid claims. [S10](#source-S10)

Insurance thus gives the bank an additional claim, with its own collection conditions. Reinsurance gives the insurer another. The ultimate risk bearer may itself transfer a share of its obligations through [retrocession](/en/glossary/retrocession/). Identifying that final bearer would require the successive contracts, limits, participations and protections at every level. The public information assembled here stops well short of that depth. ## Buildings can multiply faster than customers

An operating company provides a view of concentration before insurance enters the picture. In its quarterly report for the period ended 30 September 2026, Core Scientific says CoreWeave accounts for approximately 84% of its nine-month revenue and all of its colocation revenue. Colocation involves providing space, electricity and associated services in data centres. Operating multiple sites therefore leaves the company dependent on payments from its main customer. [S15](#source-S15)

The same report describes the transfer of certain contracts to CW SPV, an indirect CoreWeave subsidiary. CoreWeave remains a primary obligor for the relevant obligations. The new entity adds a legal layer; the filing specifies that the practical ability to enforce recourse would still depend on CoreWeave’s financial condition at the time of default. [S15](#source-S15)

That concentration can change. Announced contracts with AMD and a neocloud counterparty are due to begin generating revenue in phases from 2027. A neocloud is a specialised cloud capacity provider, often focused on AI computing. As of 30 September, Core Scientific reports 1,120 MW of contracted customer power, of which 485 MW is already billable. The ratio, 43.3%, measures progress towards billing within that scope. It is not a measure of machine utilisation. [S15](#source-S15)

## A reinsurer may meet the same buyer several times

The following scenario describes no actual portfolio. Four projects each borrow $100 million. All depend on the same buyer of computing capacity. A common shock produces, by assumption, a credit loss of $40 million on each loan. With the same protection shares as before, the banks together bear $80 million, the insurers $32 million and their common reinsurer $48 million. The total loss is $160 million.

The four borrowers have separate identities. The commercial shock affecting them is shared. Reinsurance can diversify exposure by spreading it among strong institutions with other risks. It can also bring several paths back to the same balance sheet. Multiple insurers at the front of the chain leave that question unresolved.

The protection provider’s assets also deserve scrutiny. An insurer might hold securities issued by a major technology customer while covering loans repaid from that customer’s orders. In this scenario, a common deterioration would affect both its investments and its insurance obligations. International principles call for consideration of these interactions between assets and liabilities, including concentrations by sector and group. [S10](#source-S10)

The public record does not allow that combined exposure to be attributed to any particular insurer in Barclays’ reported programme or HSBC’s discussions. It does establish what needs to be checked: insured exposures, risks assumed through reinsurance, securities held and the corporate groups generating the payments. A useful map must preserve gross and net amounts, dates and ownership links. It must also identify the gaps in the available information.

## A customer dispute can take several paths

A default sometimes begins far from the bank. A customer disputes late delivery of capacity, insufficient availability or performance under the contract. Depending on its terms, the customer may seek a remedy or a price reduction, or exercise an exit right. The project’s expected revenue then changes, potentially affecting debt repayment. Operators’ disclosures show why delivery, commissioning and contractual recourse matter as much as the announced value of orders. [S15](#source-S15)

The insurance needs to be examined at its own level. The loan may remain payable despite the commercial dispute; a challenge may also concern the debt’s validity or the lender’s conduct. The policy determines how these situations are treated. Inferring that every customer dispute is automatically excluded from loan cover, based on the wording of an invoice insurance policy, would apply a potentially different rule to the wrong contract. [S09](#source-S09) [S19](#source-S19)

For policies governed by English law, the practice described by law firm Sullivan calls for particular attention to notification duties, insurer consent to amendments of the loan and the treatment of accelerated repayment. A bank may have the right to demand its entire claim immediately while insurance payments remain tied to the original schedule, depending on the policy. The relevant gap is between the date when the lender needs cash and the date when the protection must pay. [S19](#source-S19)

The word “sanctions” requires the same precision. In its analysis of clause LSW1865, BPL distinguishes a borrower’s non-payment caused by sanctions from a legal prohibition on the insurer performing certain acts. The former may be covered; the latter may require further steps or a licence before payment. This broker commentary on a specific clause establishes neither Barclays’ policy wording nor the outcome of a future claim. [S17](#source-S17)

## Useful diversification, measured where it matters

The strongest argument for insurance is sound: specialised capital can bear credit risk that some banks want to limit. Insurers select borrowers, impose conditions, collect a premium and arrange protection of their own. Reinsurance can broaden geographical and financial diversification. The IAIS describes these benefits while requiring assessment of counterparties and scenarios involving multiple claims. [S10](#source-S10)

The Willis survey published on 26 May 2026 illustrates how capacity and selectivity can coexist. Conducted in January among 76 credit and political risk insurers, it describes a market with ample capacity overall, while reporting that several participants are approaching or have reached their data centre exposure limits. This broker finding reflects the responses collected; it provides neither a worldwide total for insured AI loans nor their ultimate allocation among reinsurers. [S05](#source-S05)

Supervisory data also call for perspective. EIOPA’s dashboard published on 29 October presents European insurance solvency as broadly stable. Its quarterly indicators refer to the second quarter of 2026; annual indicators refer to the end of 2025. In July, the IAIS had described stable aggregate solvency and liquidity positions at the end of 2025. These observations weigh against the idea that the sector as a whole is already weakened. They do not test the new AI financing policies or simultaneous losses that a shared customer could cause. [S13](#source-S13) [S14](#source-S14)

As in our investigation of [the strength of the insurer’s promise](/en/analysis/insurers-asset-managers-08-promise/), actual resilience depends on a specific combination: the carrier’s resources, concentrations in its commitments, collateral quality, the strength of its own protection and the time needed to access funds. A well-capitalised insurer with limited exposure to the same sector and appropriate reinsurance can absorb a loss that would have weighed more heavily on a concentrated bank. Cover involving counterparties already dependent on the same cycle offers less diversification than it may appear to.

## The missing document is a map of commitments

Further investigation would require the complete chain for each transaction: the borrower and its controlling group, the projects concerned, the customers supplying revenue, guarantees, the bank’s retained share, the insurers, reinsurers and any retrocessions. Limits and maturities must accompany the names. Gross amounts show payment obligations; net amounts help prevent the same loss being counted several times.

A stress test would then apply the same commercial shock to every project dependent on the customer concerned. It would need to vary recoveries on buildings and equipment, the time required to redeploy capacity, the maturities of protection and settlement dates. A solvent promise that pays late can create a funding need; a guarantee expiring before the loan leaves an uncovered interval. Supervisory frameworks already recognise these dimensions. [S08](#source-S08) [S10](#source-S10) [S11](#source-S11)

Public data currently leave this map incomplete for the recently reported policies. That documentary limitation must be kept distinct from the controls exercised within the institutions and by their supervisors. This investigation neither estimates future losses nor establishes an insurer failure.

The issue it identifies is more precise. Banks are looking for partners to keep financing highly capital-intensive infrastructure. Insurance can help, provided that the new risk carriers bring effective loss-absorbing capacity and diversification. **The arrangement’s resilience is tested where the contracts meet: at the customers paying the data centres, then at the institutions able to settle several claims at once.** That is where the next coverage announcements will need to be brought together.

## Method and limitations

This investigation compares dated reporting, corporate disclosures and supervisory documents available as of 11 November 2026. Source notes identify the passages used and access limitations. Statements by insurers, brokers and banks are attributed to their authors. Basel, PRA and IAIS frameworks explain the mechanisms within different scopes and effective dates.

Both simulations are deterministic and use US dollars. In the first: final loss = 1,000 − 600 = $400m; direct insurance payment = 400 × 50% = $200m; reinsurance = 200 × 60% = $120m; insurer’s net cost = 200 − 120 = $80m. In the second, each project loses $40m: bank $20m, insurer $8m, reinsurer $12m. Four projects produce 80 + 32 + 48 = $160m. The chosen shares describe no actual policy and estimate no statistical correlation.

The billable-power ratio is calculated separately: 485 / 1,120 × 100 = 43.3%, rounded to one decimal place. Revenue concentration covers the nine months ended 30 September 2026. The insurers’ identities, their onward transfers and the ultimate accumulation of risks behind the new policies remain unknown in the documents reviewed. No future loss or individual weakness is inferred from those gaps.
