*As of 30 October 2026. The new EU handling fee applies from 1 November.*

A T-shirt, a toy and a few taps on a phone: the shopping basket looks simple. Behind the checkout, somebody must describe the goods, classify them, submit a customs declaration and settle the charges. A change to that paperwork can alter the economics of an inexpensive purchase long before the parcel reaches the doorstep.

From 1 November, the European Union will add a €2 handling fee to eligible goods imported following a distance sale. For low-value consignments already covered by July’s temporary €3 customs duty, the two charges can add up to €5 per customs item. That [customs item](/en/glossary/article-douanier/) carries much of the story: several physical objects can form a single item on the declaration. [R01](#source-r01) [R04](#source-r04) [R06](#source-r06)

The retail outcome remains open. A seller can raise the final price, accept a smaller margin, renegotiate costs or change how it brings goods into Europe. Each route has constraints. Following the transaction from the order to the warehouse and then to the checkout helps explain who might ultimately bear the expense.

## Three dates behind the bill

For French shoppers, the succession of measures is unusually easy to muddle. France introduced its own €2 tax on qualifying low-value imports on 1 March 2026. That national measure was repealed with effect from 1 July, when the EU’s temporary €3 customs duty began. The November handling fee is a new EU measure. Adding the former French tax to both European charges would count a tax that has already disappeared. [R09](#source-r09) [R10](#source-r10) [R03](#source-r03)

The July duty concerns qualifying distance-sale goods in consignments with an intrinsic value of €150 or less. It covers imports using [IOSS](/en/glossary/ioss/), the system for collecting VAT at checkout, and goods within the customs definition of postal consignments. The November fee has a wider value range: within its distance-sale scope, it also applies above €150. Above that threshold, the applicable ordinary customs tariff takes the place of the temporary €3 duty; the €2 handling fee remains relevant. Ordinary duty depends on the goods and any applicable preferences. [R03](#source-r03) [R04](#source-r04) [R06](#source-r06)

Timing follows the customs process. For the new fee, the relevant event is acceptance of the declaration for release into free circulation. An order placed in October can therefore fall under the November rules if the declaration is accepted after they start. An order confirmation alone cannot establish which regime applies. [R06](#source-r06)

As of 30 October, the €2 amount and its 1 November application date are established in adopted legislation. There is still no November trading history from which to measure its effect on consumer prices. [R01](#source-r01)

## Count the lines before counting the euros

In everyday shopping, an item is usually one thing you can hold. Customs uses a more specific unit. A declaration item can contain several goods sharing the required tariff classification and description, together with the same origin where origin forms part of the declaration’s data requirements. Correctly grouping identical goods on one line can therefore matter as much as their number. [R04](#source-r04)

The level of classification varies with the declaration. The simplified H7 dataset uses a six-digit Harmonised System code; H6 uses an eight-digit Combined Nomenclature code; H1 uses a ten-digit TARIC code. These are progressively more detailed product classifications. A retailer’s broad catalogue heading, such as “clothes”, cannot establish how many lines the customs declaration requires. [R07](#source-r07)

Consider four identical T-shirts, with the same origin, legitimately grouped on one declaration line. Assume an eligible distance sale, an intrinsic consignment value no higher than €150, and no special relief. From November, that one line produces €3 of temporary duty plus €2 of handling fee: €5 in total, before VAT and any private carrier charges. Four physical garments need not generate four lots of €5. This is a constructed example using the legal grouping rule. [R01](#source-r01) [R03](#source-r03) [R04](#source-r04)

Now assume a T-shirt and a toy require two separate lines in an otherwise comparable eligible consignment. The duty becomes €6 and the handling fee €4, giving €10 before VAT and private charges. These examples assume the classifications and grouping have already been checked. Counting products in a basket is an unreliable substitute for that work.

The €150 threshold also needs its own definition. “[Intrinsic value](/en/glossary/valeur-intrinseque/)” generally means the price of the goods themselves, excluding separately identified transport and insurance and identifiable taxes or charges. Where transport is bundled into the price without being separately stated, that bundled amount remains included. The threshold applies to the consignment, rather than to each object or automatically to the VAT-inclusive figure displayed at checkout. [R18](#source-r18) [R03](#source-r03)

This creates a distinctive economic pressure. A fixed charge takes a larger share of a cheap sale than of an expensive one. Different assortments can also produce different charges at the same merchandise value. A basket’s price and physical size reveal only part of its customs cost.

## Billions of imports, with a measurement problem

The scale helps explain the policy. A DG TAXUD chart based on an extraction of the EU’s Surveillance data on 6 February 2026 reports 1.39 billion “items in LVC” in 2022, 2.44 billion in 2023, 4.67 billion in 2024 and 5.88 billion in 2025. LVC means low-value consignments. These four observations come from the same published chart and data vintage. [R12](#source-r12)

<PetitsColisFigure lang="en" kind="volumes" number={3} caption={"DG TAXUD’s reported ‘items in LVC’ rise from 1.39 billion to 5.88 billion between 2022 and 2025. These statistical units describe the flow; they are not a 2026 tax base."} sources={["R12"]} method={"EU27, Surveillance database, extracted 6 February 2026. The chart’s wording deserves to travel with its numbers. Its units cannot safely be relabelled as physical objects, individual delivery parcels or lines liable for the November fee. The public presentation does not establish a conversion between those categories. Multiplying 5.88 billion by €2 would therefore produce a misleading revenue estimate, even before allowing for changes in behaviour and the fee’s legal scope. [R12](#source-r12)

What the figures establish is a very large flow recorded in the low-value import system. The regulation setting the fee points to the associated work: processing data, assessing risk, carrying out controls, maintaining infrastructure and providing staff. Its costing is an aggregate assessment of customs activity. Paying the fee does not mean that an officer opens and individually examines every package. [R01](#source-r01)

Large volumes also complicate the question of who pays. An individual seller may consider a few euros manageable on a single transaction. Across a large business, the same rule can change which products are worth offering, which orders are economical to fulfil and where stock is held. Those are possible commercial responses, whose extent must be measured separately from the statutory charge.

## A warehouse matters when the order comes before or after it

Imagine two routes for the same product. In the first, a European customer places an order while the goods are outside the EU. They are imported to fulfil that sale, perhaps passing through a European warehouse on the way. The intermediate stop does not by itself change the transaction into a purchase from stock already cleared into the European market. The rules follow the underlying distance sale. [R05](#source-r05)

In the second route, an importer brings in a batch, clears it into free circulation and holds the stock before the final customer orders. The import belongs to the ordinary customs and VAT framework. Subsequent deliveries draw on goods already imported. The sequence changes both the customs treatment and the business commitment: the importer must decide what to stock before knowing which individual customers will buy it. [R07](#source-r07)

That commitment has an economic cost. Money sits in inventory; storage must be paid for; some sizes or models may remain unsold. Larger shipments can spread certain transport and administrative costs across more goods. Whether those savings outweigh ordinary duties, warehousing and stock risk depends on the product and the operation. A fast-selling standard product and an obscure accessory need not reach the same answer.

There is also an explicit safeguard against changing only the paperwork. The implementing rules address chains of successive sales before entry into the EU. When customs identifies a distance sale within that chain, it takes that sale into account for the relevant customs procedure. Consolidating goods already sold to individual consumers, or routing them through another European hub, does not automatically erase the original transaction. [R05](#source-r05)

Some movement is already documented. On 25 March, French customs said that certain platforms had redirected flows through other European hubs after the national tax began. The announcement did not identify those platforms or quantify the effect on retail prices. It establishes an official account of rerouting at that time, with narrower evidential reach than a measured estimate of tax avoidance or consumer savings. [R13](#source-r13)

An EU preliminary assessment published on 30 October adds a more recent observation: some low-value goods appear to be arriving in bulk, with the relevant duties paid, for storage and distribution within the Union. The Commission interprets this as a possible change in business models. Its assessment uses a subset of raw Surveillance data, subject to transmission delays, revisions and outliers. It offers an early indication of changing flows, without isolating a causal effect on final prices. [R14](#source-r14)

European warehousing also has a history before these charges. SHEIN announced a new logistics hub in Wrocław on 22 December 2025 and described its Polish logistics presence as dating from 2022. That company announcement supports a useful limit on the story: the existence of a local hub cannot, on its own, be attributed to the November fee. [R19](#source-r19)

## The customs debtor and the shopper occupy different places

In the ordinary platform arrangements described by French customs, a professional handles the declaration and liability: the seller or platform within the relevant VAT arrangement, another qualifying operator or an indirect customs representative. Those roles determine who must account for the public charges. [Tax incidence](/en/glossary/incidence-fiscale/) describes how the economic burden is ultimately shared. They leave room for the commercial price to move afterwards. [R06](#source-r06)

Across the EU, the guidance also preserves a residual case in which an individual can declare goods through a free national online customs service, after other options have been ruled out and where IOSS has not been used. Legal liability therefore needs the actual procedure and country arrangements. A blanket promise that the consumer can never be the customs debtor would go beyond the rules. [R07](#source-r07)

The economic burden can travel further than the legal payment. A platform might take the hit through a lower commission, a merchant through its margin, or a supplier through a negotiated reduction. A customer might face a higher product price, a delivery charge or a smaller discount. These are possible ways to distribute the cost, rather than findings about how any named business will respond.

Competition matters to that distribution. A seller whose buyers can switch easily may have less room to increase its total price. A merchant with very little margin has less capacity to absorb a fixed charge. A platform may also choose to support some products while changing the conditions on others. The regulation supplies the amount due; it supplies no universal percentage that will reach the checkout.

Private clearance charges add another layer. La Poste describes its management fees as payment for handling customs formalities, separately from the public duties and taxes it collects or advances. Their application depends on the service and payment circumstances. A carrier’s administrative fee and the EU handling fee should therefore remain separate when comparing bills. [R17](#source-r17)

## Platform terms and announced promotions

Temu’s French terms, updated on 23 September 2026, provide a concrete example of how import costs may be presented. Section 13.2 says applicable amounts are shown before checkout confirmation, including import charges for products dispatched from outside the EU, and describes payment at checkout without additional payment afterwards. This is evidence about the contractual checkout arrangement. It gives no measured share of the new public charges absorbed by Temu, merchants or shoppers. [R15](#source-r15)

Promotions offer a second caution. In an announcement dated 15 September, Amazon advertised an Amazon Haul promotion of up to 15% off from two items, scheduled for 1–5 October. The offer came after the July duty had begun. Its existence is consistent with sellers continuing to compete aggressively while import costs rise. It cannot establish that the same products became cheaper overall, or that Amazon absorbed the full duty. [R16](#source-r16)

A discount needs a reference price; a checkout total needs a basket and a delivery arrangement. To measure a change attributable to the new fee, the useful comparison would hold the products and conditions steady, then examine the total actually charged. Changes in coupons, quantities, shipping or the source of fulfilment can otherwise obscure what happened.

## Four euros of new cost can mean €2.40 at checkout

VAT connects the commercial decision to the customer’s final bill. Under IOSS, the import itself is exempt from import VAT, while VAT is collected on the sale. The Commission’s revised guidance of 6 October says that when the duty or handling fee is charged to the buyer as part of that sale, it belongs in the sale’s taxable amount. Under the relevant non-IOSS import arrangements, those charges enter the import VAT base. The VAT result therefore follows the actual route and invoicing. [R08](#source-r08)

Take a deliberately simplified example of the first arrangement. Before November, a basket costs €20 excluding VAT. Assume VAT of 20%, collected at sale, making the customer’s total €24. Assume two valid taxable declaration lines. All figures are illustrative, including the VAT rate and the seller’s response. The July duty is already reflected in this starting situation and stays unchanged throughout the calculation.

The new handling fee is €2 multiplied by two lines: a €4 additional cost. Suppose the seller passes on half by increasing the price before VAT by €2. The other €2 reduces the seller’s margin, with every other cost held fixed. Applying the assumed 20% VAT rate to the €2 price increase adds another €0.40. The customer pays €26.40, an increase of €2.40, while the seller absorbs €2 of the handling fee.

The arithmetic makes the distinction visible. Customs receives €4 in additional handling fees in this example. The buyer funds €2 of that cost through the price and pays €0.40 of additional VAT. The seller funds the remaining €2. The €0.40 is a consequence of the assumed taxable price increase; adding the July duty again would distort the comparison.

Change the assumption and the outcome changes. Full absorption leaves the assumed VAT-inclusive price at €24 and reduces the seller’s margin by €4. Full pass-through adds €4 before VAT and €0.80 of VAT, producing €28.80. Neither endpoint is a forecast. The calculator below exposes these relationships, provided its line count is treated as an input already established elsewhere, rather than as a customs classification service.

The same fixed fee also weighs differently across merchandise values. Holding the line count constant, a €4 cost is a much larger challenge to a €20 sale than to a €100 sale. That simple proportion helps explain why cheap, varied baskets deserve particular attention. It does not establish how much any individual platform will charge.

## Follow the total, and keep the transaction in view

The adopted rules establish a new cost from 1 November. The evidence available on 30 October also shows businesses with several ways to respond: price presentation at checkout, promotions, rerouting and stock held within Europe. Each piece answers a different question. None yields a single, observed consumer pass-through rate for a fee that has yet to apply.

For a meaningful comparison, follow the same goods, quantities and purchase conditions through to the final amount payable. Keep public charges, VAT and private handling fees visible, and establish whether the order triggered an import or drew on stock already cleared into the EU. That is where a small parcel becomes an economic question: who has room to adjust the transaction, and who ends up paying for the adjustment?
