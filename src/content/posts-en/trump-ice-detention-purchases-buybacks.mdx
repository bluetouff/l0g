On April 13, 2026, Donald Trump’s reported portfolio bought shares in CoreCivic. At 5 p.m. Washington time that day, a confidential review of the company’s Appleton detention center was scheduled to end. The government bought the site in August for $495.6 million. The April date appears in the sale agreement filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The trade appears in Trump’s own financial disclosure. [S04](#source-S04) [S38](#source-S38) [S41](#source-S41)

Appleton was one of eight facilities the administration bought from CoreCivic and GEO Group, the two operators whose shares Trump had reported holding. The sellers retained the operating contracts. In Tacoma, GEO announced a planned share buyback the day after selling to the government. [S04](#source-S04) [S07](#source-S07) [S12](#source-S12) [S13](#source-S13) [S20](#source-S20)

*The Trump Clan’s War Chest, part four.* Earlier stories covered [federal money redirected to detention](/en/analysis/trump-frozen-funds-federal-transfers/) and [Washington’s power to authorize payments from other accounts](/en/analysis/trump-venezuela-oil-custody-accounts/).

## Washington buys the buildings

Congress gave Immigration and Customs Enforcement, or ICE, $45 billion in July 2025 to expand detention capacity. The funds are available through September 30, 2029. ICE used the appropriation to fund existing centers during partial government shutdowns. Having money available for several years gives the agency room to plan property deals beyond the annual budget. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

ICE first pursued warehouses it could convert. Officials told congressional auditors that seven of the eleven purchased properties were slated for resale. Buying an operating center can make space available sooner. ICE officials also said federal ownership gives the agency more leeway in areas where state or local authorities oppose expansion. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) wants the agency to assess the full costs over the life of the facilities before it proceeds. [S02](#source-S02) [S03](#source-S03)

The sellers’ SEC filings document six completed sales between July and November, covering **eight facilities and $3.609 billion** in gross prices. CoreCivic sold four sites. GEO sold the three properties in its Adelanto complex together, then Tacoma. CoreCivic still runs the four sites and expects to adjust its contracts to reflect the change in ownership. GEO still runs Adelanto. Assigning each deal to a specific appropriation would require the department’s own accounting records. ## GEO keeps its customer in Tacoma

The sale agreement describes the assets and provides for payment at closing through an escrow agent. The center and related assets passed to the government. The operating contract is separate. It took effect in October and is expected to run through 2036. GEO forecasts about $140 million in annual revenue. ICE can terminate the contract, including if funding runs out. [S12](#source-S12) [S14](#source-S14) [S36](#source-S36)

<TrumpDetentionFigure lang="en" kind="ownership" />

The $453 million price in Tacoma drew a [request for an investigation](#source-S15) from Senator Patty Murray. GEO carried the center at about $60 million on its books at the end of September. Pierce County upheld a combined $75.7 million assessment for two parcels after appeals by GEO subsidiaries. The sale agreement covers a broader set of property and related assets. The book and tax figures are useful reference points with different scopes. The procurement appraisal remains the document to obtain. [S10](#source-S10) [S15](#source-S15) [S36](#source-S36) [S42](#source-S42)

In **Appleton**, Minnesota’s register confirms a $495.6 million price and lists a $14.75 million county assessment for the two parcels. The filer entered the exact sale price in the field for the buyer-paid appraisal. CoreCivic attributes the figure to appraisers hired by the federal government and cites replacement costs and depreciation. The public entry gives the figure. The full appraisal would show how it was calculated. Minnesota lawmakers have also asked for records of an earlier $75 million offer that fell through and included work to restore the facility to service, according to an email they cited. [S04](#source-S04) [S37](#source-S37) [S40](#source-S40) [S51](#source-S51)

In California, *CalMatters* reported assessments of $171.5 million at **California City** and $164.9 million at **Otay Mesa**, against purchase prices of $732.6 million and $739.2 million. At **Adelanto**, where three sites sold together for $950 million, Representative Norma Torres asked the inspector general to review the negotiations and any role of a former GEO executive who joined ICE. At **Leavenworth**, the filing gives a price of $238.4 million and the city has raised local oversight issues. We found no comparably documented appraisal reference for that sale. [S04](#source-S04) [S07](#source-S07) [S43](#source-S43) [S44](#source-S44) [S45](#source-S45)

The GAO identified a program-wide problem. ICE provided no economic analysis to support its decisions to buy and sell warehouses. Nine of eleven **warehouses**, distinct from the eight detention centers above, cost at least 50% more than their tax assessments. The finding documents a gap in the review. Estimating any overpayment takes a comparison of the price, assets and terms of each contract. [S02](#source-S02)

## The sellers get cash

CoreCivic says it received about $1.6 billion from its four sales after taxes and costs. It put some of that money into an [accelerated share repurchase](/en/glossary/rachat-accelere-d-actions/). It paid a dealer $500 million and received an initial delivery of shares. The final number depends on the stock price over the period set in the agreement. The company also reports repaying more than $700 million in debt. [S04](#source-S04) [S05](#source-S05) [S06](#source-S06)

GEO used the two transactions differently. After Adelanto it redeemed $650 million in bond principal. It expects about $340 million net from Tacoma and plans to add operating cash flow for a $500 million accelerated buyback. As of November 26, the latest public records still describe that buyback as planned. [S09](#source-S09) [S11](#source-S11) [S13](#source-S13)

<TrumpDetentionFigure lang="en" kind="buybacks" />

For shareholders who keep their stock, a buyback can increase their stake in the company. The effect depends in part on how many shares remain outstanding after the transaction. The sale proceeds go to the company. Establishing how much a particular shareholder benefits would require knowing their position at the time. [S04](#source-S04) [S06](#source-S06)

### The confidential review in Appleton

The Appleton agreement traces the review to a February 26 access and confidentiality agreement between CoreCivic and SK2 LLC. On that date, the portfolio reported a sale of CoreCivic shares. It then bought between $15,001 and $50,000 of the stock on April 13, the scheduled deadline for the government to walk away on the basis of its review. The stock filing gives the date without an execution time. The next CoreCivic trade we located was a sale on May 15. [S21](#source-S21) [S22](#source-S22) [S38](#source-S38) [S41](#source-S41)

The February sale also fell within the confidential review period for CoreCivic’s California City and Otay Mesa sites. Their agreements cite a February 1 access agreement date and a March 3 deadline. In Leavenworth, the access agreement was dated April 21, eight days after the CoreCivic purchase. [S21](#source-S21) [S53](#source-S53) [S54](#source-S54) [S55](#source-S55)

Appleton had been discussed as a possible ICE site since 2025. In June, *Sahan Journal* published February correspondence about a possible operating contract and zoning. We found no public notice of a firm property purchase or its terms before the April 13 trade. The SEC published the sale agreement on August 5. [S41](#source-S41) [S58](#source-S58) [S60](#source-S60)

GEO presents a different timeline. The portfolio bought GEO on March 4. In May, GEO publicly disclosed talks about possible sales to ICE. The portfolio sold GEO shares on July 29, the day GEO announced a five-year ICE services contract to reactivate its Rivers facility. The filing gives no time of day for the trade. GEO still owned Rivers. Its property sales in Adelanto and Tacoma came later. The latest periodic report we located was signed November 1 and covers trades through September 24. Searches of the available text of the later reports turned up no additional trades in either stock. The filings give ranges for individual transactions. Holdings and any realized gain require account statements. [S04](#source-S04) [S07](#source-S07) [S12](#source-S12) [S21](#source-S21) [S39](#source-S39) [S46](#source-S46) [S47](#source-S47) [S52](#source-S52) [S59](#source-S59) [S61](#source-S61)

The White House told *The Daily Beast* that outside institutions manage the portfolio independently. It said automated models track indexes and cited the Schwab 1000 as an example. The filing for the ETF tracking the Schwab 1000 lists neither CoreCivic nor GEO among its May 31 holdings, though both appear in other Schwab funds. Schwab offers several indexing strategies. The account mandate would identify the model used for these trades. [S25](#source-S25) [S62](#source-S62) [S63](#source-S63)

US insider trading rules apply to the president. The shared dates at Appleton make it possible to test whether whoever decided on the trade knew material information about the purchase while it was still private. In August, members of Congress including Elizabeth Warren and Robert Garcia requested account mandates and records explaining how trades were generated. To reconstruct April 13, the key records are the timestamped order and communications between the acquisition team and anyone involved in making the trade. ## Brokerage fees and missing records

The Appleton and Tacoma contracts identify CBRE as the sellers’ broker and put the commissions on the sellers. A separate agreement governs the amount. Reuters has also reported a criminal investigation into former adviser Corey Lewandowski following a referral from the DHS inspector general. NBC reported allegations that he sought payment tied to GEO’s contracts and that GEO refused. His representative denies any request or payment. A letter from Robert Garcia calls for preservation of records. None of the public records we reviewed ties a payment in that matter to the six sales. [S36](#source-S36) [S41](#source-S41) [S48](#source-S48) [S49](#source-S49) [S50](#source-S50)

For people in detention, inspections matter in daily life. In a separate November audit, GAO said ICE still needed to assess the effects of changes to its inspections. [S33](#source-S33)

In Tacoma, federal ownership can last well beyond the deadline for committing detention funds. GEO can keep earning management revenue for as long as the contract runs. To judge whether the government got a good deal, the purchase price has to be weighed alongside the cost of owning the building and the bill for running it. Only then can taxpayers assess the price Washington paid to take control of the site. [S02](#source-S02) [S12](#source-S12) [S14](#source-S14)
