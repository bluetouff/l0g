Imagine an ordinary commute. A driver leaves a car park in the morning, accelerates onto a main road, brakes hard at a junction and arrives at work. The car can generate very different kinds of information along the way: speed, acceleration, diagnostic codes and [location](/en/glossary/donnees-de-localisation/). [Telematics](/en/glossary/telematique/) covers the remote exchange of such information. [S05](#source-s05) Some information may stay on board. Some may travel to remote services, depending on the vehicle and the settings. Once it leaves the car, it may also cross from one business relationship into another.

In the United States, that commercial journey has led to a series of proceedings involving General Motors and OnStar, its connected-vehicle service. On October 29, 2026, **36 attorneys general** announced a settlement providing for **$220 million in payments to participating states**. Their investigation concerned driving data sold to brokers serving the insurance market. The announced settlement amount is separate from the revenue generated by those sales. [S11](#source-s11)[S12](#source-s12)

The case involves **Smart Driver**, a feature that offered driving feedback. Authorities challenged how drivers were enrolled and informed about data sharing. The **Federal Trade Commission (FTC)**, the federal consumer-protection agency, and California had already reached separate settlements. The documents examined here set out settlement terms and allegations attributed to the authorities. [S01](#source-s01)[S02](#source-s02)[S03](#source-s03)[S11](#source-s11)

## From a braking event to a commercial product

Inside the car, sudden braking is an observation. In a data supplier's system, repeated observations can become inputs to a driving score. An insurer may regard the score as potentially useful when estimating risk, subject to applicable law and the quality of the model. These are different transformations, performed by different actors for different purposes.

In the GM case, the FTC challenged the sign-up process for OnStar and Smart Driver and the sale of driving behavior and precise location information. The final order was issued on January 13, 2026 and announced the following day. It prohibits disclosure of covered driving and location data to consumer reporting agencies for five years. Other provisions address consent, access, deletion and the ability to disable collection within the order’s overall twenty-year term. Exceptions for certain processing requested by a driver apply to those other provisions; the five-year disclosure ban remains in force. [S01](#source-s01)[S02](#source-s02)

According to the California authorities, GM supplied Verisk and LexisNexis Risk Solutions with data that the firms intended to use in products for automobile insurers. The California DOJ said GM reportedly made about **$20 million nationwide** from these sales. The figure describes the cumulative proceeds attributed to the sales at issue nationwide. [S03](#source-s03)[S04](#source-s04)

The two deliveries were materially different. The California complaint, re-examined for this article, describes the difference: the Verisk contract called for identity-linked driving information while excluding precise GPS coordinates. The same complaint says LexisNexis also received precise GPS information, which it did not use to calculate driving scores. The fields a broker acquires, the fields it actually models, and the output an insurer buys must be kept separate. [S04](#source-s04)

## Connecting a data transfer to an insurance decision

A commercial transfer and an insurance decision are separate steps. The public record documents sales to two suppliers, their intended market and alleged consequences for some US consumers. Attributing an individual premium increase requires the report supplied, the insurer’s actual use of it and other rating factors. A complete customer list and the insurers’ pricing models remain outside the documents examined here. [S01](#source-s01)[S02](#source-s02)

California offers an especially important qualification. The state's investigation said insurers had not requested reports concerning California drivers and did not find premium increases there attributable to GM's data sales. The state has restrictions on using driving data for rate-setting. That finding does not erase the privacy issue: a contested disclosure can matter even without a demonstrated price increase. [S03](#source-s03) [S04](#source-s04)

There are two questions here. Did personal information travel beyond the service consumers thought they were using? And did the information subsequently affect an insurer's decision? The first can be investigated through transfer records, contracts and notices. Answering the second requires evidence of the downstream report, the insurer's actual decision process and other relevant rating factors.

In a statement published on April 24, GM said it had stopped sharing with LexisNexis and Verisk on **March 20, 2024**. The FTC announced its case on January 16, 2025, then announced the final order on January 14, 2026; the order itself had been issued the previous day. California announced a settlement on May 8, 2026, including **$12.75 million in civil penalties** and obligations concerning data governance and deletion. The proposed civil penalty is separate from the roughly $20 million in reported sales proceeds. [S01](#source-s01)[S02](#source-s02)[S03](#source-s03)[S04](#source-s04)[S13](#source-s13)

The **October 29, 2026** announcement adds a **$220 million** settlement with the attorneys-general coalition. The agreement copy examined here specifies an effective date of **November 30, 2026**, followed by payment to the states within thirty days. That schedule lies ahead of this article’s October 31 cutoff. The agreement also establishes data-protection requirements and oversight of dealership enrolment; GM settles the allegations without admitting liability. The three amounts describe separate transactions and proceedings. [S11](#source-s11)[S12](#source-s12)

The dates matter because the transfers at issue are historical. Their contemporary significance is the model they reveal: a consumer-facing feature can generate inputs for a much less visible commercial market.

## Europe has a different legal structure

In France, the analysis starts with EU rules. The **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** governs personal-data processing, while **ePrivacy** rules can also apply to accessing information stored in connected equipment, including vehicles. The European Data Protection Board's connected-vehicle guidance favors local processing where feasible and data minimization. The applicable legal basis and safeguards depend on the purpose and the specific processing activity. [S07](#source-s07)

In June 2026, France’s data-protection authority, the **CNIL**, issued recommendations on connected-vehicle location data. Travel histories can reveal highly personal routines and frequented places. Its document covers private owners and lessees and distinguishes legal obligations, recommendations and good practices. It addresses minimisation, retention, security and changes of user. Employee vehicles require a separate analysis. [S05](#source-s05)[S06](#source-s06)

The European Data Act has applied since September 12, 2025. It introduces rules governing access to data generated by connected products, including vehicles, and can help independent providers offer repair or maintenance services. Personal-data protection continues to apply. Vehicle-specific guidance issued by the Commission on the same day explains that access covers readily available raw or preprocessed data. The guidance lists driving scores among examples of inferred or derived data, produced by analysis that creates new insights and excluded from that chapter of the Data Act. The original observations and the commercial assessment therefore retain different legal treatment. [S08](#source-s08)[S09](#source-s09)

A separate date matters for newer vehicles: the requirement to design connected products and related services for data access applies to those placed on the market after **September 12, 2026**. That timetable is distinct from the regulation’s general application since September 2025. [S14](#source-s14)

## Match data collection to the service

Roadside assistance may need location when help is requested. A repair service may need a fault code. Mileage-based insurance may need distance traveled rather than a full map of every journey. These examples illustrate why the amount and detail of data collected should reflect a defined purpose.

Practical control goes beyond a checkbox. Drivers need intelligible information on recipients, purposes, retention and the consequences of their choices. They can explore applicable access and deletion rights and the vehicle's settings, while recognising that some safety functions have distinct requirements. CNIL also highlights the importance of clearing or disconnecting user data when a vehicle changes hands. [S05](#source-s05)[S06](#source-s06)

## Contracts and decisions to examine in France

An investigation into the French market would need contracts and evidence: the actual data categories shared by manufacturers and telematics companies, named recipients, insurer statements about variables they use, and where appropriate technical examinations of data flows. Any claim that a particular driver's premium was influenced would then require a documented decision path. That final step would connect the technical data flows to their consequences for drivers.

The GM case shows what happens when the economics of connected services become detached from a driver's understanding of the product. The durable question is who controls the journey of the data once it leaves the vehicle.
