Five ships a day is the scale of the passage proposed by the UN official overseeing efforts to move fertilizer through the Strait of Hormuz. Speaking to Reuters in Bangkok on 14 October, UNOPS Executive Director Jorge Moreira da Silva said a mechanism could be activated within seven to ten days of an agreement between Washington and Tehran. At the time of the interview, that agreement was still missing. He argued that five daily sailings could sustain fertilizer supplies to countries in critical need. <sup><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source S01">[S01]</a></sup>

The warning acquired a more immediate dimension the following day. Alvaro Lario, president of the International Fund for Agricultural Development, told Reuters that the disruption was beginning to show up in harvests. He described more cautious planting decisions and reduced fertilizer use. It is a significant assessment from the agency’s leadership, although the report does not provide country-level estimates separating the effect of Hormuz from other pressures on yields. <sup><a href="#source-s16" aria-label="Source S16">[S16]</a></sup>

A protected passage could remove a major obstacle. Between a ship clearing the strait and a crop receiving the nutrients it needs, however, lies a chain of manufacturing, financing and distribution. The contents of the hold matter as much as its weight. So do the buyer’s access to credit and the stage of the crop when the shipment arrives. The most serious risk emerges where these constraints coincide, even when the international fertilizer price has stopped rising.

Our [investigation of shipping chokepoints and inventories](/en/analysis/shipping-chokepoints-cost-of-buying-time/) follows goods in transit and their financing. Fertilizer adds a crop-specific deadline: supplies need to arrive during a useful application window.

## A shipping corridor needs a functioning chain ashore

The task force announced in March brings together UNOPS, UN Trade and Development, the International Maritime Organization and the International Chamber of Commerce. Its humanitarian purpose is pursued through the movement of commercial fertilizer and related raw materials. Buyers still need to finance those cargoes. Facilitating navigation and distributing free inputs are very different operations. <sup><a href="#source-s18" aria-label="Source S18">[S18]</a></sup>

UNOPS described the proposed mechanism in July as temporary and limited, with vessel registration, transit coordination, prioritization, monitoring and verification. The plan outlined to Reuters in October adds a coordination centre in Oman and UN monitors checking loading at Gulf ports, followed by tracking during the voyage. The purpose is to make movements identifiable and acceptable to the parties, reducing the uncertainty that obstructs commercial traffic. <sup><a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source S02">[S02]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source S01">[S01]</a></sup>

Such an arrangement addresses an essential constraint while leaving insurance, payment and distribution to be resolved. ICC has separately advocated clearer rules for eligible voyages, an insurance facility and financing for importers, distributors and farmers. Those are proposals from a body representing business, rather than evidence that a fully funded system is already in place. <sup><a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source S15">[S15]</a></sup>

The five-ship figure becomes meaningful only when accompanied by cargo information. Vessel capacity, loading rates, products and destinations would all be needed before translating sailings into hectares served. The seven-to-ten-day estimate concerns activation after an agreement. The voyage to the importing country, any further processing and delivery to agricultural districts come later. None of those downstream stages has a verified duration in the public description of the plan. <sup><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source S01">[S01]</a></sup>

## Urea, phosphates and sulfur serve different needs

Fertilizer is a broad commercial category. [Urea](/en/glossary/uree/) supplies nitrogen, while ammonium phosphates supply both nitrogen and phosphorus. Crops need the relevant nutrient, rather than an interchangeable quantity of material. The industries producing those nutrients also share inputs, allowing a disruption in the Gulf to affect plants well beyond it. <sup><a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source S05">[S05]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source S09">[S09]</a></sup>

In the conventional gas-based route, natural gas provides hydrogen and process energy to make ammonia from nitrogen in the air. Ammonia then feeds the production of several fertilizers, including urea. Coal offers another industrial route. The ability to source nitrogen elsewhere therefore depends on energy resources and installed manufacturing capacity, as well as access to ships. <sup><a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source S05">[S05]</a></sup>

Sulfur introduces a less visible connection. Industrial supplies of elemental sulfur are recovered during oil refining and natural-gas purification. Converted into sulfuric acid, it is used to process phosphate rock into phosphoric acid. Adding ammonia produces fertilizers such as diammonium phosphate, or [DAP](/en/glossary/dap/). A producer outside the Gulf can consequently have phosphate rock and willing customers while remaining exposed through its sulfur purchases. <sup><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">[S04]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s06" aria-label="Source S06">[S06]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s07" aria-label="Source S07">[S07]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup>

Our [analysis of sulfur across fertilizers and nickel](/en/analysis/sulfur-gulf-crisis-fertilizers-nickel/) follows those industrial uses and their shared dependencies.

Counting tonnes requires care even after manufacturing is complete. One hundred tonnes of standard-grade urea contain 46 tonnes of nitrogen. The same weight of DAP 18-46-0 contains 18 tonnes of nitrogen, alongside 46 tonnes of available phosphate conventionally stated as P₂O₅ equivalent. The second number does not mean 46 tonnes of elemental phosphorus. A heavier shipment need not provide more of the nutrient that a particular crop requires. Its usefulness depends on composition and the condition of the soil. <sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source S09">[S09]</a></sup>

For a passage with limited capacity, this creates a practical allocation problem. Shipping sulfur may support fertilizer plants downstream, but it leaves an industrial step to complete before nutrients reach farms. Shipping urea can address a nitrogen requirement more directly. Choosing between them requires information about inventories, processing capacity and the crop seasons each cargo would serve. A universal ranking of “essential” fertilizer cargoes would miss those differences.

## Urea has retreated while DAP has held up

Recent prices challenge the idea of a uniform shortage. The World Bank’s *Pink Sheet*, released on 2 October, records average urea prices falling from **$693.5 a tonne in the second quarter to $399.2 in the third**, a calculated decline of **42.4%**. DAP moves in the opposite direction, from **$759.5 to $791.8**, an increase of **4.3%**. These are quarterly averages through September, not market quotes for 16 October. <sup><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source S03">[S03]</a></sup>

Calculation: (Q3/Q2 − 1) × 100, giving −42.4% and +4.3%. FOB Middle East and US Gulf are different delivery bases. July–September monthly points are not current market quotes.</p></details>
</HormuzFertilizerFigure>

The delivery bases also matter. Despite the legacy series label “Urea (E. Europe)”, the World Bank’s notes define its benchmark as spot prilled urea, free on board in the Middle East. DAP is quoted free on board in the US Gulf. Neither measures the bill at the farm gate. The comparison reveals different price paths for different products and locations, rather than the relative cost of an identical nutrient delivered to the same buyer. <sup><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source S03">[S03]</a></sup>

Prices can fall because supplies recover, inventories are released or buyers postpone purchases. They can also fall when customers lose the ability to pay. ICC’s policy note explicitly discusses demand that has been abandoned **or deferred**. Treating those categories as equivalent would turn a delayed purchase into a permanent loss of fertilizer application. Establishing which has occurred requires evidence from the use of inputs, rather than an export price alone. <sup><a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source S15">[S15]</a></sup>

AMIS adds a useful recent qualification. Its October monitor reports nitrogen prices firming in September, continuing affordability constraints on phosphate demand and a more comfortable potash market. The monitor uses nutrient-tonne measures and benchmark baskets different from the World Bank’s product-tonne series. Together, the sources show why each market needs to be followed separately, and why urea’s large quarterly decline does not imply an uninterrupted fall from month to month. <sup><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source S13">[S13]</a></sup>

## Upstream dependencies follow alternative suppliers

An IFPRI analysis published on 18 September offers a view of the trade adjustment behind these prices. Its authors reconstruct flows for the first six months of 2026 using declarations from 77 importing countries for nitrogen fertilizers. They also estimate sulfur trade through mirror data, without stating a separate reporter count for that series. This “mirror” approach helps estimate exports from suppliers whose own reporting is unavailable or incomplete. It describes a monitored trade universe, with coverage limitations, rather than a census of every international cargo. <sup><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">[S04]</a></sup>

Within that universe, nitrogen-fertilizer exports measured in nitrogen equivalents decline by about 9% from January–June 2025. Elemental sulfur exports fall from approximately 13.5 million tonnes to 9 million tonnes, a reduction of about one-third. The contrast points to uneven adjustment across the two supply chains. Constraints outside the Gulf also contribute to the sulfur decline, so attributing the entire reduction to Hormuz would overstate the evidence. <sup><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">[S04]</a></sup>

<details><summary>Method and scope</summary><p>IFPRI-attributed estimates for January–June 2026 against 2025. Each family has its own 2025 base of 100: about 91 for nitrogen and 67 for sulfur. Sulfur falls from approximately 13.5 to 9 million material tonnes; nitrogen uses nutrient equivalents. The 77 reporters explicitly concern nitrogen, with no separate sulfur reporter count stated. </HormuzFertilizerFigure>

Increased nitrogen shipments from alternative suppliers show that trade is adapting. They reveal less about final use. A country may import more to replace lost domestic production, rebuild inventories or prepare for a crop season. Lower imports can reflect sufficient stocks, a change in sourcing that the data capture imperfectly, or postponed purchasing. Customs figures cannot by themselves establish fertilizer application, still less the resulting yield. <sup><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">[S04]</a></sup>

Sulfur deserves particular attention because switching the supplier of finished fertilizer can preserve the same upstream exposure. Buying phosphate from a more distant plant avoids a particular shipping route, but offers less protection when that plant is also struggling to secure sulfur. The customer encounters the limiting input through another branch of the chain. Industrial chemistry explains that possibility; the trade evidence makes it plausible without establishing the operating rate of every affected plant. <sup><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">[S04]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s07" aria-label="Source S07">[S07]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup>

## Bangladesh is already financing the next crop

The agricultural calendar turns a delivery problem into a production risk. FAO warned on 7 May that delayed fertilizer could miss a useful application stage and affect subsequent harvests. There is no single global deadline: it depends on the crop, its development, weather and soil fertility. A late shipment can retain value without being equally useful for the operation or season it was originally intended to support. <sup><a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source S10">[S10]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup>

Bangladesh provides a concrete example of the financing needed to bridge that gap. On 26 June, the World Bank approved a **$300 million** food-security project intended to finance imports of **600,000 tonnes of fertilizer**, half of it urea. The announcement identifies the Aman season, July–October 2026, and Boro, October 2026–April 2027. Those are the seasons supported by the programme, rather than precise planting dates for every rice field. <sup><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">[S11]</a></sup>

</HormuzFertilizerFigure>

By mid-October, the programme sits at the junction between the two seasons. Funding is meant to prepare one crop while the other is under way. Assessing delivery would require import contracts, unloading dates and records of supplies reaching distributors and farms. The June announcement establishes a financing envelope and targets. It does not certify that the intended volumes have already arrived or been applied. <sup><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">[S11]</a></sup>

Current harvest assessments need to be placed on the same calendar. FAO’s 4 September country brief still forecasts above-average paddy production in Bangladesh and notes that the 2026 Boro harvest finished in June. That completed crop is distinct from the 2026–2027 Boro season the financing is intended to support. Relatively favourable past production can coexist with difficult purchasing decisions for the next crop. <sup><a href="#source-s12" aria-label="Source S12">[S12]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">[S11]</a></sup>

The timing gap also exists within countries. A farmer who bought early may have adequate stocks, while another who relies on the proceeds of the preceding harvest is still waiting to purchase. National import dependence is a useful starting point. Where the stocks are held, and when individual growers can afford to buy them, move the analysis closer to actual agricultural exposure.

## Credit determines access to fertilizer

The economics of fertilizer begin before application. A farmer weighs its cost against the extra crop revenue it may generate, subject to the cash available to make the purchase. AMIS tracks fertilizer-to-crop price ratios and financing conditions for precisely this reason. A tonne that becomes cheaper in dollars can remain hard to afford when the local currency weakens, freight rises or credit becomes scarcer. <sup><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source S13">[S13]</a></sup>

An illustrative calculation makes the transmission clear without assigning it to a fictional country. Suppose a fertilizer’s dollar price rises by 25%, while buying a dollar costs 10% more in local currency. Excluding all other costs, the local price is multiplied by 1.25 and then by 1.10, an increase of **37.5%**. An unchanged purchasing budget now buys **72.7%** of the original volume, a reduction of **27.3%**.

<HormuzFertilizerFigure lang="en" kind="timing" number="06" sources="" caption="Illustrative arithmetic, not a country estimate or forecast: dollar price +25%, local-currency cost of one dollar +10%, purchasing budget unchanged. Other costs are excluded. Lower purchasing volume does not imply an equivalent yield loss.">
<details><summary>Method and scope</summary><p>Entirely hypothetical inputs: dollar price +25%, local cost of one dollar +10%, purchasing budget unchanged. Local unit cost: 1.25 × 1.10 = 1.375. Purchasable quantity: 1/1.375 = 72.7% of the original volume, a 27.3% reduction. Freight, taxes, subsidies and credit costs excluded. No numerical crop-yield response is estimated.</p></details>
</HormuzFertilizerFigure>

This is purchasing-power arithmetic, with explicit assumptions. It provides no automatic estimate of lost output. Soil reserves, earlier applications, placement and the crop’s response all affect the yield obtained from an additional unit of fertilizer. Research on phosphorus shows that the response to application depends on the nutrient already available in the soil. Cutting an excessive dose and missing an essential one have different consequences. <sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup>

A subsidy that holds down the farmer’s price can transfer part of the shock to the public budget. External finance can in turn ease that constraint. The timing remains important: funds must be available early enough for orders and distribution. Money arriving after the useful application window can still support incomes or a later crop, but cannot necessarily recover its original value for the season that has passed.

France’s official release of 15 October says the G7 has mobilized **US$2.5 billion since the Middle East crisis began**, almost entirely as grants, under its fertilizer and food-systems resilience action plan, particularly in Africa. This is a reported cumulative amount, with no basis for treating it as wholly new funding on 15 October. The release provides no country-by-country or instrument-by-instrument disbursement schedule. <sup><a href="#source-s17" aria-label="Source S17">[S17]</a></sup>

It would be premature to add that reported amount to the Bangladesh programme: their scopes and any overlaps would first need to be checked. Instead, the two announcements illustrate different levels of the response. One reports cumulative collective funding; the other identifies a national programme, a target quantity and the crop seasons it is intended to serve. Monitoring needs to move from those commitments to purchasing, and then to usable deliveries. <sup><a href="#source-s17" aria-label="Source S17">[S17]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s11" aria-label="Source S11">[S11]</a></sup>

## The 81% increase rests on a severe scenario

Recent coverage cites a possible 81% increase in cereal prices from a study commissioned by ICC. The organization’s 18 August press release describes that figure relative to **2020 levels**. Its most severe scenario assumes nitrogen-fertilizer costs triple in 2026 and remain elevated, alongside shocks to energy, transport and other inputs. These are stylized cost assumptions, rather than a result calculated from the proposed five daily ships. <sup><a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source S14">[S14]</a></sup>

ICC identifies the work as an early-stage preprint that has not been peer reviewed. The full manuscript could not be accessed for this investigation; the assumptions described here come from the sponsor’s release and methodological notes. Those notes also describe model results measured against a no-shock reference scenario. Each figure’s exact comparison therefore needs to be checked before it is carried into a new claim. <sup><a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source S14">[S14]</a></sup>

Such a simulation can explore the consequences of a persistent shock. It does not establish an 81% rise from October’s market prices or attribute the entire increase to the strait’s disruption alone. The distinction between a modelling exercise and a dated forecast matters particularly when observed urea prices have already changed direction so sharply. <sup><a href="#source-s14" aria-label="Source S14">[S14]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source S03">[S03]</a></sup>

Our [analysis of wheat between Hormuz and the Bosphorus](/en/analysis/wheat-between-hormuz-and-the-bosphorus/) also separates cereal availability from the ability to buy and transport it.

## A comfortable global balance can conceal vulnerable farms

Agricultural evidence still offers substantial reasons to resist a generalized-shortage scenario. AMIS’s October monitor describes global supply prospects as generally favourable, despite downward revisions for some crops and weather risks. Alternative suppliers, inventories and scope to adjust fertilizer use can absorb part of the disruption. Their effectiveness varies with the input, the soil and the buyer’s financial capacity. <sup><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source S13">[S13]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s04" aria-label="Source S04">[S04]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup>

A relatively comfortable global balance is nevertheless a poor measure of every household’s access to food. FAO’s Bangladesh brief combines an above-average paddy production forecast with a projected deterioration in food security, linked in part to purchasing power and flood damage. Adequate national output can coexist with severe difficulties paying for food locally. <sup><a href="#source-s12" aria-label="Source S12">[S12]</a></sup>

The evidence points toward specific purchasing constraints and agricultural windows, rather than a uniform collapse in harvests. The risk is greatest where a grower reaches a sensitive crop stage with inadequate stocks, a poorly suited replacement and no financing available. Supplies secured early enough, soil reserves that can cushion reduced application or accessible seasonal credit can interrupt that transmission. <sup><a href="#source-s10" aria-label="Source S10">[S10]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source S08">[S08]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s13" aria-label="Source S13">[S13]</a></sup>

As of 16 October, the sources reviewed offer no public confirmation that the proposed UN passage has been activated. Its introduction would be an important advance. Its agricultural impact will need to be assessed beyond the number of crossings: which products and nutrients move, where they go, when distributors can supply them, what growers pay, and whether application takes place while it remains useful. Five daily ships could reopen a route. Their contribution to the harvest depends on the chain waiting for them ashore. <sup><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source S01">[S01]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s02" aria-label="Source S02">[S02]</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source S15">[S15]</a></sup>
