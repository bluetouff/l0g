For a merchant selling on Amazon, the commission is sometimes only one line on the statement. Amazon charges a digital services fee on certain selling and fulfilment fees. A platform liable for a tax can charge the merchant more. The merchant then has to decide how much to absorb and how much to pass on. Economists call that division of the cost [tax incidence](/en/glossary/incidence-fiscale/). The terms of a seller’s contract help determine where it lands. [S06](#source-S06)

The same question now faces the European Union. On **21 November 2026**, European Council President António Costa proposed using a [digital services tax](/en/glossary/taxe-sur-les-services-numeriques/) to help finance the EU budget for **2028 to 2034**. His draft conclusions form the basis for talks at the **26–27 November** summit. As of **24 November**, the levy remains a proposal. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02) · [S03](#source-S03)

## The revenues the draft would tax

Paragraph 145 proposes an annual levy of **3% of net turnover** from online advertising, intermediation and marketplaces, and the monetisation of user data. A company would have to exceed both **€750 million in annual global turnover** and **€50 million in annual EU digital turnover**. Global turnover determines whether a company clears one of the thresholds. The levy would apply to the revenue from the services in scope. [S01](#source-S01)

<DigitalServicesTaxFigure kind="scope" lang="en" />

An online purchase helps explain the distinction. The merchant receives payment for the item and the platform charges for its service. The Commission’s **2018** draft directive could tax the marketplace’s commission. It excluded the value of the goods changing hands and direct sales through a retailer’s own website. Costa’s draft calls for talks to resume on that directive. The final wording will determine where those boundaries lie. [S01](#source-S01) · [S04](#source-S04)

A levy on revenue takes a bigger bite out of a business with slim margins. On a hypothetical **€100** of taxable revenue, a **€3** charge would absorb three fifths of an initial **€5** profit, or 15% of a **€20** profit. The example holds revenue and operating costs steady to show how the base affects the burden. The Commission’s 2018 proposal justified taxing revenue by pointing to users’ role in generating value and the difficulty of assigning that value to their countries. [S04](#source-S04)

## Sellers and advertisers already see surcharges

Amazon publishes a schedule of fees tied to national digital services taxes. It lists a **3%** surcharge on selling and Fulfilment by Amazon fees for a France-based merchant using its French store. Rates and covered fees vary with the seller’s location and the store. On a hypothetical **€15** commission for a **€100** sale, that surcharge would add **45 cents**. [S06](#source-S06)

<DigitalServicesTaxFigure kind="incidence" lang="en" />

Economists Dominika Langenmayr and Rohit Reddy Muddasani tracked the burden through to listed prices. They compared identical products sold directly by Amazon and by merchants that use its fulfilment service. After national taxes took effect, they estimated a relative increase of about **2%** in listed prices for the standard-size products in their sample in France, Spain and the United Kingdom. The corresponding estimate for Italy was too uncertain to draw a conclusion. The results describe that sample and those earlier taxes. An EU-wide tax could change fees and merchant behaviour differently. [S07](#source-S07)

Advertisers can see the charge on their bills as well. Google Ads says its regulatory surcharge for ads served in France will rise from **2% to 3% on 1 January 2027** and ties the charge to the cost of complying with the French tax. Faced with a higher bill, an advertiser can trim its campaigns, move some of its spending or accept a higher cost of reaching customers. We traced the same billing channel in our [report on France’s proposed gigabyte levy](/en/analysis/france-gigabyte-tax-internet/). [S08](#source-S08)

## France already depends on some of the revenue

France has had its tax since **2019**. It charges **3%** on taxable service revenue attributable to France. The **€750 million** global threshold counts revenue from taxable services. For the **€25 million** domestic threshold, the law apportions that revenue according to activity attributable to France. Costa’s draft uses a different scope for its global threshold. [S01](#source-S01) · [S09](#source-S09)

In France’s draft budget for **2027**, line 1430 records **€902 million in actual 2025 receipts**, a **€906 million revised estimate for 2026** and **€981 million projected in the draft for 2027**. The 2025 outturn includes **€0.9 million** in residual state revenue previously presented alongside funds allocated to ARPE, France’s labour relations authority for work platforms. All three amounts are in current euros. [S10](#source-S10)

<DigitalServicesTaxFigure kind="france" lang="en" />

A common levy that replaced national taxes would raise the question of how to handle revenue governments already collect. If the taxes ran alongside one another, the question would be whether both applied to the same services. The draft conclusions set out a budget mechanism. How it would fit with national levies will need more detailed legislation. [S01](#source-S01) · [S10](#source-S10)

## Revenue estimates depend on the tax base

The impact assessment behind the 2018 draft put annual revenue at **€4.7 billion to €6 billion** at **3%**. A study commissioned by the European Parliament uses a much broader base. It estimates **€25.7 billion to €42.9 billion in 2028** at rates of **3% to 5%**. Online retail sales to consumers account for about **70%** of its modelled 2028 revenue. That activity goes beyond the scope of the earlier directive. The estimates use different tax bases and, at the upper end of the newer study, a different rate. Costa’s version still needs an estimate on its own terms. [S04](#source-S04) · [S11](#source-S11)

Costa also puts the annual figure at **around €60 billion** for his proposed package of new and existing revenue streams combined. That figure belongs to the broader budget negotiation. The Commission had previously proposed the **Corporate Resource for Europe**, a lump-sum charge on large companies forecast to raise an annual average of **€6.8 billion** from **2028 to 2034**, at 2025 prices. [S02](#source-S02) · [S12](#source-S12)

## Two decisions requiring unanimity

The Council dropped work on an EU tax in **March 2019** after the compromise had narrowed to digital advertising. A separate route would reallocate some taxing rights over large multinationals to the countries where they have customers. The OECD’s multilateral convention for Amount A of Pillar One is still awaiting opening for signature. [S05](#source-S05) · [S13](#source-S13)

An indirect tax harmonisation directive requires unanimity in the Council. An EU own-resources decision also requires unanimity, followed by approval from every member state under its constitutional rules. Costa is seeking agreement on the directive in **2027**. Under paragraph 141, the digital revenue would flow to the EU from **1 January of the year after the relevant provisions of the directive start to apply**. At the summit, leaders will be negotiating the full funding package. Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babiš has already rejected the budget proposal, citing issues including administrative spending and emissions trading. [S01](#source-S01) · [S03](#source-S03) · [S14](#source-S14) · [S15](#source-S15)

## The US trade precedent

In **December 2019**, the US Trade Representative concluded that the French tax discriminated against US companies and consulted on additional duties of up to **100%**. Washington settled on a **25%** rate for certain French goods in **July 2020**, deferred implementation and suspended the measure in **January 2021**. The case illustrates the US trade procedure known as [Section 301](/en/analysis/tariffs-cash-flow-section-301-litigation/). The US trade report of **March 2026** still lists national digital taxes among its grievances. Those documents establish the US position on existing taxes. An official response to Costa’s draft remains to be established. [S16](#source-S16) · [S17](#source-S17) · [S18](#source-S18) · [S19](#source-S19)

If the levy goes through, the public accounts can tell us how much the EU collects. Following the burden will mean checking platform commissions, advertising surcharges and listed prices for comparable services and products. A vote in Brussels could eventually leave its mark on a merchant’s margin or a shopper’s price tag.
