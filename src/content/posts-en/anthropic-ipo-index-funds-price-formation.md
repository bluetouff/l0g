One portfolio manager may buy Anthropic on a forecast of Claude’s future revenue. Another may buy the same stock to keep a fund close to its index. A third may intend to sell to the second. Their decisions meet in the same order book, alongside holders deciding how much they are willing to sell and at which prices.

The proposed listing of Claude’s developer offers a revealing case. On November 12, Reuters reported an estimate from index rebalancing specialist Intropic: **roughly $19 billion in passive demand**, equivalent to around 28% of the shares offered. The report also describes investors positioning ahead of those expected purchases. Assessing the figure requires understanding the conditions under which the trades might occur. [S01](#source-S01)

As of November 12, the offering is still being prepared. Anthropic has begun its *roadshow*, the presentation of the deal to prospective investors, offering **825 million shares at an indicated $70 to $78 each**. Its November 10 announcement states that the SEC registration statement has yet to become effective. Reuters identifies November 19 as the expected first trading day. [S02](#source-S02) [S03](#source-S03)

## The offering price takes shape before trading begins

During a roadshow, banks gather indications of interest: the quantities investors want, the prices they accept and their interest in the business. They then recommend an offering price to the issuer and allocate the shares. Demand, financial disclosures and market conditions inform that negotiation. The SEC notes that the company, its banks and their clients can have different interests in the outcome. [S04](#source-S04)

Listing day brings a fresh set of decisions. Investors allocated shares in the offering may sell, while others seek to buy. Nasdaq describes a process that reveals orders and the depth available at different prices before trading opens. The first public price emerges from that interaction. It can differ from the price paid by investors in the offering. [S05](#source-S05)

Expectations of index purchases may already affect the interest expressed during the roadshow. They can then influence trading between investors. That is the economic interpretation developed here: a known rule can change the price at which a holder is willing to sell before the rule takes effect.

## Index funds face their own timetable

An index fund structures its portfolio to follow a benchmark. Its manager retains choices about execution while managing the gap between the fund’s returns and those of the index. Funds may hold every constituent, use a representative sample or obtain exposure through derivatives. Assets managed against a benchmark therefore do not all translate into identical purchases of the underlying shares. [S06](#source-S06)

Adding Anthropic would change the target holdings of the funds concerned. Estimating their purchases would require the stock’s assigned weight in each index, the assets actually replicating it, any positions already acquired and the replication methods used. The calculation would also need to avoid counting the same investment vehicle twice and distinguish replicated assets from portfolios merely measured against the benchmark.

This demand is less dependent on an individual assessment of Anthropic’s value than the demand of an investor choosing each company freely. An index manager is primarily seeking exposure consistent with the mandate. Buying early introduces exposure to the stock before it enters the benchmark; waiting may concentrate execution at the moment other funds seek the same shares.

Specialist investors can take the other side of that timetable, accumulating stock in the hope of selling it to index funds later. Their positioning may bring forward part of a price movement and supply shares when the rebalance occurs. The result depends on their purchase price, the eventual demand and other holders’ willingness to sell. A position that becomes too expensive to carry may also be unwound before the expected event.

## Fast entry still has several conditions

Index providers have adapted some procedures for large listings. For Russell US indexes, the May 26 reform normally provides for entry after the close of the fifth trading day for qualifying IPOs, subject in particular to an investable market capitalization threshold. A freeze period can postpone entry until the rebalance. The official notice sets out the conditions and the treatment of shares offered. [S07](#source-S07)

For the Nasdaq-100, an IPO is assessed at the end of its seventh trading day. Among other requirements, it must rank within the 40 largest companies by market capitalization. Entry normally follows the fifteenth trading day. **A calendar provision changes the treatment when that date falls in a reconstitution or rebalance month.** [S08](#source-S08)

Consider the expected listing date, assuming the deal proceeds on that schedule. With trading beginning on **November 19** and the market closed for Thanksgiving on November 26, the seventh trading day would be **November 30** and the fifteenth **December 10**. The annual reconstitution’s reference date is the last trading day of November. In that configuration, the methodology aligns entry with the annual reconstitution, whose rules point to the **December 21 market open**. This is l0g’s conditional calendar calculation; Nasdaq would still need to confirm the stock’s eligibility and inclusion. [S03](#source-S03) [S08](#source-S08) [S09](#source-S09)

The distinction matters for pricing. A later purchase date changes how long an investor must carry a position and the risk incurred along the way.

S&P maintains a separate distinction. Its broad indexes, including the **S&P Total Market Index**, have admission procedures different from those of the **S&P 500**. For the latter, the June 4 decision retained a trading history of at least 12 months before consideration, along with profitability requirements. Any forecast of purchases must identify the exact index each fund follows. [S10](#source-S10)

## Available shares at each price determine execution

[Free float](/en/glossary/flottant/) describes shares considered available to the market under a given set of rules. [Market depth](/en/glossary/profondeur-de-marche/) describes quantities available at different prices at a particular moment. A company can have a substantial free float and few willing sellers at the current price. A holder can also raise an asking price upon anticipating buyers facing a deadline.

The following diagram illustrates a purchase during continuous trading, when a single order can execute at several prices. It shows two fictional order books at the same instant and the same purchase of 100 shares. In the first, all 100 are available at $100. In the second, the buyer takes 20 at $100, 30 at $101 and 50 at $104. The total cost is $10,230, or $102.30 per share. The last traded price is $104. These figures explain execution; they are not observations of Anthropic trading. [S05](#source-S05)

The **additional $230** comes from the fictional distribution of sell orders: 20 × 100 + 30 × 101 + 50 × 104, compared with 100 × 100. A real order book changes during execution. Orders arrive, others are withdrawn and intermediaries offer stock. Those responses can absorb some demand or increase the pressure on prices.

With the share count held constant, market capitalization applies the share price to the relevant outstanding shares. A marginal transaction can therefore change the stated market value by far more than the cash exchanged. That cash goes to the seller. The company receives the proceeds of newly issued shares under the terms of the offering. [S04](#source-S04) [S11](#source-S11)

## Share availability starts changing with the IPO

Anthropic’s prospectus provides for a public offering of 825 million shares: approximately 801.35 million newly issued and 23.65 million sold by existing holders. It also identifies **up to 316 million shares potentially eligible for sale from the first trading day following the prospectus date**, held by certain non-founder officers and directors, and current and former employees and consultants. Legal restrictions and internal policies continue to apply. This is potential supply; actual sales will have to be observed. [S11, pp. 319 and 334](#source-S11)

The **$26 billion private placement** introduces a further distinction. Its Class N shares are exempt from contractual lock-up agreements with the banks, yet remain restricted securities under resale regulations. A sale requires registration or an applicable exemption. Their availability therefore needs to be assessed under the rights and restrictions attached to them. [S11, pp. 321 and 348](#source-S11)

Nasdaq also distinguishes the market capitalization used to rank a company from the capitalization used to assign its index weight. For low-float stocks, its methodology caps the share count used for weighting at three times the freely floating shares, up to the total shares reported. The final weight therefore requires examining the share classes and the index adjustments. [S08](#source-S08)

## The limits of the $19 billion estimate

Intropic’s detailed model was not found in the public documents reviewed. Its assumptions about assets under management, reference prices, free float and timing remain unavailable. The reported figure is an attributed estimate of scale.

A simple arithmetic check makes the limitation clear. **28% of 825 million is 231 million shares.** At $78, the top of the announced price range, those shares would be worth approximately **$18 billion**. The $19 billion figure may reflect other pricing or scope assumptions that the available evidence cannot reconcile. We retain the published estimate with that qualification and do not replace it with an l0g model. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

Inclusion demand also needs to be distinguished from net fund inflows. A fund can buy Anthropic by reallocating existing positions or using cash. Client subscriptions provide another source of funding. In turn, the seller receives cash that can be held or reinvested.

Once the stock is held, a rise in its price increases both its weight in a capitalization-weighted index and the value of the position in a fund replicating that index exactly. With share counts and rules unchanged, price movements alone preserve the alignment. Client flows, changes in constituents, float adjustments and weighting caps can subsequently require trades. [S12](#source-S12)

## The inclusion trade has an endpoint

An inclusion purchase is a portfolio event. Once the required exposure has been established, further purchases depend on other flows or rule changes. Investors who accumulated stock beforehand may seek to exit at the same time; existing shareholders may also offer shares. The interaction among those decisions sets the next price.

Academic research supports an open assessment of the outcome. In *The Disappearing Index Effect*, Robin Greenwood and Marco Sammon document a substantial decline in the average price effect of S&P 500 additions over time, despite the expansion of index investing. Their research examines historical inclusions and provides no coefficient that can be applied to this IPO. It makes a case for examining anticipation and trading arrangements before inferring a price increase from the size of expected purchases. [S13](#source-S13)

Anthropic’s first market prices will be informative if their construction is examined: initial allocations, inclusion announcements, available quantities, executions and permitted sales. A rise during that period could combine revised business expectations with portfolio constraints. Attributing the entire move to one factor would require additional evidence.

The company’s funding needs and compute commitments remain in its accounts. Our investigation into [the suppliers financing Anthropic](/en/analysis/anthropic-supplier-financing-ipo/) follows those obligations. The analysis of [Indonesia’s free float and MSCI](/en/analysis/msci-indonesia-free-float-index-risk/) explores the other side of the mechanism: investability rules changing a whole market’s representation in portfolios.

**An index rule can make a buyer predictable. The price emerges where that demand meets holders willing to sell.**

Documentary research as of November 12, 2026. Anthropic’s disclosures describe a proposed transaction.
