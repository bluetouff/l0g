A lender needs to know who can buy a data center’s computing power before financing its chips. On **17 November**, Bloomberg reported that bankers had paused work on a **$480 million loan** for **Aolani Cloud**, a Singapore-based computing provider. The money would fund Nvidia GPUs for a Malaysian facility whose capacity **ByteDance** would use remotely. Prospective lenders are examining the implications of Washington’s new restrictions. [S04](#source-S04)

Nvidia has meanwhile published a detailed account of its financial commitments. It reserves production with suppliers, buys computing services and provides guarantees that can support data-center construction. Its involvement at several points in the chain gives the Aolani case wider significance: permission to use the chips can affect a facility’s financing even when the hardware remains outside China. [S01](#source-S01) [S05](#source-S05)

This nineteenth installment follows [our seventeenth on remote access](/en/analysis/nvidia-bis-remote-access-cloud-controls/) and [our eighteenth on board oversight](/en/analysis/nvidia-board-national-security-export-controls/). The new documents connect the manufacturer’s commitments with the conditions under which banks will finance demand for its products.

## Nvidia’s commitments serve different purposes

**Colette Kress’s financial commentary**, attached to the 8-K accepted by the SEC on **17 November at 21:20:51 UTC**, or **22:20:51 in Paris**, describes the position on **25 October 2026**. That snapshot predates Huang’s resignation. It brings together categories with different contractual obligations. [S01](#source-S01)

| Category as of October 25, 2026 | US dollars | Purpose described by Nvidia |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Supply and capacity | **$390 billion** | Reserve components and production for the coming years |
| Cloud services and leases yet to commence | **$102 billion** | Provide physical and computing capacity, including for R&D |
| Additional neocloud agreements | **$32 billion** | Support access to infrastructure, subject to financing and operational conditions |
| Guarantees, maximum gross exposure | **$118 billion** | Cover specified partner obligations following a default |

Supplier commitments rise from **$279 billion to $390 billion**, mainly because of multiyear memory purchases. The **$102 billion** comprises **$28 billion in cloud services** and **$74 billion in leases that have yet to commence**. Nvidia links these resources to product design and testing, open models and automotive software. Some leases could be assigned to third parties. [S01](#source-S01)

Within the guarantees, **$105 billion** concerns the SB Energy credit support announced in August for the Ohio campus intended for OpenAI. Nvidia describes activation building by building, with the first expected in its fiscal year 2029. The table also includes **$3.5 billion** in neocloud guarantees and **$9.5 billion** in power and systems credit support. Nvidia says it had made no guarantee-related payments as of its commentary. [S01](#source-S01)

Each category has its own timetable and triggers. A purchase commitment reserves a future resource. A guarantee creates an exposure that can require payment when contractual conditions are met, including a partner’s default. Adding them together as losses or immediately payable cash would discard the terms that determine the risk.

## Neocloud support depends on specific milestones

The **$32 billion** of additional agreements concerns *neoclouds*, providers specializing in renting out computing power. Nvidia says some customers are expanding faster than their balance sheets and credit capacity can support. The manufacturer helps them secure the land, power and buildings required to grow. [S01](#source-S01)

Its commitments become effective through a sequence of milestones. Neoclouds must obtain equipment financing, deploy the capacity, bring it into operation and secure Nvidia’s acceptance. The amounts presented assume those conditions are satisfied. Delays can change the timetable, with some commitments potentially reduced or eliminated. [S01](#source-S01)

The arrangement connects two potential revenue streams for Nvidia. It first sells infrastructure to the computing provider. If the agreed criteria are met, it may subsequently earn a share of the revenue that provider receives from its own customers. Those customers’ ability to use and pay for the service therefore matters to the project’s economics. [S01](#source-S01)

Banks have to finance the stages before operations begin using the resources available, customer contracts and protections actually secured. A commitment activated only after computing capacity has been delivered and accepted has a particular role in that assessment. The contract determines the risks covered during construction and then during operation.

## Suppliers also receive financial support

Nvidia’s industrial announcement of **17 November** identifies partners at the other end of the chain. A new multiyear agreement with **Kinsus** would expand substrate capacity dedicated to Nvidia at its Taoyuan site in Taiwan to more than **3.5 times** its previous level. Production is expected in the second half of 2027, with further capacity targeted for the third quarter of 2028. An amendment with **Samsung Electro-Mechanics** would improve the operating capabilities of existing plants. [S02](#source-S02)

Nvidia also announces an expansion of advanced assembly with **SPIL** in Taiwan, backed by multibillion-dollar financing. Additional capacity is expected by mid-2029. The nearly **$3 billion** cited for **Amkor** refers to agreements announced in July and October, including prepayments to expand production. [S02](#source-S02)

These relationships help explain Kress’s priorities. In her CNBC interview after the results, she emphasizes supply, engineering and Nvidia’s own capital needs. She also says selected investments and work with financing partners will continue. Her account maintains support for the ecosystem while stressing scrutiny of projects, their funding and their eventual customers. [S03](#source-S03)

## Aolani’s prospective lenders reassess the deal

The loan described by Bloomberg is still being prepared. **DBS** is identified as mandated lead arranger and bookrunner, responsible for organizing the financing and its distribution. Five banks considering the deal reportedly discussed withdrawing or declining to participate because of concerns about the US restrictions. The report leaves those five lenders unnamed. [S04](#source-S04)

In a [syndicated loan](/en/glossary/syndication/), arranging the transaction and committing a bank’s own money are distinct functions. DBS’s reported role describes the preparation of the financing. Establishing how much each lender has committed and disbursed would require the agreement and final allocations.

Aolani tells the news agency it has spent time with banking and financing partners anticipating regulatory risk. It says it is working with authorities and those partners to deliver the project in compliance with the new framework. DBS declines to comment in the Bloomberg edition consulted. Aolani’s response expresses its intention to proceed while the banks assess their participation. [S04](#source-S04)

The Nvidia collaboration Aolani announced on **21 September** already envisaged revenue sharing and credit support. Its release leaves the amount, lenders and terms of that support unspecified. Any Nvidia guarantee specific to the $480 million loan remains to be documented. [S06](#source-S06)

## The end customer enters the credit assessment

The BIS text would put US licensing at the center of certain advanced-chip exports to countries including **Malaysia and Singapore**. It also addresses access to substantial computing capacity by companies headquartered in destinations of concern, including China, or whose ultimate parent is based there. On **18 November**, the public-inspection notice still schedules publication for **19 November**, with effectiveness tied to publication. Banks can therefore reassess a transaction after the framework is announced, ahead of its formal obligations taking effect. [S05](#source-S05)

For a lender, the assessment extends beyond the machine’s location. If serving a major customer requires a license, the timing of revenue can become less certain. Equipment financing then has to be assessed alongside the service contract, permitted customers and licensing timetable. Aolani’s reported pause offers a case through which to examine that economic mechanism.

Our [map of AI creditors](/en/analysis/ai-debt-map-banks-funds-insurers/) follows banks, funds and insurers through the different layers of a financing. Aolani adds a condition earlier in that chain: the ability to serve the customer whose payments are expected to help repay the debt.

The [AI-financing dossier](/en/topics/ai-financing/) connects these contracts with the guarantees already examined. Nvidia’s support can help build a facility or secure particular obligations. Licensing governs access to customers within its scope. To judge the credit, lenders need to understand both arrangements and how their timetables fit together.

## Limits

Nvidia’s tables describe commitments as of October 25, with distinct categories and conditions. They provide a basis for assessing risk while full contracts and some counterparties remain to be obtained. Bloomberg’s Aolani report relies on anonymous sources and includes the company’s response. The reported pause concerns work on financing, with no definitive cancellation, default or block on funds already disbursed established. Assessing effects on other loans and individual licensing outcomes would require documents specific to each transaction.
