**The serial number in a Shanghai loan file gave Bloomberg something to look up. It belonged to an Nvidia GB300 NVL72 rack assembled by Gigabyte.** Shanghai Shuoyao offered the equipment as security for a **96 million yuan** loan from Shanghai Innovation Bank, according to lender records examined by the news agency. The filings placed the rack at the *Yangtze River Delta Project*, a data centre already familiar from reporting on Megaspeed. [S01](#source-S01)

Bloomberg’s reporters entered the serial number in Gigabyte’s public warranty database. That linked the filing to a particular kind of machine, a rack designed to run **72 Blackwell Ultra GPUs** together. It also gave the investigation a declared address for a piece of equipment whose sales trail remains incomplete. Bloomberg published its findings on **November 27 at 11:45 UTC**. The papers containing the number date from November. The exact filing day is still unknown. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

In [our report on Nvidia’s customers](/en/analysis/nvidia-customers-end-use-controls/), lenders in Southeast Asia were trying to establish who could use the hardware before paying for its installation. In Shanghai, the records start at the other end of the transaction. Companies are using equipment described at a data centre to raise money. The lender’s paperwork gives reporters details about a machine whose original sale they are still trying to trace. [S01](#source-S01) [S12](#source-S12)

## Two loans at the same data centre

**Shanghai Shuoyao** is the company that offered the GB300 rack to the bank. Shanghai Innovation Bank is a Chinese lender based in Shanghai. Its accounts trace its origins to a joint venture between Shanghai Pudong Development Bank and Silicon Valley Bank, and record its renaming in 2024. The newer figures come from Shanghai Pudong Development Bank’s interim accounts. It directly owned **100%** of the lender on **June 30, 2026**. Those records establish the lender’s identity and ownership. The details of the Shuoyao loan come from Bloomberg’s reporting. [S01](#source-S01) [S04](#source-S04)

A second borrower, **Shanghai Kezhan**, described a **GB200** server at the same site. According to Bloomberg, the machine appeared in records for a **90 million yuan** financing obtained in late 2025 from a finance-leasing company wholly owned by a local Shanghai government agency. The report describes a server and leaves its exact configuration open. The NVL72 designation identifies the full GB300 rack. There is no comparable specification for the server in Kezhan’s financing. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

Kezhan also declared **32 servers equipped with H200 chips** at the site, Bloomberg reports. Their model names point to Gigabyte. Without serial numbers in the paperwork, Bloomberg could not trace these machines the way it traced the GB300 rack. The report leaves open whether the 32 servers were part of the 90 million yuan financing. [S01](#source-S01)

The records illustrate two reasons for tracking machines already in use or intended for use. A bank wants to know which asset the borrower offers as security. A lessor wants to be able to identify the equipment it is financing. Both have a reason to record a model and a location. The **96 million** and **90 million yuan** figures describe the reported financing amounts under separate arrangements. Equipment valuations and the money actually received would have to be checked against the schedules to the agreements and payment records. [S01](#source-S01) [S03](#source-S03)

## A public record of the security interest

Consider a company that wants to borrow against servers it already operates. Putting the equipment up as [collateral](/en/glossary/collateral/) gives a lender a potential claim on that property if the borrower stops paying. The value of that claim depends on what the lender could recover, on existing claims and on the contract. The servers can keep running during the loan. If the lender ever has to sell them, their model, condition and identity become questions of money. [S03](#source-S03)

The credit reference centre of the People’s Bank of China runs the registry for security interests in movable property and related rights. The rules call for the names of the parties, a description of the property and the term of registration. Parties can also record the agreed amount of the principal debt or the obligations covered. For a maximum-amount security interest, the registry must show the maximum secured claim. The meaning of an amount on the record depends on the field it appears in. [S03](#source-S03)

The registry makes the filing searchable. The parties are responsible for what they enter. The creditor has to check the equipment exists and look for prior encumbrances. The centre records the filing without investigating it on the merits. A serial number can identify a specific rack. Delivery and disbursement dates still have to be checked in other records. [S03](#source-S03)

## The earlier filing for 193 servers

[Part VIII](/en/analysis/nvidia-shanghai-server-collateral-lutnick-bessent/) examined another set of Shanghai records. According to the November 13 Bloomberg investigation and the details reported by TaiSounds, **Shanghai Longjie Yaoteng** declared **193 servers** as collateral in January 2025. It listed the equipment at **485 million yuan**. Some Dell and Supermicro machines were associated with the previous generation of Nvidia GPUs. The exact chips in the Gigabyte equipment remained unknown. [S06](#source-S06)

Bloomberg’s latest story also returns to Longjie, reporting a **200 million yuan** loan from Shanghai Innovation Bank secured by restricted Nvidia equipment. That is a loan amount reported November 27. Comparing it with the declared equipment value of 485 million yuan from January 2025 would require the contract and a matching list of assets. The borrower’s full legal name would also have to be checked because the latest report uses a shortened form. [S01](#source-S01) [S06](#source-S06)

## Leasing already financed servers at the site

Two days before the Bloomberg story, Reuters found five **Century Litong** leases in China’s registry dating from May to December 2024. Four listed equipment at the Shanghai site. Together the five covered up to **512 servers** with a declared value of **1.42 billion yuan**. Some filings gave a performance figure or a GPU count without identifying the chip models. The serial number identifies the GB300 in a separate financing. [S15](#source-S15)

The site also appears in Jiangsu Lettall Electronic’s 2023 interim report. The company names it as one of the locations where its Century Litong subsidiary runs its compute rental business. In July 2024, Lettall disclosed a signed **185.37 million yuan** [guarantee](/en/glossary/garantie-de-pret/) for one of its subsidiary’s leases with Jiangnan Financial Leasing. The lease gives the subsidiary use of the equipment in exchange for rent. The guarantee gives the lessor a claim against the parent company. Linking the stock exchange notice to one of the five filings Reuters identified would require the equipment schedule and the site address. [S15](#source-S15) [S16](#source-S16)

## Megaspeed’s commercial connection

The Shanghai site and the companies operating there are part of the Megaspeed story. Bloomberg reports that the Singapore company used an image of the data centre in a presentation to investors in 2024. Shanghai Shuoyao says it does work for Megaspeed. Its **November 11** statement describes an ordinary relationship between a service provider and its customer. It also says Alice Huang, whose Chinese name is Huang Le, has never held an equity stake or a position at Shuoyao. The statement concerns Shuoyao and predates publication of the GB300 findings. [S01](#source-S01) [S05](#source-S05)

Bloomberg describes a different connection at Kezhan. According to company data cited by the news agency, NeXPower Japan indirectly holds **83.3%** of Kezhan. The Chinese outlet Caixin, cited in the report, says Alice Huang controls the Japanese company. NeXPower’s official listing establishes its legal identity and its Tokyo address. The reported shareholdings require separate records. [S01](#source-S01) [S14](#source-S14)

The November 27 report gives investigators a better view of where equipment ended up. For the GB300, Gigabyte says it followed the rules and declined to identify its original customer or the sale date. Nvidia did not respond to Bloomberg. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing and did not answer questions about the Shanghai site. How the rack reached Shanghai remains open. Shuoyao’s commercial relationship points to records worth examining and leaves the identity of the company that brought the machine into China unresolved. [S01](#source-S01) [S05](#source-S05)

## Export controls can reach the lender

Under [US export rules](/en/glossary/controle-export/), shipping covered advanced equipment to China can require a licence, depending on the item, the parties and the date. Bloomberg places the Blackwell hardware described here in a category subject to licensing. The rules set out how the government reviews applications. For the specific machine in this story, the transaction itself and any individual authorisation still have to be established. [S01](#source-S01) [S07](#source-S07)

The US rules expressly address financing. The tenth general prohibition covers financing an item subject to the rules that has been or is going to be exported, re-exported or transferred within a country. It applies when the lender knows that a violation involving the item has taken place, is about to occur or is intended. The US definition of knowledge includes awareness of a high probability and takes account of deliberately disregarded facts. In 2024, the Bureau of Industry and Security published guidance on due diligence for financial institutions. Identifying the equipment in Shanghai gives the inquiry somewhere to begin. The shipping records, any licences and the information available to the lender at the time of the loan still have to be examined. [S08](#source-S08)

## Lenders wait as governments clarify their instructions

After our last instalment, *The Wall Street Journal* reported that Nvidia had asked manufacturers to pause server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. A person familiar with the matter told the paper the company had a process for eventually releasing shipments once it had checked its partners. On **November 27**, Singapore’s Gan Kim Yong said the government was still checking the reported pause and talking to companies and Washington. In Malaysia, a government spokesperson said that day that companies had been instructed to avoid transactions with the businesses covered by the US listing. The text governing implementation remains to be obtained. [S09](#source-S09) [S10](#source-S10) [S11](#source-S11)

[Aolani’s financing](/en/analysis/nvidia-financing-guarantees-aolani-loan/) shows why this reaches the banks. Bloomberg reported that lenders had paused work on a project intended to supply computing power to ByteDance from Malaysia. The proposed financing was **$480 million**. The sources consulted for this instalment disclosed no confirmed restart of the loan. In September, Aolani also announced a partnership with US-based FriendliAI. The company’s announcement establishes that commercial outlet. It leaves the scale of the reserved capacity and its distribution across sites open. [S12](#source-S12) [S13](#source-S13)

In Shanghai, we now have a rack model and a bank’s name. The serial number gives investigators somewhere to start looking for the delivery note and the lender’s signed agreement. The first would tell them who took delivery of the equipment and when. The second would spell out the rights the lender acquired. Put those records together and it would be possible to explain how the Blackwell rack reached Shanghai and what rights the bank could exercise over it.
