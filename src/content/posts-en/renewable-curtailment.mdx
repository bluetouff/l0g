A solar farm can have all the sunlight it needs at noon and still cut output. If the line carrying its electricity away is full, the grid operator can limit how much the farm puts onto the system. If the wholesale price falls below zero, the operator of the farm may decide that selling another megawatt-hour would cost it money. Either way, the meter records less generation than the site could have delivered. Finding out who bears the cost starts with who called for the cut and under which contract. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02) · [S04](#source-S04)

## Two reasons to cut output

[Renewable curtailment](/en/glossary/ecretement-renouvelable/) means reducing generation below the available output. In the International Energy Agency (IEA) definition of technical curtailment, a grid operator instructs a plant to cut generation to manage a network or system constraint. A line out of a particular area can fill up even when the national wholesale price is positive. Equally, a negative price can make some farms choose to stop while they still have room to deliver power. [S01](#source-S01) · [S02](#source-S02) · [S04](#source-S04)

Across the key markets it studies, the IEA estimates that technical curtailment of wind and solar reached **nearly 200 TWh in 2025**. Its main case puts the figure **above 800 TWh in 2031**, equivalent to nearly **8%** of total variable renewable generation, according to the IEA. That projection incorporates assumptions about both the rollout of renewables and the system’s capacity to integrate them. [S01](#source-S01)

France’s transmission operator RTE tracks a different measure for mainland France. Its flexibility report estimates that wind and solar farms cut output by **0.5 TWh in 2023**, **1.7 TWh in 2024** and **about 3 TWh in 2025** during periods of negative wholesale prices. In its annual review, RTE separately reports around **0.1 TWh** in 2025 from its requests to reduce output for system balancing. That figure covers a specific set of instructions. ## Negative prices spread through the day

French day-ahead electricity averaged **€61/MWh in 2025**. In the same year, RTE recorded **513 hours** of negative prices, up from **352** in 2024. After the market moved to fifteen-minute trading intervals in October 2025, an hour is classified from the average of its four intervals. A farm selling into the wholesale market earns the price for its delivery interval. RTE links the midday dip to lower demand at those hours and more solar generation in France and its neighbours. [S04](#source-S04)

The trend continued in 2026. RTE estimates that wind and solar farms cut **2.5 TWh in the first half** during negative-price periods, compared with **1.9 TWh in the first half of 2025**. By **8 October 2026**, France’s energy regulator CRE had counted **568 hours** of negative day-ahead prices, more than in the whole of 2025. That count measures time on the wholesale market. The amount of energy curtailed also depends on wind and sunlight and on the contracts that govern the operating farms. [S05](#source-S05) · [S06](#source-S06)

## The contract determines who pays first

A farm selling directly to the market can avoid a cost by stopping when the price goes negative. A long-term power purchase agreement changes the calculation. CRE found contract terms that allow buyers to request a shutdown, compensate the producer and buy cheaper electricity on the market. The agreed price for delivery and the payment due if generation stops are separate clauses. [S04](#source-S04) · [S07](#source-S07)

Some French market-premium contracts allow a payment for stopping in negative-price periods beyond a threshold and subject to the scheme’s conditions. Eligible support costs are funded through the state budget. EU law separately requires compensation from the grid operator requesting non-market redispatch, with an exception for producers that agreed to a connection without guaranteed firm delivery. Different events trigger those payments and different parties owe them. [S04](#source-S04) · [S08](#source-S08) · [S09](#source-S09)

## Choosing between a battery and a power line

A battery can charge when electricity is plentiful and supply it later, with conversion losses and limits on its power, capacity and state of charge. Where it sits determines the service it provides. On the side where generation backs up, charging can temporarily reduce flows through a congested line. Flexible demand can also absorb some of the surplus in that area. Reinforcing the grid may address a more persistent constraint. Comparing the options means looking at the hours each one covers and its cost over time. [S01](#source-S01) · [S03](#source-S03) · [S10](#source-S10) · [S11](#source-S11)

France’s regulator already sets a framework for RTE to make this trade-off. Occasional output cuts, with compensation where applicable, can defer or avoid some grid upgrades. The case depends on how often and for how long the network is constrained, the value of the power withheld and the cost of the proposed upgrade. Storage, new lines and occasional cuts serve different needs over the course of a year. On the demand side, the [French Tempo tariff](/en/analysis/tempo-red-days-algorithm-electricity-bill/) offers a familiar example of how a varying price can encourage customers to shift usage. ## Limits of a financial estimate

Pricing an output cut requires its timing, the applicable sale price, available generation and the compensation terms. A grid constraint also calls for its location and the cost of relieving it another way. Applying the annual average of **€61/MWh** to curtailed energy mixes hours and overlooks contracts. Public support, forgone revenue and avoided grid expenditure belong in separate calculations. A useful comparison starts with the specific farm and network line, using the rules that applied when output was cut. [S04](#source-S04) · [S07](#source-S07) · [S10](#source-S10)
