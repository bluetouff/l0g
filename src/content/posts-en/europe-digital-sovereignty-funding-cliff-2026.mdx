Buried in the European Commission's June 2026 Digital Decade review was a warning more revealing than any of the accompanying declarations about technological independence. **Almost half of the public funding in Member States' national digital roadmaps is expected to be phased out by the end of 2026.** The very same report calls for more investment in semiconductors, artificial intelligence, secure cloud infrastructure and cybersecurity. Put the statements together and an awkward question emerges: **who finances the next stage?** <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

A grant can put a machine into a research centre. Keeping the machine useful requires replacement parts, power and specialist staff. Subsidies can help a company move its workload onto a different cloud platform. That company will still need reliable software, support, upgrades and customers willing to pay the operating costs. A launch budget creates an opening. What follows determines whether the new capability survives.

This investigation examines evidence available as of **5 November 2026**. We trace three linked questions: where the public money goes, what capacity it actually creates, and whether that capacity translates into durable commercial and strategic control. Europe is making measurable progress, but its funding announcements combine grants, loans, private-investment targets and multi-year spending plans. Treating those as interchangeable cash piles would obscure the most important finding.

## The expiry warning is already in Europe's own roadmap

The Commission's State of the Digital Decade report, published on **17 June 2026**, records **1,934 national measures valued at €289.3 billion**, including **€205.9 billion of public-budget funding**. Subtracting the latter from the former leaves **€83.4 billion** outside that public-budget category. That calculated residual has no detailed funding breakdown in the cited summary. The headline figure describes planned roadmap measures; actual payments follow the conditions of each programme. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

<SouveraineteNumeriqueFigure lang="en" kind="commitments" />

The warning concerns **public funding expected to phase out**, with the precise amount and project-level distribution still unknown. Some interventions will complete their planned work; others will need replacement appropriations or commercial revenue to continue. Establishing the outcome requires following each initiative through its funding decisions and operating accounts.

The immediate reason for the timing is the end of the **[Recovery and Resilience Facility](/en/glossary/frr/)**, or RRF. Following any pre-financing, regular payments to governments depended on satisfactory fulfilment of agreed milestones and targets for reforms and investments. The deadline for completing those milestones was **31 August 2026**. Final payment requests were due on **30 September**, with the Commission's last payments due by **31 December**. Its annual report, published on 7 October, said **€450 billion had been disbursed** since the programme began, equivalent to **79% of its envelope** as of **1 October 2026**. That figure covers all policy areas, rather than digital spending alone. A further **€123 billion in payment requests under assessment** also covers the full range of policies supported by the programme. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The real question is what happens when an exceptional instrument used to finance projects is replaced by the ordinary economics of operating them. Public reporting does not yet provide a consolidated bridge between RRF-backed digital investments and the recurring expenses that governments or private operators will face in 2027.

## Funding continues through 2027

The **2021–2027 EU budget** continues through 2027. DIGITAL Europe, a programme designed to deploy technologies and infrastructure rather than primarily fund academic research, has a stated **€8.1 billion multi-year budget**. Its 2025–2027 work programmes continued to receive updates in 2026. The European Investment Bank, national treasuries and other EU instruments can also continue financing projects. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

<SouveraineteNumeriqueFigure lang="en" kind="timeline" />

In July 2025 the Commission proposed a **nearly €2 trillion** budget for **2028–2034**, which includes a **€409 billion competitiveness category**. That figure includes Horizon Europe and covers a broad range of sectors. Its scope extends well beyond digital sovereignty. As of this investigation, the next multiannual financial framework remains a proposal subject to negotiation. Its figures are expressed in current prices over seven years. Comparing them directly with a one-year financing requirement would be misleading. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

For someone developing computing infrastructure, the availability of a headline multi-year envelope is only one part of the problem. Servers must be purchased before they generate income; electricity and support bills recur monthly; software requires continuing maintenance. A project can have a credible long-term value yet fail for want of financing at the right moment. **Payment schedules and funding conditions are as important as budget size.**

## Semiconductors: an independent audit tests the ambition

The European Court of Auditors provides a rare independent assessment of the gap between the stated objective and what a funding strategy can deliver. Its **Special Report 12/2025** found progress under the EU Chips Act but judged the goal of a **20% share of the global semiconductor value chain by 2030 highly unlikely**. The figures shown in the audit are **9.9% in 2019**, **9.8% in 2022** and a **projected 11.7% in 2030**, from a July 2024 Commission estimate reproduced in the audit. The 2030 number is a projection examined in 2025, not a measured outcome in 2026. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

<SouveraineteNumeriqueFigure lang="en" kind="chips" />

The auditors identified **€86 billion in expected funding for 2021–2027** associated with the chips strategy. Only **€4.5 billion**, or roughly **5%**, was directly managed by the EU; **€43 billion** was expected to come from private investment. The remainder involved other public financing and financial instruments. These are expected contributions from several sources, potentially overlapping with Digital Decade measures. Their execution needs to be followed through the relevant accounts and contracts. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The policy continues to develop. On **4 November 2026**, the Commission launched the European Chip Design Platform, which aims to **mobilise €525 million in investment** and give young companies access to design tools and manufacturing. Following that announcement through to commitments and delivery will show its contribution. The 2025 audit provides a historical benchmark for assessing these subsequent initiatives. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-17" aria-label="Source 17">17</a></sup>

The challenge is industrial as much as financial. Semiconductor manufacturing requires specialised machinery, electricity, materials, trained workers and reliable demand. A fabrication plant in Europe can reduce one vulnerability while still importing vital tools, design software or components. A production line may be valuable for automotive or industrial users without creating full independence in leading-edge chips. Neither plant count nor global market share captures every aspect of resilience.

The audit is important precisely because it respects this distinction. Public money can make a project possible. Manufacturers' investment cycles, customers, global competition and the availability of specialised inputs determine how large that project becomes. A target may be useful politically and still be an unreliable forecast.

## Cloud adoption and operational control follow different measures

Eurostat reports that **52.7% of eligible EU enterprises** bought cloud services in **2025**, up from **17.8% in 2014**. Its news release is dated **3 February 2026**. The survey covers companies employing at least ten people in specified sectors and includes activities ranging from email and file storage to computing platforms. It does **not** identify the nationality of the suppliers behind those purchases. It cannot therefore tell us how much of the market's revenue belongs to European providers. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

AI is spreading as well. **20.0%** of eligible companies reported using at least one AI technology in 2025, compared with **13.5% in 2024**. The breakdown by company size is striking: **55.0%** of large companies used AI, versus **18.9%** of small and medium-sized companies, according to Eurostat's 2026 business publication. Cloud purchasing and AI adoption measure different activities. Eurostat also added an image, video and sound generation category to the 2025 AI questionnaire, which calls for care when interpreting annual growth. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup>

<SouveraineteNumeriqueFigure lang="en" kind="adoption" />

The economic meaning of sovereignty appears in the details of a supplier contract. Where is the data processed? Which legal jurisdictions apply? Who can access administrative keys, update the system or discontinue a service? Can a customer migrate its workload without rebuilding the business around a different technical architecture? A data centre may be located within the EU while relying on foreign chips and a proprietary platform. An EU-developed application can, conversely, run across providers and offer greater freedom of movement.

The Commission identifies significant dependencies on non-EU providers in cloud and other strategic services, but the institutional sources used for this investigation do not contain one comprehensive, audited annual measure of cloud sovereignty. Commercial market-share estimates can be helpful if their scope and methodology are clear. We have not treated an aggregate number for American hyperscalers as an official finding when the market definitions are not comparable. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## An AI gigafactory starts as a financing structure

On **30 July 2026**, the Commission announced a call intended to establish **up to seven AI gigafactories**. The proposal envisaged **up to €10 billion in EU and national public support** to unlock **at least €20 billion in private investment**. The private amount is a mobilisation objective and the public amount is a contemplated support ceiling. Both remain conditional on the selection and financing process. Their sum describes the proposed scale of the initiative. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

<SouveraineteNumeriqueFigure lang="en" kind="giga" />

The funding model relies on **public procurement of compute access time**. EuroHPC and participating countries will purchase capacity from the selected consortia. Acting as anchor customers, they can make part of future demand more predictable for investors. Contracts must turn the planned demand into revenue that can support equipment and operations. Payment schedules and the actual allocation of risk will depend on the agreements concluded. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

The timetable exposes the practical difficulty. **The call page checked on 5 November sets the large-scale deadline at 3 December 2026 and extends the medium-scale deadline to 4 February 2027**, allowing countries to complete national budget procedures and submit financial commitments. This supersedes the November date in the launch announcement. The notice states the reason for the extension, while leaving its announcement date unspecified. Organising the funding has become a condition of the industrial schedule. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

The Commission also lists **19 AI Factories being established**. Its EuroHPC framework anticipates **€10 billion in combined EU, national and associated-country investment** in supercomputing infrastructure and AI Factories over **2021–2027**. That is a different initiative and time horizon. Public announcements alone do not establish whether these amounts overlap with subsequent gigafactory plans. Adding them together would require detailed project-level reconciliation that is unavailable here. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

A gigafactory is first a physical proposition: processors, networking equipment, energy, cooling and skilled engineers. It is also an economic one. A useful facility has to offer researchers and firms dependable access at a price and performance level that enables them to build products. Publicly funded compute can produce valuable scientific research without automatically becoming a commercially competitive rival to major cloud operators. **Hours of compute actually used, access conditions, operating costs and the profile of users** will be more revealing than the number of proposed sites.

## TechEU uses public financing to attract investors

The European Investment Bank operates through a different mechanism. Its **TechEU** initiative targets **€70 billion in financing for 2025–2027** using instruments including loans, venture debt and equity. It aims to **mobilise €250 billion of total investment**. The €250 billion mobilisation target includes the €70 billion of support financing and the other investment it aims to attract. In its 2025 activity report, the EIB also reports **€22 billion in financing for innovation** across its operations that year. That is a broad activity measure and should not be added again as a separate TechEU disbursement. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

The case for using financial leverage is strong where a project can generate future returns but private lenders are reluctant to absorb early-stage risk. Patient equity may be appropriate for a young technology company; debt can work for infrastructure with predictable revenues. Public participation can draw in other investors. Yet the resulting investment depends on **[additional private funding](/en/glossary/additionnalite-financiere/)**, commercial demand, repayment prospects and the terms on which the risk is shared. Counting an intended multiplier as an accomplished return would settle none of these questions.

There is also a coordination problem. Separate countries can subsidise similar projects that lack the scale or [interoperability](/en/glossary/interoperabilite/) to serve a single European market. The Commission is promoting European Digital Infrastructure Consortia and Important Projects of Common European Interest to align investment decisions. Such structures might reduce duplication and help promising suppliers grow across borders. Their existence should be judged by delivery, usage and market access rather than their institutional labels. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## The sovereignty test arrives after the grant

<SouveraineteNumeriqueFigure lang="en" kind="system" />

Consider a hypothetical European cloud provider. A grant helps establish it. The company buys processors, leases space and hires an engineering team. A public body begins to use its platform. From the outside, that can look like a complete sovereignty success. But four tests have barely begun: Can the provider finance its hardware refresh? Can its software run on competing infrastructure? Could the public body exit without major disruption? And can the firm keep buying the components it needs during a supply shock? The grant supports the opening stage. Commercial and technical choices determine what happens next.

Those questions suggest measurable indicators: **money disbursed rather than announced; facilities actually operating; recurring costs covered by recurring income; tested exit and portability arrangements; access to critical components; and real utilisation by businesses and public services**. Some measures can be assembled from official project reporting. Others would require contracts or independent technical audits that are not currently public. A defensible investigation should identify the missing information rather than insert guessed values into an attractive chart.

There is a serious alternative to trying to own every layer of the technology industry. Europe can improve resilience by diversifying trusted suppliers, maintaining the ability to substitute services and controlling a smaller number of critical bottlenecks. Open standards may offer better protection against lock-in than duplicating an entire foreign ecosystem at prohibitive cost. Some overseas dependencies are economically efficient and manageable. The right choice depends on the criticality of each service, the reliability of alternatives and the credible cost of a disruption.

## Financing continuity determines the next stage

The Commission has acknowledged a **funding-continuity risk**. The RRF has completed its investment milestone period, existing programmes run through 2027, and the proposed post-2027 budget is still being negotiated. Semiconductors demonstrate how a heavily funded industrial strategy can create valuable capacity yet fall short of a global market-share ambition. Cloud and AI statistics show impressive adoption without answering who controls the underlying systems. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

None of this proves the European strategy is about to fail. Nor does a proposed future budget prove that it is sufficiently funded. The evidence identifies a transition from creating assets to **financing their upkeep, winning customers and securing operational control**. That is where the next meaningful assessment of digital sovereignty will be made: in functioning facilities, sustainable services and credible alternatives when a critical supplier fails or becomes inaccessible.

## Method and limitations

Evidence cut-off: **5 November 2026**. The article distinguishes multi-year plans, budgets, commitments, lending, expected private co-investment and actual disbursements. Overlaps between financing initiatives cannot be ruled out, so no cross-programme grand total is calculated. The 2030 semiconductor figure is a forecast reproduced in the European Court of Auditors' 2025 assessment. Eurostat's company measures refer to the **2025 reference period** and enterprises with at least ten persons in the covered industries. Paid cloud adoption and AI use are distinct concepts. The diagrams describe sourced mechanisms, not reconstructed financial records for a real project. Future investment targets and budget allocations remain conditional.

The next step for a full project-level investigation would be to obtain a matched list of RRF-supported digital investments, their maintenance and renewal costs for 2027–2028, the replacement appropriations actually passed, and service-usage data. Without that evidence, any claim that a precise sum is “missing” would be speculation dressed as accounting.
