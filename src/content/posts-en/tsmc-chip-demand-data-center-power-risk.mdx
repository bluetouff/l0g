The company manufacturing chips for the AI industry is taking a closer look at the buildings that will eventually house them. On 15 October 2026, TSMC chief executive C. C. Wei said the company was sending its own teams to check construction progress and power supply at US data centers. He described the feedback as favorable and assessed demand as still exceeding TSMC's supply. The revealing detail is how far this work reaches: decisions about semiconductor factories now draw on the physical readiness of infrastructure used by its customers' customers. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

A processor order can have a budget behind it, a signed contract and a real need for computing capacity, while still depending on a grid connection whose schedule needs to be secured. Businesses throughout the chain incur costs before the whole installation becomes operational. A delay can then travel through delivery dates, cash requirements and, eventually, subsequent equipment orders. Following that process gives a more useful view of the AI investment cycle: an industrial forecast is only as executable as the projects, connections and supply agreements on which it rests.

Public documents reviewed through 21 October make it possible to reconstruct this mechanism. They also provide meaningful reasons for caution about a uniformly pessimistic interpretation. Some commitments have financial protection, buyers have very different resources, and TSMC continues to invest. The relevant questions concern which orders can turn into deployed capacity, when that can happen and who bears the cost of a change in schedule.

## Looking beyond the immediate customer

A [foundry](/en/glossary/fonderie/) such as Taiwan-based TSMC manufactures circuits designed by its customers. In [the semiconductor supply chain](/en/analysis/semiconductors-a-stack-of-constraints/#the-foundry-sets-the-pace), those components must subsequently be incorporated into equipment and installed in a working computing facility. The manufacturer and the cloud operator therefore earn revenue at different stages, under their respective contracts. A chip may already have been sold while the facility intended to use it is still being prepared. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

That separation creates a reason to investigate demand beyond the immediate buyer. A customer may reserve additional components to secure its expansion, while a cloud operator anticipates strong demand for services. Each decision can make sense in its own setting. Adding them together still requires scrutiny: projects may target overlapping customers, depend on shared infrastructure or assume opening dates that cannot all be reconciled with the necessary construction work. These are possibilities to investigate at project level, rather than conclusions that follow from the total value of announcements.

The checks discussed in October build on a process TSMC had already described on 16 July. It was then examining data center projects and server racks to assess where the equipment would be deployed. That history matters: the company is extending a form of demand verification it had already explained. The public material reviewed does not identify the sites visited in October or provide the inspection reports. The favorable assessment of those visits remains management's account. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## Electricity forecasts also begin with chips

The latest national assessment identified in this investigation is Berkeley Lab's *United States Data Center Energy Usage Report: 2025 Update*, published on 18 June 2026. The year in the title describes the update, while the publication took place the following year. The researchers estimate that US data centers consumed **192 terawatt-hours in 2024**. Their reference scenario reaches **649 TWh in 2030**, with the scenarios combining extreme assumptions spanning **521 to 843 TWh** that year. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

These figures measure energy consumed over a year. Gigawatts quoted for a site describe power: the amount it can draw at a given moment. Converting between the two requires assumptions about operating hours, equipment load and the facility's additional needs. Location introduces another distinction. National electricity consumption can grow while a particular project encounters a transmission or connection constraint, far from resources available elsewhere.

The Berkeley report is especially useful for its method. It starts partly with server and accelerator shipments, then applies assumptions about power requirements, utilization, equipment lifetime and supporting infrastructure. The authors explain that their scenarios leave out major disruptions associated with factors such as grid power availability, regulation and supply chains. Even the historical estimates are reconstructed from equipment data, rather than from a comprehensive census of electricity meters. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

This matters when interpreting financial forecasts. An electricity projection built from chip shipments estimates the energy requirements associated with an equipment deployment path. Independent scrutiny of its feasibility calls for different evidence: a location, a connection, a credible completion date and financing. TSMC's reported visits seek information of that kind. This comparison is our interpretation of the documents; there is no public indication that TSMC uses the Berkeley model.

## Dominion puts megawatt requests through several filters

Dominion's presentation to PJM on 29 September shows how a utility handles the pipeline. For contracts executed as of July 2026, it distinguishes electric service agreements (ESA), construction letters of authorization (CLOA) and engineering letters of authorization (ELOA). The three categories account for **11,997, 9,356 and 32,473 megawatts**, respectively. Their sum, **53,826 MW**, brings together commitments at different stages and with different schedules. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

The engineering authorizations make the filtering visible. Of the associated 32,473 MW, Dominion includes **13,429 MW** in its forecast from 2032 onward and excludes **19,044 MW**. The model then considers energization dates and the gradual increase in load. Dominion reports an average five-year ramp period and a mature demand-to-contracted-capacity ratio of 63%. Those parameters describe its electricity forecasting method; they do not measure processor activity. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

The meaning of the headline number changes as a project advances. Engineering work prepares a possible connection. A construction authorization commits the parties to building infrastructure. A service agreement governs supply. Actual electricity demand then depends on the site's deployment and operation. A useful grid forecast emerges from this sequence, with adjustments for timing and load, rather than from adding requested capacity alone.

PJM, which coordinates this regional electricity system, also describes [how a campus enters the load forecast](/en/analysis/pjm-electricity-forecast-7-billion/#how-a-campus-becomes-a-line-in-the-load-forecast). Its 29 September article discusses developer commitments and the handling of duplicate requests. It receives information from electricity suppliers for its forecasts, adding another stage of collection and verification between a project's initial request and the system-wide outlook. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

The gap between initial requests and the retained forecast reflects the maturation and screening of projects along the way. A property or power option can be abandoned while the computing demand it was intended to serve moves elsewhere. For the chip manufacturer, the deployment’s destination and timing remain central, beyond the fate of one address.

## Contracts determine where the cost lands

Power requests come with financial protections. In its May 2026 report, Dominion describes an initial **$250,000 engineering payment**, with further payments possible. The construction stage triggers deposits and, under the arrangement it describes, reimbursement of costs incurred if the project is cancelled before energization. The utility presents a sequence in which customer obligations increase as the development becomes more concrete. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

Virginia's regulator also confirms stricter contractual requirements for Dominion's large-load customers in the state. New customers contracting for service from **1 January 2027** will face a minimum fourteen-year term, together with collateral requirements depending on credit quality. A long-term payment obligation helps the utility recover its costs even when the electricity consumption originally expected takes longer to materialize. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

Understanding risk transmission therefore requires identifying the beneficiary of each protection. Reimbursement for construction can protect the utility while leaving a developer exposed to land, a building or equipment whose revenue arrives later. A guarantee can reassure a lender by moving an obligation to another company. Across the supply chain, exposure is distributed among balance sheets and contracts; the economic cost still depends on whether assets can be used or redeployed.

[Contracts for reserved grid capacity](/en/analysis/the-ghost-kilowatt/#virginia-and-wisconsin-charging-for-the-reservation) give substance to headline gigawatt announcements. A request carrying an expensive exit obligation provides different information from an engineering-stage request. Its actual force must be established from the relevant agreement: a forecasting model's classification of a project as “firm” first describes the category assigned by the model's author.

## Reserved capacity still has to become an available service

CoreWeave's disclosures illustrate the distinction between stages of development. At **30 June 2026**, it reported **1.5 GW of active power** and approximately **3.7 GW of contracted power**. Its *revenue backlog*, an indicator of future revenue under customer contracts, stood at about **$104 billion**, subject to delivery and service-availability requirements. These figures were published on 11 August. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

The comparison is useful when its definitions are preserved. Active power describes an operational position under the company's metric; contracted power concerns capacity covered by its agreements. Identifying delayed projects requires additional information, site by site. The financial issue lies in the service condition: the promised capacity has to become available before it can support the expected revenue under the contracts.

On 17 September, CoreWeave subsequently reported approximately **4.2 GW of contracted power as of 11 August**. That release did not provide an active-power figure for the same date. A recent example shows how power schedules can change a customer's decision. In October 2025, Nscale announced a **50 MW project at Loughton in the United Kingdom**, intended to host roughly **23,000 GB300 GPUs** from the first quarter of 2027. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup> Reuters reported on 19 October 2026 that Microsoft had withdrawn after grid access slipped into the 2030s; Microsoft and Nscale say their other agreements remain in place. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

## How a delay can reach the foundry's investment decisions

NVIDIA explicitly identifies the relevant link in its quarterly report for the period ended 26 July 2026. The company says a lack of available data center infrastructure or customer financing can delay purchases of new architectures. In the same document, it describes supply commitments made to meet demand over several years. Sales schedules and industrial obligations can consequently move out of alignment. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

If a site falls behind schedule, its operator may seek another location or renegotiate delivery dates. If the supplier can allocate equipment to another buyer, aggregate component demand may hold up. If inventories accumulate or future requirements are revised, the timing of new orders can change. A problem must therefore pass through several commercial decisions before it reaches TSMC, with opportunities for absorption along the way.

TSMC's investment program explains why this question matters financially. The company maintains a worldwide **2026 [capital expenditure budget](/en/glossary/capex/) of $60–64 billion** and reports **$40.64 billion spent from January through September**. The annual budget was raised in July. These figures cover the group's overall investment, extending beyond AI applications and US factories. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The annual report explains why commercial visibility and contractual protection can differ. Customers generally place product orders with limited advance notice, although some make payments to reserve capacity. The treatment of those payments depends on the agreements reached. The report also describes a substantial fixed-cost base and the way factory utilization changes the allocation of those costs across units produced. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

That relationship gives on-site verification its economic purpose. A factory requires decisions taken well in advance, while its future markets continue to evolve. If new capacity becomes available when orders shift to later dates, fewer units must absorb the same fixed costs. Adjustments may involve reallocating capacity between customers, changing future equipment purchases or altering the production ramp. Their scale depends on the length of the delay, the manufacturing processes involved and the availability of alternative demand.

## The buffers matter as much as the bottleneck

Computing buyers have very different resources with which to absorb a delay. For the twelve months ended 30 June 2026, Microsoft reported **$182.9 billion of operating cash flow** and **$115.9 billion of cash additions to property and equipment**. Both figures cover the entire group. The second is a cash outflow measure that excludes newly entered finance-lease commitments. They demonstrate substantial funding generated by existing businesses, while leaving the economics of each individual project open for examination. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

Location and timing create further opportunities to adapt. Subject to technical, contractual and data-location constraints, some computing work can move between sites or be deployed in stages. Grid work already underway and financial guarantees may also make a promised service date more credible. Delays are harder to absorb when several locations are affected together or an operator has little financing headroom. These are conditions to examine in individual cases; the sector contains a wide range of business models and exposures.

The strongest counterpoint is embedded in the statement that prompted this investigation: TSMC considers the feedback from its checks favorable and its assessed demand above supply. The exercise can help determine where to accelerate investment as well as where to avoid a mismatch in timing. The public sources reviewed establish no amount of TSMC orders or revenue lost because of US power constraints. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## Following demand through to the service date

A useful reading of the evidence brings together the commercial commitment, the site's stage of development, its power-availability date and the financial capacity of the parties. An order figure becomes more informative when it can be linked to those conditions. A connection schedule becomes more credible when the construction work and payment obligations behind it are identifiable.

Tracking the risk over time will require comparisons on consistent terms: contracted and active power at the same date for an operator, service dates for the same set of projects, revisions to investment plans, and comments on inventories or capacity utilization. A setback at one location may represent a move elsewhere; strong demand can coexist with delivery deferrals. The evidence has to be reconciled across those possibilities.

The public record available on 21 October shows that infrastructure constraints have become part of the industry's demand assessment. Companies themselves document the channels through which those constraints can affect finances, while their aggregate effect on TSMC's future sales remains unknown. Inspecting construction sites adds information that budgets and global forecasts struggle to provide: whether a specific project can turn its equipment into an available service on the date it has promised.
