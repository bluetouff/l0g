**Beijing has cut the central-bank funding rate for share-purchase loans to 1%. Company filings reveal how differently that support can be used: executing a buyback, replacing its funding or holding stock for a later sale. Following the money exposes both the reach of the policy and the debt left behind.**

*Evidence reviewed as of 14 November 2026. Monetary amounts are in nominal renminbi.*

On 9 November, Shenzhen Qiangrui Precision Technology published an RMB80 million to RMB120 million [share-buyback](/en/glossary/rachat-d-actions/) programme. Agricultural Bank of China had issued a commitment letter for up to RMB100 million of financing, with a maximum three-year term. The first purchases followed on 12 November: 123,200 shares for exactly RMB8,979,579.40, excluding transaction costs, disclosed the next day. The programme, the lender’s commitment and the trades each describe a different stage. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

Nationally, the rate at which the People’s Bank of China, or PBOC, refinances banks for these loans fell from 1.25% to **1% on 4 November**. Banks may add a margin when lending to companies and major shareholders. The policy’s headline rate therefore sits at the start of the funding chain. The buyer’s borrowing cost still depends on its contract. [S03](#source-S03)

The aim is to facilitate debt-funded demand for shares. Its effectiveness will depend on purchases added to existing plans, the use of cash preserved by borrowing and the eventual repayments. Filings from Qiangrui, Sanhua and Avary already show why those outcomes need to be examined separately.

## The 1% rate belongs to the central-bank loan

A bank lends to a listed company or a major shareholder. It can then seek PBOC refinancing against eligible loans already disbursed. The founding rules require dedicated accounts and information submitted to the authorities. Banks make their own lending decisions and bear the credit risk. [S04](#source-S04)

This [targeted refinancing](/en/glossary/refinancement-cible/) may cover 100% of eligible principal. It provides funding to the bank, which remains responsible for its exposure to the borrower. Financing the full principal and protecting a lender against default are separate commitments. [S03](#source-S03) [S04](#source-S04) [S05](#source-S05)

<ChinaBuybackCredit2026 figure={1} lang="en" />

The spread between the funding cost and the customer rate must cover administration, capital requirements and default risk. A lower funding rate can benefit the borrower, the lender’s margin or both. The negotiated terms determine the split. A strong company may secure attractive terms while already holding cash, whereas a financially weak applicant can remain difficult to finance.

A **worked example** shows the scale of the rate cut: RMB200 billion refinanced in full for a year at a rate reduced by 25 basis points, or 0.25 percentage points, would save banks **RMB500 million in interest**. The calculation is RMB200 billion × 0.0025 × one year. It assumes a constant balance and application of the new rate throughout the period. Actual savings require the dates and amounts of individual refinancing transactions. [S03](#source-S03)

## Different columns give the billions different meanings

Around the 2 November meeting, the authorities reported roughly **RMB440 billion in signed loans** and **more than RMB280 billion in cumulative disbursements** since the scheme began. Two days later, the PBOC announced **more than RMB200 billion in refinancing-quota applications** submitted after that meeting. These figures describe contracts, lending delivered and requests to the central bank. Their overlapping stages call for separate reading. [S07](#source-S07) [S03](#source-S03)

The securities regulator, the CSRC, also used a figure close to RMB440 billion in its 2 November response to reporters. There it described the aggregate upper limits of loan applications, including the state-owned platforms China Chengtong and China Reform. That scope differs from signed contracts. The publications retain their respective definitions and provide no reconciliation table. [S08](#source-S08) [S07](#source-S07)

The much-cited **RMB800 billion** has a different origin again. In May 2025, the authorities pooled capacity across two instruments: the share-buyback and stake-purchase lending facility, and the SFISF securities-swap facility for eligible securities firms, funds and insurers. Dividing 440 by 800 would use a shared funding capacity to produce a misleading utilisation rate for buyback loans alone. [S06](#source-S06) [S09](#source-S09)

<ChinaBuybackCredit2026 figure={2} lang="en" />

A programme balance sheet would need available quotas, drawings, repayments and renewals measured on a common date. Cumulative disbursements can stay high after some loans have been repaid. A signed contract can authorise future drawings. Attributing a stock-market move to the policy requires similar care: a support announcement can alter expectations before funds arrive, while some purchases might have proceeded using other resources.

## Repurchased shares can return to the market

In a secondary-market transaction, the seller receives the purchase money. A company buying its own stock pays departing shareholders. A major shareholder increasing its stake also buys existing shares. The listed company receives new funding only if the transaction involves a new issue, outside the route described here.

The next question is the destination of the stock. Qiangrui plans to use half its repurchased shares for equity incentives or an employee share-ownership plan. The other half is earmarked for resale at an appropriate time under its value-support policy. Its programme envisages holding shares before returning them to circulation through different channels. Stock still unused three years after the buyback is completed must be cancelled through the required procedures. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

<ChinaBuybackCredit2026 figure={3} lang="en" />

Cancellation permanently reduces the share count. A later sale or employee transfer creates a different schedule of supply and dilution, as our analysis of [Microsoft and Airbus buybacks](/en/analysis/share-buybacks-anti-dilution-microsoft-airbus/) explains. Qiangrui combines purchases today with two future uses. How much of its first purchases was funded by a bank drawing remains to be documented. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

## Avary changes the funding of an existing plan

Avary Holding illustrates the facility’s appeal to a company that already has a buyback programme. Its board approved a change in funding for the remaining purchases on 13 November. The announcement dated 14 November disclosed a CITIC Bank commitment of up to **RMB153 million**, for a maximum of three years. The programme’s other terms remain unchanged. [S12](#source-S12)

Avary says the switch will preserve cash for capital expenditure, dividends and operations. It presents the decision as a financing choice and says its liquidity is adequate. The loan may be drawn partly or left unused, depending on its needs. Those explanations set out the company’s purpose and its financing options. Future accounts will show how the preserved cash is used. [S12](#source-S12)

This raises the issue of [financial additionality](/en/glossary/additionnalite-financiere/): the activity or financing conditions made possible by an intervention. Credit can replace funding allocated to a buyback already approved. It can also preserve worthwhile projects by freeing cash. Distinguishing those effects requires purchases, investment spending and project schedules to be followed together.

Sanhua provides another comparison. Its 6 November filing recalls a programme adopted in July and funded from its own resources. The company reported **RMB222,959,089.84 in cumulative purchases by 5 November**, excluding costs, before discussing the appeal of the new refinancing policy. The disclosed purchases belong to its existing programme. Its stated interest in the facility concerns the next stage. [S11](#source-S11)

The impact on productive investment depends on several responses. Sellers may reinvest the proceeds. Borrowers retain cash while committing future borrowing capacity. Measuring the national effect requires those flows to be tracked against a credible account of the decisions that would have been made without the support.

## Risk follows the borrower

A company borrowing to repurchase its own shares retains the debt. [Treasury shares](/en/glossary/actions-propres/) receive a particular accounting treatment: the Chinese standard deducts the repurchase price and costs from equity. Repayment depends on operating cash flows and the company’s other resources. [S10](#source-S10)

A borrowing shareholder, by contrast, holds shares in another entity against its debt. The stock price changes while the principal remains due. In a **hypothetical example**, an RMB100 million purchase funded with RMB90 million of debt and RMB10 million of equity loses the entire initial equity contribution after a 10% fall in the shares. At −20%, the portfolio is worth RMB80 million against RMB90 million of debt: the transaction’s net position is −RMB10 million before interest and costs. This is leverage within one isolated transaction. Overall repayment capacity also depends on income, other assets and guarantees.

Maturities add another constraint. The rules were eased to permit borrower loans of up to three years and reduce the minimum own-funds contribution from 30% to 10%. The central-bank refinancing announced in November remains a one-year facility, extendable according to banks’ needs. Extension helps manage liquidity, while the underlying loan still depends on the debtor’s resources. [S06](#source-S06) [S03](#source-S03)

## A lasting backstop needs selective lenders

On 2 November, the CSRC announced the scheme’s transition to a permanent arrangement. It called for more prominent examples among major index constituents and important technology companies, alongside faster execution of repurchase programmes. That policy direction coexists with banks’ responsibility for lending decisions and credit risk. [S08](#source-S08) [S04](#source-S04)

Those interests can align when sound companies buy shares they consider undervalued. Predictable funding can also limit disorderly selling during market stress. Selection becomes harder if the success of the support is judged mainly by lending volume. A lender must retain the ability to refuse an applicant whose repayment prospects look uncertain.

In its China report published in February 2026, IMF staff recommended confining stock-market intervention to the protection of financial stability. It highlighted risks to price formation and the potential for herding. Written before the November announcements, that analysis offers a framework for examining the programme’s incentives. [S13](#source-S13)

The IMF’s financial-system assessment published in April 2025 also distinguished the buffers of large banks from greater vulnerabilities at some smaller and medium-sized institutions. Those differences call for scrutiny of borrower quality and lending concentrations at individual banks. [S14](#source-S14)

The decisive follow-up will link applications, commitments, drawings and trades for each transaction, then track repayments and the destination of the stock. **Beijing is lowering the price of funding. Companies choose how to use it, and their balance sheets will carry the outcome.**

## Limits

This documentary investigation uses regulatory texts, institutional statements and company disclosures. Aggregate figures reproduce the authorities’ reporting without independent audit. The three company cases illustrate mechanisms within a limited sample. Public documents leave private contract terms, the reconciliation of some reporting scopes and the drawings funding individual purchases open for further investigation. The leverage example and interest calculation are reproducible illustrations excluding fees and tax. Measuring causal effects on share prices, investment or bank losses would require a separate study.
