*When investment firms buy insurers · Article 2/8 · Why buy an insurer?*

**Apollo was already managing Athene’s investments before the two companies merged. KKR paid a second time for Global Atlantic without acquiring its portfolio twice. Brookfield and Blackstone have found other ways to divide ownership and management fees. These transactions reveal four ways of putting a value on the years for which savings remain invested, together with the obligations that come with them.**

The [first instalment on AT&T pensions](/en/analysis/when-pension-contracts-change-hands/) follows the contract to the insurer that owes the payments. This article examines the rights investment managers acquire when buying an insurer.

On January 1, 2022, Apollo and Athene complete their combination: the former asset manager and the insurer’s holding company become direct subsidiaries of the new Apollo Global Management. The consolidated accounts now include a large retirement insurer, together with its investments, contracts and obligations. Yet on the eve of the merger, Apollo was already managing or advising on **$212.6 billion of assets in Athene accounts**. The client relationship was firmly established before the two companies brought their ownership together. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-s05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a>, <a href="#source-s06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

That sequence raises a question. If an investment firm can manage an insurer’s money and collect fees without owning it outright, why spend billions buying out its shareholders? The portfolio explains part of the attraction, but the transaction also concerns the durability of the mandate, control over decisions and the share of insurance earnings that goes to the owner. Each of those rights has a different value and carries different responsibilities.

The Apollo–Athene, KKR–Global Atlantic and Brookfield–American Equity transactions allow us to trace those rights through acquisition documents and published accounts. Blackstone’s partnership with Corebridge offers a comparison: a minority stake accompanies a contract to manage tens of billions of dollars. Understanding these deals means keeping both sides of the promise in view: the income that savings can generate over many years and the payments that the insurer must make over those same years. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a>, <a href="#source-s25" aria-label="Source 25">25</a>, <a href="#source-s37" aria-label="Source 37">37</a></sup>

## The price of the shares and the policyholders’ money

In a share acquisition, the seller receives the agreed price and the buyer takes its place as an owner. Investments remain on the balance sheets of the companies that hold them, alongside those companies’ liabilities. The buyer gains a claim on earnings and, depending on its stake, the power to direct the business; policyholders retain their contractual claims. This distinction matters whenever a deal announcement places a purchase price of a few billion dollars beside assets worth many tens of billions. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s43" aria-label="Source 43">43</a>, <a href="#source-s28" aria-label="Source 28">28</a></sup>

American Equity Investment Life Holding Company, or AEL, provides a particularly clear example. In July 2023, Brookfield Reinsurance announces a transaction valuing AEL’s equity at about **$4.3 billion**, then completes the purchase of the remaining shares on **May 2, 2024**. In the final accounts of Brookfield Wealth Solutions, the buyer’s new name, the cash and shares delivered amount to **$3.636 billion**. Brookfield already held a stake, so the announced valuation of all the shares and the payment for the remaining shares relate to different dates and scopes. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s25" aria-label="Source 25">25</a>, <a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source 27">27</a>, <a href="#source-s28" aria-label="Source 28">28</a>, <a href="#source-s32" aria-label="Source 32">32</a></sup>

The acquired balance sheet is much larger. The final purchase price allocation records **$75.166 billion of identifiable assets** and **$71.123 billion of liabilities**, measured at the acquisition date. Those liabilities include $61.473 billion of policyholder account balances. The $4.043 billion difference between assets and liabilities is an accounting balance before non-controlling interests; how much can be used to fund dividends depends on other requirements, including insurance capital rules. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s28" aria-label="Source 28">28</a></sup>

The composition of the assets also calls for careful language. Alongside investments and cash, the table includes the accounting value of the acquired insurance business and other intangible assets. The value of business acquired, or VOBA, arises from the accounting and actuarial valuation of the acquired contracts and is amortized over their remaining lives. This intangible asset has a different liquidity profile from a bond or a bank deposit. The roughly $75 billion balance sheet, the $49 billion that will enter the investment manager’s fee-bearing capital base and the price paid for the shares therefore need to retain their separate labels. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s28" aria-label="Source 28">28</a>, <a href="#source-s30" aria-label="Source 30">30</a></sup>

What the buyer acquires is a business able to receive premiums, invest and pay benefits. Its US contracts, often grouped under the term *annuities*, also include savings products during their accumulation phase; the business extends beyond lifetime pensions already in payment. The timing of cash outflows, the guarantees offered and policyholders’ ability to withdraw their money all shape the assets that an insurer can reasonably hold. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s25" aria-label="Source 25">25</a>, <a href="#source-s43" aria-label="Source 43">43</a></sup>

## Apollo buys a more durable relationship and insurance earnings

Athene illustrates what an acquisition can add when the main investment management mandate already exists. In its March 2021 announcement, Apollo presents the combination as a way to make the relationship more durable and link the growth of the insurance business directly to the growth of asset management. At closing, the new Apollo shares issued as consideration are valued at **$11.455 billion**. This closing value pays for equity rights; the $212.6 billion managed at the end of 2021 measures a mandate. The acquisition note separately reconciles the full accounting consideration. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>, <a href="#source-s06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a>, <a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

For an investment manager, a secure mandate changes how it plans ahead. A credit team hires people, builds relationships with borrowers and arranges financing in the expectation of having capital to invest over time. Bringing an insurer into the group can make that activity more predictable, provided new business and portfolio reinvestment offset benefit payments and withdrawals. Here, durability takes the form of a commercial relationship that the shareholder can organize more closely, while each insurance contract continues to have its own payment schedule.

The acquisition also increases the share of insurance earnings attributable to the group: Apollo already held 28.4% of Athene’s shares at the end of 2021. For products with guarantees, part of the earnings comes from the difference between investment income and the cost of the obligations. Apollo tracks this activity through [*spread-related earnings* (SRE)](/en/glossary/sre/), a pre-tax, non-GAAP measure after the costs and adjustments included in its definition. The owner gains greater exposure to that margin, to the quality of the assets, to the guarantees and to capital requirements. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

The scale of the obligations is visible from the outset: the merger’s acquisition table records **$219.779 billion of liabilities assumed at fair value**. The insurance companies remain responsible for their contracts, while the shareholder now controls a business whose resources must be sufficient to honor them. The value of the management mandate and the value of the insurance operation come together within the group, along with risks that require different measures. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

## KKR buys a larger share of the same earnings

Global Atlantic offers a view of two successive purchases. When KKR’s acquisition of control closes on **February 1, 2021**, the cost attributable to KKR is approximately **$2.914 billion for 61.5%**, after adjustments. The roughly $4.7 billion highlighted for the transaction refers to the value of the whole business, including other investors’ interests. KKR’s accounts identify **$97.503 billion of additional [assets under management](/en/glossary/aum/)**, also classified as fee-paying assets. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

Three years later, KKR buys the remaining interest of approximately 37%. Final consideration consists of **$2.622 billion in cash and $40.8 million in securities exchangeable for KKR shares**, or approximately **$2.663 billion**. Global Atlantic had already been controlled and consolidated since 2021, so the second purchase increases KKR’s economic interest in a business and portfolio already within the group. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s19" aria-label="Source 19">19</a></sup>

The effect is especially clear in the earnings figures. In 2023, Global Atlantic produces $1,289.7 million of pre-tax operating earnings under KKR’s adjusted measure; after non-controlling interests, $816.6 million is attributable to KKR. In 2024, earnings for the entire business fall to $1,014.5 million, all of which now belongs to KKR. Earnings attributable to KKR **rise by 24.2%**, while earnings for the whole business **fall by 21.3%**. Both changes are calculated from the accounts and relate to explicitly different scopes. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s19" aria-label="Source 19">19</a></sup>

This divergence helps explain what the second purchase pays for. KKR has acquired a claim on a larger fraction of future earnings, together with the corresponding exposure to losses and capital requirements. The increase in attributable earnings reflects that change in ownership; assessing the insurer’s economic performance also requires looking at the earnings of the business as a whole. The annual report explicitly identifies the purchase of the remaining stake as a contributor to the increase in earnings attributable to KKR. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s19" aria-label="Source 19">19</a></sup>

## Brookfield separates the owner from the manager

At Brookfield, similar names cover different functions. Brookfield Reinsurance, renamed Brookfield Wealth Solutions, acquires the insurer. Brookfield Asset Management, or BAM, receives the investment management mandate. These are related companies, but the purchase price, insurance obligations and manager’s revenues appear in separate accounts. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s27" aria-label="Source 27">27</a>, <a href="#source-s31" aria-label="Source 31">31</a>, <a href="#source-s32" aria-label="Source 32">32</a></sup>

That separation makes the manager’s interest particularly visible. In its August 2023 shareholder letter, BAM anticipates approximately **$50 billion of AEL capital to manage** and **$125 million in additional annual [fee-related earnings](/en/glossary/fre/)**, or FRE. This is management’s forecast for an adjusted earnings measure, after the costs included in that measure. The 2024 report subsequently records **$49 billion of fee-bearing capital** added through the AEL mandate. The addition to the manager’s fee base is therefore documented. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s29" aria-label="Source 29">29</a>, <a href="#source-s30" aria-label="Source 30">30</a></sup>

The available evidence goes less far in testing the earnings forecast. The 2025 report attributes a $55 million annual increase in fee revenue to the AEL mandate. That figure measures a **change in revenue**, whereas the forecast of $125 million described an **expected annual level of earnings**. The documents reviewed do not isolate realized AEL FRE that would allow a direct assessment of the 2023 target. The two figures cannot establish whether that target was met. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s29" aria-label="Source 29">29</a>, <a href="#source-s31" aria-label="Source 31">31</a></sup>

The manager’s ambitions also extend beyond the overarching mandate. The same 2023 letter envisages allocating about 40% of insurance capital to its private funds, generating additional fee revenue. That percentage is a target for the insurance capital discussed by BAM, rather than a reported allocation for AEL. It reveals another attraction of durable access to an insurer’s investments: the relationship can support several parts of the manager’s business, from selecting investments to managing specialist vehicles. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s29" aria-label="Source 29">29</a></sup>

Understanding this arrangement requires looking beyond the Brookfield name to the company that bears the guarantees, the company that charges for investment management and the shareholders who receive their earnings. The value of the owner’s investment also depends on the insurance business’s capital needs; the manager is paid for a service whose fee base and terms are determined by contract. The economic division runs between those functions, even when they sit within the same network of ownership relationships.

## A fee moves through the group

Apollo’s and KKR’s accounts make it possible to trace the fees recorded between the two businesses. In 2025, Apollo reports **$1,441 million of management fees between its insurance and asset management businesses**. KKR reports **$673.9 million** charged by its asset management segment to its insurance segment. Each amount is revenue for the manager and an expense for the insurer within the respective group. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a>, <a href="#source-s20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

As early as February 2021, KKR’s management was targeting at least $200 million in additional annual net management fees once the business had ramped up. That forecast combined the general mandate and directly managed strategies, then deducted certain expenses. The subsequent series of internal fees measures gross revenue; assessing whether the target was achieved would require reconciling costs and reporting scopes. As at Brookfield, the announcement explains the economic benefit being sought, while tracking its delivery requires a consistent measure from beginning to end. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

In consolidated accounts, the internal revenue and corresponding expense are eliminated. The investment teams still incur costs, the assets still generate income and the insurer still has obligations, but a payment between two businesses in the same group does not by itself create additional external revenue. The reported insurance earnings already reflect these fees. Any reconstruction must therefore begin with the definition of each measure, to avoid deducting the same expense twice or treating a gross fee as net profit. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a>, <a href="#source-s20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

These charges still matter economically within each company. Fees compensate the investment management business and affect its profitability; their level reduces the investment income retained by the insurer. Assessing whether the arrangement creates value requires examining the service provided, its cost, the performance achieved and the capital at risk. Rising fees may accompany a larger portfolio or a different mix of investments. Their amount alone does not measure either an improvement in the return on savings or a loss to policyholders.

Athene’s own disclosures describe the incentive that deserves scrutiny. Its documentation distinguishes a base fee from fees linked to asset categories, and its risk factors acknowledge that higher remuneration on certain investments may encourage the manager to allocate more to them, without guaranteeing a higher return for the insurer. That disclosure provides a precise line of inquiry: who approves the allocations, and what net results are used to assess them? It establishes a potential incentive; determining whether any fees are excessive requires further evidence. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

## Blackstone puts a value on a secure contract

The partnership with AIG’s life and retirement business, now Corebridge, shows how far a mandate can reach alongside a minority investment. Announced on **July 14, 2021**, the agreement provides for **$2.2 billion in exchange for a 9.9% equity stake** and an initial mandate to manage **$50 billion of existing assets**. The transaction closes on November 2. The minority investment and management relationship are arranged together, with distinct rights and sources of remuneration. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s37" aria-label="Source 37">37</a>, <a href="#source-s38" aria-label="Source 38">38</a></sup>

At the end of 2025, Corebridge reports that Blackstone manages **$71.2 billion of its assets at book value**. The schedule calls for $92.5 billion by the third quarter of 2027. The contract provides visibility well beyond the size of the equity stake: the assets entrusted to Blackstone, their scheduled growth and the termination provisions all shape the relationship. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s40" aria-label="Source 40">40</a>, <a href="#source-s41" aria-label="Source 41">41</a></sup>

The published fee schedule makes that visibility tangible. The annual rate is **0.30% on the original assets allocated**, rising to **0.45% on additional assets and on the original amount as it is reinvested**. Applied to $1 billion of eligible assets for a full year, those rates generate $3 million and $4.5 million in fees respectively. This illustrative calculation uses a constant fee base; an actual bill depends on the assets and terms involved. The schedule excludes investments in funds or structures sponsored by Blackstone or an affiliate, or entitling them to other fees. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s40" aria-label="Source 40">40</a></sup>

Another clause contributes to the mandate’s value. If the insurance subsidiaries fail to allocate the scheduled amounts at the quarterly deadlines, Corebridge remains liable for fees on the amount that should have been allocated. The commitment therefore provides a minimum level of remuneration as well. The contract also contains termination mechanisms, whose terms determine how secure the relationship is over time. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s40" aria-label="Source 40">40</a>, <a href="#source-s41" aria-label="Source 41">41</a></sup>

Corebridge’s recorded expenses for Blackstone’s services reach **$175 million in 2023, $243 million in 2024 and $323 million in 2025**. The interim accounts report $176 million for the first half of 2026. These are expenses incurred by the insurance client, from which Blackstone’s net profit cannot be inferred. They nevertheless establish that substantial management revenue can develop alongside an initial minority equity investment. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s39" aria-label="Source 39">39</a>, <a href="#source-s42" aria-label="Source 42">42</a></sup>

On the issuer page reviewed on October 23, 2026, Corebridge describes its proposed combination with Equitable as pending closing, with the companies continuing to operate separately until then. The management terms and expenses examined here retain the dates of their source documents. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s47" aria-label="Source 47">47</a></sup>

This arrangement completes the comparison with the three acquisitions. Apollo strengthens an established relationship; KKR increases its share of insurance earnings; Brookfield adds a fee base while distributing functions across related companies; Blackstone combines a minority investment with a long-term contract. The attraction of an acquisition depends on the right the investment manager seeks to secure and the risk it is willing to bear to obtain it.

## Duration has a value, and so does risk

The strongest economic argument offered by these groups deserves serious examination. An insurer with predictable cash outflows can finance long-term loans and projects, while a manager able to select and monitor those investments can improve their fit with the insurer’s obligations. The authors of a study published by the BIS highlight both the capital and expertise these firms bring and the vulnerabilities associated with less liquid assets. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s43" aria-label="Source 43">43</a></sup>

A Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago working paper by Ralf Meisenzahl, Jackson Overpeck and Andy Polacek, revised on **April 27, 2026**, offers a more specific finding: certain forms of private credit improve maturity and cash-flow matching, with effects concentrated in the US market for *indexed annuities*, a form of indexed savings product. The finding applies within the scope of those products and that research; it cannot certify the quality of every portfolio held by the groups examined here. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s44" aria-label="Source 44">44</a></sup>

Distinguishing economic efficiency from a transfer of revenue therefore requires examining the portfolios themselves. Any additional yield must be assessed alongside losses, liquidity, guarantees and all the fees involved. A bond that is difficult to sell may suit a distant payment obligation yet become problematic if policyholders withdraw their savings faster than expected. The owner must also account for the capital committed to the business: a claim on more potential earnings brings greater exposure to the insurer’s results. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-s43" aria-label="Source 43">43</a>, <a href="#source-s09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

The public documents assembled here allow us to compare acquired rights, management fee bases and several streams of fees. Important questions remain open, including the net return an independent manager would have achieved, the full cost of each chain of investment vehicles and the earnings attributable to particular mandates. Ranking the acquisitions by profitability would require those data, together with capital contributions and distributions measured consistently over time.

For policyholders, the question ultimately concerns how this arrangement pays the manager while preserving the resources needed to fulfill the promise. Buying the insurer gives the owner influence over the duration of the relationship, asset allocation and residual earnings. The quality of the transaction will depend on how those rights are used: fees justified by the service provided, capital sufficient for the obligations and investments capable of funding benefits through to the final payment.
