On 19 October 2026, the *Financial Times* reported a complaint to the UK’s Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) about private-credit swaps being allocated to the bank’s trading book. A matching swap was reportedly part of the justification. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Barclays says its accounting and capital treatment complies with the rules, supported by internal governance and external advisers. The public material examined for this investigation leaves the contractual details and the outcome of the reported scrutiny unresolved. The mechanics of a hedge identify the payments and collateral on which its resilience depends. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Contractual hedges can protect a lender by passing a loan’s economic performance to another party. They also introduce payment commitments and, depending on the terms, collateral requirements. The strength of that protection depends on the resources the counterparties can deliver when loan values fall. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-S08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

## Transferring a loan’s total return

Consider a bank that owns a loan and an investor that wants exposure to its performance. Under a [*total return swap*, or TRS](/en/glossary/trs/), the bank pays the investor amounts reflecting the loan’s income and any appreciation. The investor makes a financing payment in the other direction. When performance turns negative, the investor bears the decline under the agreed terms. Economic exposure can therefore change hands while legal ownership of the loan remains with the original holder. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-S15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

The distinction from an outright sale becomes clear when something goes wrong. The owner retains its claim on the borrower, while the swap introduces a further payment obligation. An assessment must follow both the indebted business and the derivative’s [counterparty risk](/en/glossary/risque-de-contrepartie/). The contract can pass a credit loss to that counterparty, provided it can perform its obligations. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>, <a href="#source-S08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

Take a deliberately simple movement: a loan is worth 100 and then 80, with no coupon or financing costs during the period. If the swap transfers precisely that decline and the party owing the money pays, the holder receives 20 under the derivative, offsetting the asset’s 20 fall in value. The economic protection is real. These figures isolate a mechanism; they do not describe a Barclays transaction.

“Repackaging” can consequently mean more than one thing. It may describe a change in presentation, but it can also involve a substantial change in rights to income and responsibility for losses. Assessing a structure requires identifying the risk that has been transferred, then examining the claim that replaces it on the lender’s side. A strong promise of reimbursement and a fragile one can look identical in a flow diagram. Their behaviour in a crisis can be very different.

This distinction also helps keep separate product categories in view. A TRS transfers the economic performance of a reference obligation. Recognition of a significant risk transfer, or SRT, involves a different prudential assessment, particularly in securitisation. The public Barclays allegations concern the classification of derivatives. The product name alone does not establish that the trades constitute an SRT securitisation. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a>, <a href="#source-S11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup>

## Notional, market value and reported exposure

In its statement reported by Reuters on 19 October, Barclays distinguished **£15 billion of total private-credit exposure**, predominantly in the banking book, **roughly £1.5 billion of underlying notional assets** for the loan TRS, and **about £300 million in the mark-to-market value of its exposure** to those swaps. These figures should retain their attribution: they are the bank’s disclosures in response to the report. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S02" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

[Notional](/en/glossary/notionnel/) gives the scale of the contractual references; mark-to-market gives a valuation at a particular time. Translating either into a possible loss requires information about future payments, collateral and enforceable netting. The available statement does not give enough detail to make that conversion. Dividing £300 million by £1.5 billion produces 20% as arithmetic, without establishing a loss rate. Comparing the notional directly with the £15 billion of total exposure would raise a similar problem of definitions and scope.

The distinction matters throughout the investigation. **A derivative amount, a regulatory exposure and a capital requirement answer different questions.** The first describes a contractual activity, the second applies a risk measurement method, and the third follows from the relevant capital framework. Estimating capital savings, or the cost of a possible reclassification, would require more information.

## Collateral brings the shock forward into the fund’s cash needs

A Barclays presentation published on 28 July provides a useful worked example. Slide 33 describes a vehicle holding **£350 million of loans**, funded by **£200 million of senior secured bank debt** and **£150 million of equity**. The fund’s investors have committed more than £150 million. The structure gives the bank a cushion: asset values can fall before the collateral is worth as little as the senior claim. This is an illustration of lending to a vehicle, separate from the swaps discussed in October. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The debt-to-[collateral](/en/glossary/collateral/) ratio, or *loan-to-value* (LTV), begins at 200 divided by 350, equal to **57.1%**. The slide specifies an LTV limit below 60%. A 10% fall reduces the assets to £315 million and raises LTV to **63.5%**. Barclays then illustrates an additional **£20 million of cash or collateral**. The collateral pool rises to £335 million and LTV falls to **59.7%**. The decimal ratios are our calculations from the published amounts; the bank uses rounded percentages. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

For the bank, the arrangement reinforces protection during the life of the loan. The fund must maintain an agreed relationship between debt and collateral, rather than allow the cushion to erode until final maturity. For the fund, the decline in value creates a need to produce resources. It can draw on cash, call available investor commitments or deliver eligible assets. Its response determines how the shock affects the rest of its portfolio. Our margin-call guide works through the [reduction in borrowing capacity](/en/guides/understand-margin-calls-and-collateral/#calculate-the-reduction-in-borrowing-capacity) as collateral values decline.

The same arithmetic shows the distance between the start of that pressure and the exhaustion of gross collateral coverage. With debt of 200 and a 60% threshold, the original 350 of assets need fall only to about 333.3 to reach the limit. That is a decline of **4.8%**, close to the 5% indicated by Barclays. If the original assets remained in place and no collateral were added, their value would fall to the 200 principal amount only after a **42.9% decline**. The bank describes the latter order of magnitude as approximately 40%. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

That second threshold measures equality between asset value and principal owed. A realised loss would also depend on default, liquidation proceeds, any further recourse and costs. The chart keeps those assumptions separate. Its main insight is how early a cash demand can arise in a financing arrangement that still has substantial collateral coverage.

After the 10% decline, the exact addition needed to bring LTV back to 60% is **£18⅓ million**, or approximately £18.3 million. The bank’s stated condition is a ratio below 60%, so a larger addition is required to satisfy it strictly; the £20 million in the example restores that small margin. The calculation reveals the rule behind the scenario. The illustration establishes neither the frequency of margin calls nor any particular clause in the October TRS contracts.

A fund with readily available resources can meet the call and keep its loans. If it needs to sell quickly, the sale price becomes critical. A disposal below the previous valuation can increase pressure on collateral coverage. This is a possible channel through which stress spreads. Its strength depends on liquidity reserves, available commitments, eligible assets and contractual deadlines, all of which need examination before drawing conclusions about an actual vulnerability. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a>, <a href="#source-S13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

## Payment obligations in a matched hedge

A pair of opposing contracts can provide a meaningful hedge. To examine it, take another wholly hypothetical example involving three separate legal persons. A owns the loan, B is an intermediary bank, and C receives its economic performance. A passes the total return to B, which passes it on to C. Both swaps use the same references, dates, currencies and calculation terms. Their financing payments offset in this model. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

If the loan falls from 100 to 80, excluding income and fees, its return is minus 20. The direction of payment reverses: **C owes 20 to B, and B owes 20 to A**. With both contracts performed, the bank receives and pays the same amount. Its exposure to the movement being modelled is neutralised. Ignoring that genuine reduction would misrepresent what the structure does.

Now change one assumption: C defaults. Suppose B already holds 8 of cash collateral that is immediately available, and recovers nothing further. B remains obliged to pay 20 to A. Its conditional loss is **12**: the claim of 20 less the 8 recovered from collateral. The scenario excludes closeout costs, further price moves and a default by A or B. It illustrates a dependency, with no assigned probability and no calibration to Barclays.

The legal explanation fits the arithmetic. Under the Basel framework, a bilateral [netting set](/en/glossary/ensemble-de-compensation/) groups transactions with a single counterparty under an enforceable agreement. B’s claim against C and its debt to A involve different contractual parties. Our example excludes novation or a multilateral arrangement that would change that structure. The obligation to pay A consequently survives C’s failure to pay. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

A timing mismatch has a different result. If C eventually pays its 20 after B’s payment to A is due, B must fund the intervening period. Its final nominal balance returns to zero, before funding costs. This variant concerns the settlement timetable; the previous scenario assumes default and limited recovery. Keeping the two distinct shows why offsetting changes in value can still leave a liquidity need. Our interest-rate swap guide examines [clearing and margin](/en/guides/read-interest-rate-swaps/#clearing-and-margin-the-safeguard-that-can-bite) for those instruments; its examples leave the clearing arrangements of Barclays’ TRS open.

The Basel Committee’s final guidelines call for banks to examine margin, the availability of collateral, closeout periods and exposures that remain when protections work less effectively under stress. If the market movement increasing a counterparty’s obligation also weakens its ability to pay, the hedge becomes more vulnerable just as it becomes more valuable. That relationship needs to be tested against contracts and portfolios whose details remain unknown in this case. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

## Trading-book treatment requires supporting evidence

The prudential boundary determines how positions are treated for capital purposes. The banking book and the trading book organise that assessment under different rules. Accounting classification is a separate question: Article 105 of the UK Rulebook itself provides for positions outside the trading book that are measured at fair value. Prudential classification therefore needs to be assessed directly, rather than inferred from the accounting valuation method alone. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

**The rule applicable in October 2026 expressly recognises hedgeability.** Article 102(1) requires a trading-book position to be freely tradable or capable of being hedged. The surrounding provisions address trading intent, active management, documentation and prudent valuation. For model-valued positions, Article 103(1)(c)(ii) also requires policies to assess how far material risks can be hedged using instruments with an active, liquid two-way market. This gives a matching swap a substantive role in the argument, while requiring evidence that the hedge and the relevant controls are effective. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

For an outside reader, the useful test connects contractual design with actual risk management. Two trades can match in notional while differing in dates, reference assets, price-setting methods or termination clauses. Even where their terms align, a counterparty’s default can change the remaining exposure. Trade confirmations, collateral agreements and risk assessments therefore matter more to the conclusion than the product name. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a>, <a href="#source-S09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

The PRA’s letter to chief financial officers dated **9 April 2025** provides relevant context. It asks banks to ensure capital treatment reflects the substance of financing arrangements and the liquidity of underlying collateral. Its warning about repackaging illiquid assets addresses accompanying securities financing transactions, or SFTs, in a letter principally prompted by SRT financing. The PRA also asks firms to consider other relevant financing portfolios. The letter explains the demand for supporting evidence; its scope is distinct from an individual ruling on Barclays’ swaps. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

Timing adds a further consideration. The new UK trading-book boundary and most of Basel 3.1 take effect on **1 January 2027**. The new internal-model approach for market risk follows on **1 January 2028**. Those future rules help explain the direction of reform. Allegations about treatment examined in October 2026 must be assessed against the framework applicable at that time. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

## Capital requirements and residual exposure

A bank’s own funds absorb losses and form part of its balance-sheet funding. Liquidity enables it to make a payment when due. The distinction explains why the bank in the delayed-payment scenario might need cash even though the two flows will ultimately balance.

Prudential classification affects a separate relationship. At a given required capital ratio, lower risk-weighted assets reduce the capital requirement calculated on those assets. The effect on the capital actually needed also depends on other applicable constraints. This can improve the return measured against the capital allocated to the activity. The question is whether the lower requirement corresponds to an adequately measured reduction in risk. Market risk, counterparty risk and, where applicable, credit valuation adjustment frameworks address different dimensions of the exposure. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a>, <a href="#source-S14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup>

The UK Rulebook offers a useful example. Under the standardised approach to counterparty credit risk, exposure combines replacement cost and potential future exposure, with a regulatory multiplier and rules for netting and collateral. A snapshot of market value therefore captures only part of the problem. For Barclays, the relevant netting sets, collateral and method actually used would all be needed. The disclosed £300 million is insufficient to calculate risk-weighted assets or an additional capital requirement. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup>

This limit matters when assessing attractive numerical claims. The evidence identifies channels through which capital treatment could change. The amount, direction and materiality of any reclassification would have to be established from the actual positions.

## Loan valuations and executable sale prices

Valuation connects all these questions. Our investigation into [reported valuations and market prices in private credit](/en/analysis/private-credit-one-asset-two-prices/) explores that gap. It determines the performance passed through a swap, can trigger a demand for collateral and informs the risk assessment. A loan that rarely trades may be valued using expected repayments, discount rates and market comparables. The Financial Stability Board’s (FSB) report of 6 May 2026 describes these practices and differences in valuation frequency, with quarterly updates common among the funds studied. That sector-wide observation does not establish the frequency used in Barclays’ contracts. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

The July materials supply a relevant qualification: Barclays says it has daily revaluation rights over a large part of the private-credit financing portfolio it presents. Such a right allows a lender to respond more promptly to new information. Its exercise and the reliability of the underlying data remain separate questions. A collateral demand, a fund’s published valuation and the price obtained in a disposal may operate on different timetables. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a>, <a href="#source-S13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

Return to the £350 million vehicle. A valuation of 315 generates a need to restore coverage. If that requires a sale, the cash realised depends on the buyers available and the time allowed. An investor able to wait for repayments may instead ride out a period of poor liquidity without selling. The risk develops where a long-lived asset meets an obligation to produce resources sooner.

The supervisor itself recognises that these protections have value. In its July Financial Stability Report, the Bank of England notes that seniority, diversified collateral and margining can reduce banks’ expected losses when financing non-bank institutions. Its September assessment continued to describe the UK banking system as appropriately capitalised and liquid, while identifying vulnerability to tighter financing conditions in parts of private credit. An analysis of a particular structure needs to preserve both levels of observation. [Bank financing of private-credit funds](/en/analysis/private-credit-test-of-patience/#banks-fund-the-lender-as-well-as-the-borrower) provides another channel for that interdependence. <sup className="l0g-barclays-ref"><a href="#source-S11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a>, <a href="#source-S12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

## Testing the contracts under stress

For the Barclays case, the most informative evidence would connect both legs of the swaps with the risks for which capital is held. A deterioration in the loans would need to be followed through valuations, collateral demands, payments and potential closeout. Details of enforceable netting and available collateral would then allow the remaining exposure to be assessed. The internal and external support cited by the bank would acquire a form that an outside reader could evaluate.

A hedge with matching references, dependable payments and realisable collateral would strengthen its defence. Divergent valuations, incompatible maturities or protection dependent on a counterparty vulnerable to the same shock would call for a reassessment of the residual risk and its treatment. These are the conditions that could distinguish the competing interpretations. Examining them fully requires evidence beyond the public material assembled here.

**Changing the contracts can change a lender’s risk by reallocating losses and payment obligations**. The borrower’s credit quality remains the starting point; the quality of the protection then determines how deterioration travels through the structure. The Barclays case calls for following that chain all the way to the final payment, especially the party that must find money when collateral loses value.
