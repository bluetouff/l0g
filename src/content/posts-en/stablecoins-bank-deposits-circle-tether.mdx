[Stablecoins](/en/glossary/stablecoin/) are digital tokens designed to track a currency, here the US dollar. With Circle’s [USDC](/en/glossary/usdc/) and Tether’s [USDT](/en/glossary/usdt/), reserve investment connects the money in bank accounts with the cost and stability of funding for US banks.

*Documentary investigation and analysis ; 8 November 2026. Reserve data as of 30 September 2026. Numerical examples identified as illustrations were constructed by l0g.*

Imagine a bank losing $100. Its customer uses the money to obtain stablecoins: tokens designed to hold a value of one dollar and move across a blockchain. When the payment settles, another bank receives the funds in the issuer’s account. The customer now owns tokens; a company owns the bank deposit.

This simplified transaction captures the challenge for banks. An institution can lose a customer relationship, a payment account and a cheap source of funding while the dollars continue circulating through the banking system. The next stage depends on where the issuer places them: in a deposit, in government debt or in a secured loan to a financial intermediary. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b04">B04</a></sup>

A bank has a familiar way to compete for that money: pay more interest. Research published by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) finds that stablecoin demand responds to US deposit rates. The rate offered to the customer, however, is only the beginning of the story. The rest unfolds inside Circle’s and Tether’s reserves, then on the balance sheets of the institutions receiving the funds. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b01">B01</a></sup>

## A new claim on the dollar

Consider a new issue at par, with no fees, funded within the US banking system. In this illustrative example, an eligible buyer transfers $100 from bank A. The issuer receives it at bank B and creates 100 tokens. The buyer’s deposit falls by $100 and the issuer’s rises by the same amount. Within this closed example, total bank deposits are unchanged. Their ownership has shifted.

Settlement between A and B also moves their balances at the Federal Reserve (Fed), the US central bank. A transfers $100 of settlement reserves; B receives $100. Those interbank reserves settle the payment. The assets the issuer keeps to back its tokens are the **stablecoin’s reserves**. The same word describes two different layers of the financial system. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r03">R03</a></sup>

<StablecoinDepositFigure lang="en" kind="mint" />

The buyer now holds a different claim. A deposit is money owed by a bank. A stablecoin rests on the issuer’s obligations and the applicable redemption terms. Direct redemption may depend on eligibility and contractual conditions; holders can also use a platform or sell their tokens. Tracking the effect on bank funding therefore requires separating token issuance and redemption from trading between users. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r04">R04</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r05">R05</a></sup>

Suppose Alice buys 100 existing tokens from Ben. Her payment may simply credit Ben’s bank account. The number of tokens outstanding stays the same. Treating the trade as another $100 of stablecoin reserves would confuse the transfer of an asset with its creation. For the same reason, blockchain transaction volume answers a different question from the amount of bank deposits displaced.

## Deposit rates change the cost of holding tokens

For someone keeping dollars readily available, a better bank rate raises the opportunity cost of holding a stablecoin: the interest forgone by choosing the token. How much that matters depends on the purpose of the balance. Money earmarked for an imminent payment bears that cost for hours or days; a cash reserve bears it for months. The same rate increase can be decisive for one user and barely relevant to another.

Ahmed and Aldasoro study the period from January 2019 to June 2025. Their paper, published by the BIS on 13 October 2026 following an initial version in March, links higher deposit rates to slower subsequent stablecoin growth, particularly for USDC. The estimated magnitude varies by method. The analysis concerns demand for an asset; it identifies neither the individual depositors involved nor an aggregate loss of credit. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b01">B01</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b08">B08</a></sup>



The economic direction is useful: a bank can improve the relative appeal of its account. Translating a statistical coefficient into a commercial decision takes more work. A response estimated from small weekly changes in a particular setting provides a research benchmark. A bank would still need to identify the customers sensitive to rates, how much money they might move and how competitors would respond.

The practical question is how much a dollar of retained funding is worth. Imagine a bank with **$1 billion in deposits**, of which **$100 million** is at risk of leaving. An increase of **0.10 percentage points**, or ten basis points, across the whole deposit base costs **$1 million a year**, assuming balances remain constant. Replacing the departing $100 million with funding priced **one percentage point above** the original funding cost produces the same annual increase in expense.

This is arithmetic built from illustrative assumptions, with no observed rate or forecast of withdrawals. It assumes the general rate increase retains all $100 million at risk, replacement funding is available and other costs stay the same. It nevertheless exposes the commercial difficulty: a bank may have to pay more to many customers who had no intention of leaving. A targeted offer changes the calculation; its feasibility and effectiveness would need to be established.

<StablecoinDepositFigure lang="en" kind="funding" />

Competition delivers a gain to the saver and a higher interest bill to the bank. The bank can absorb the expense in its margin, seek other revenue, change its services or adjust lending terms. Its market and balance sheet shape the response. In a historical review published in May 2026, Federal Reserve economists describe earlier banking adaptations to money market funds and new payment technologies through changes in products and strategy. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r01">R01</a></sup>

## Where the reserves go

Circle’s disclosures let us follow the next stage. On **30 September 2026**, assets backing USDC totalled **$73.957 billion**. Of that, **$62.467 billion** consisted of shares in the Circle Reserve Fund, a money market fund. The reserve attestation looks through the fund to its holdings: **$36.654 billion** in overnight loans secured by Treasury securities, **$25.362 billion** in Treasuries, plus cash and settlement adjustments. The remaining reserve assets were held outside the fund. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-i01">I01</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-i02">I02</a></sup>

A money market fund adds a legal intermediary. Understanding where the money goes economically requires examining what the fund owns. Adding the value of its shares to the value of its portfolio would count the same investment twice.

The secured loans are known as [*reverse repos*](/en/guides/read-repo-market-sofr/) from the cash lender’s perspective. The lender advances money for a short period and receives securities as collateral, with a reverse transaction agreed. Circle’s attestation describes overnight lending to global financial institutions, overcollateralised by US Treasury securities. A dollar can therefore finance an intermediary through a short-term secured claim whose terms differ from an ordinary deposit. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-i01">I01</a></sup>



Tether’s structure is different. Its report for **30 September 2026** lists **$191.451 billion in assets**, including **$122.840 billion in US Treasury bills** and **$16.696 billion in reverse repos**. Cash and bank deposits amount to **$20.427 million**. The scope is all assets of Tether International backing its fiat-denominated tokens, including surplus assets. That differs from the USDC reserve perimeter. The report expressly identifies Treasury collateral for overnight repos; the term-repo note describes collateral and credit-quality requirements more generally. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-i03">I03</a></sup>

A small bank-deposit line tells us what the issuer owned at a particular moment. Establishing a withdrawal from the banking system would also require knowing who sold it the securities, where the payment arrived and what the recipient did with the proceeds. A portfolio statement leaves that part of the journey unresolved.

## The Treasury seller matters

Return to the $100 sitting in the issuer’s US bank account. The issuer now uses it to buy a Treasury bill. Three transactions that look similar on a screen have different effects within the US banking system.

A **non-bank investor** sells an existing security and receives payment into a bank account. The issuer’s deposit falls by $100; the seller’s rises by $100. At this stage, total deposits are unchanged. The seller might then buy another asset or spend the proceeds. Each subsequent transaction needs its own analysis.

A **bank** sells a security from its own portfolio. It receives settlement in its capacity as a bank, rather than crediting a selling customer’s account. Across the banking sector, securities and deposits both fall by $100. Existing loans can remain unchanged at the moment of the transaction. The balance sheet has contracted through a change in securities holdings, a different process from a loan repayment.

Finally, the issuer buys a **newly issued Treasury security**, paying from its deposit. The proceeds reach the Treasury’s account at the Fed, the *Treasury General Account*, or TGA. In this isolated scenario, bank deposits and settlement reserves both fall by $100. When the Treasury spends the same $100 on a private recipient paid through a US bank account, deposits and reserves return to their starting levels in this example. The timing of government financing and spending becomes important. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r03">R03</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-m05">M05</a></sup>

<StablecoinDepositFigure lang="en" kind="counterparties" />

These are accounting illustrations, with other conditions held constant. They identify counterparties before drawing macroeconomic conclusions. Actual portfolios combine trades, maturities, redemptions, funds and repos. A single figure for stablecoin issuance can therefore be consistent with several different changes in bank balance sheets.

## The receiving bank changes too

Banking terminology distinguishes [retail deposits](/en/glossary/depot-de-detail/), held by individuals, from [wholesale deposits](/en/glossary/depot-de-gros/) placed by companies and institutions. Their characteristics are part of [assessing a bank’s soundness](/en/guides/read-bank-health/).

Bank A knows the customer it has lost. Bank B receives an issuer’s funds, which may move as tokens are issued or redeemed and reserves are invested. The amount can be identical while the cash-management behaviour changes. A large account concentrates decisions previously spread across many holders. For the receiving bank, managing liquidity becomes critical. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b04">B04</a></sup>

Lee and Tou examine this channel in a New York Fed paper published in February 2026. They link blockchain transactions to the interbank payments of an anonymous issuer. After March 2023, three new partner banks held a larger share of their assets in reserves and a smaller share in loans relative to control banks. The sample is narrow, and the authors cannot estimate the effect on aggregate credit. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-m01">M01</a></sup>



A bank creates a deposit when it makes a loan. It must then settle its customer’s payments and manage the funding, liquidity and capital of its balance sheet. Stable deposits help it meet those demands. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r06">R06</a></sup>

Maturity transformation matters too. A mortgage or business loan commits a bank’s funds over a long period. A highly mobile deposit can require a larger liquidity cushion. If the bank devotes more of its balance sheet to immediately available assets, loans can account for a smaller share even as the balance sheet grows. A lower loan ratio and a decline in the total dollar amount of loans are separate findings.

An unchanged aggregate deposit total would thus provide incomplete reassurance about financing for the economy. Extrapolating the experience of three banks into a national forecast would also stretch the evidence. The more specific finding is useful: the characteristics of incoming funding can influence the assets a bank chooses to hold.

A separate ECB paper, published in March 2026, studies deposits and lending using bank data and a measure of attention to stablecoins derived from Google searches. It provides evidence consistent with this funding risk, subject to its identification assumptions. Online attention remains a proxy for actual adoption. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b05">B05</a></sup>

On October 29, 2026, BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos highlighted a redemption risk: an issuer facing customer withdrawals could draw down bank deposits or sell reserve assets under pressure. Reserve composition and banking links therefore matter when holders want their dollars back as well. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r07">R07</a></sup>

## Yield, payments and access to dollars

Our analysis of [who receives stablecoin reserve income](/en/analysis/stablecoins-yield-reserves-occ-circle-coinbase/) follows the contracts linking issuers, platforms and token holders.

A deposit rate matters most when the user can actually choose that account. For someone abroad, access to transferable dollars, the platforms used by trading partners or a particular payment channel may outweigh a few additional basis points at a US bank. A research note published by the Fed emphasises that both the geography of demand and the assets converted into stablecoins affect the outcome for US deposits. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b04">B04</a></sup>

The income earned on reserves also needs to be distinguished from the return received by a token holder. A platform may pay rewards funded by reserve income or by its own market activities. BIS Bulletin 125 describes both arrangements. Their funding and risks differ. A bank’s rate therefore competes with a package of services, holding conditions and possible rewards. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-b03">B03</a></sup>

Regulation shapes that contest. The final rule issued by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), published on **23 October 2026**, is scheduled to take effect on **18 January 2027**. It retains the prohibition on issuer-paid yield within the statute’s scope and drops the proposed presumption covering certain indirect payments, while preserving anti-evasion authority. Arrangements involving intermediaries remain subject to assessment on their facts. Any comparison of returns needs to account for both the applicable rules and their implementation dates. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-m06">M06</a></sup> <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-m09">M09</a></sup>

A model published by White House economists on April 8, 2026 examines a ban on stablecoin yield. Its baseline estimates a small increase in bank lending. That result depends partly on ample Federal Reserve reserves and issuers’ reserve allocation. The exercise connects a regulatory choice with credit in a calibrated framework, using substitution behaviour drawn from money market funds. Its intervention and assumptions need to accompany the result before it is extrapolated to stablecoin growth. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-r08">R08</a></sup>

## Bargaining power shifts

Three observations would be especially revealing as this market develops. The first is the rate actually received by customers choosing between deposits and stablecoins, after fees and conditions. The second is the identity of the banks receiving the funds and how much of their funding is concentrated among a few depositors. The third is what those banks hold against it: settlement reserves, liquid securities or loans to the economy.

Growth at Circle or Tether alone leaves these questions open. More tokens outstanding could reflect a US bank customer switching products, an overseas business seeking dollar liquidity or additional payment needs. Each case has a different chain of transmission.

Banks have a plausible lever for defending part of their deposit base: pay more for it. The commercial effectiveness and cost need to be assessed institution by institution. Stablecoins introduce another contest over reserve investment and the services that keep funds moving. The outcome could be bank funding that is more expensive, more concentrated or associated with a greater preference for liquidity, with substantial differences between banks.

The useful starting point is to follow the dollar to its next holder and to the asset it finances. Those two destinations help determine how the role of banks changes.

## Methods and illustrations

<div id="methode-bilans"></div>

The 100 dollar paths represent isolated transactions, all else equal, at par and without fees, within US banks. A secondary-market purchase differs from a new token issue. Deposit changes are kept separate from settlement reserves and the issuer’s assets. Each participant’s accounts balance; subsequent Treasury spending is an additional stage.

<div id="methode-financement"></div>

The cost model is fictional and annualized on a constant balance. An increase of 10 basis points on 1 billion dollars costs 1 000 000 000 × 10 / 10 000 = 1 000 000 dollars a year. Replacing 100 million at an additional 100 basis points costs 100 000 000 × 100 / 10 000 = 1 000 000 dollars a year. The curve applies that second formula to hypothetical spreads. Successful retention, available replacement funding and unchanged other costs are assumptions whose feasibility requires investigation.

The cover illustration is conceptual artwork generated with an AI tool. The passive diagrams explain mechanisms or scenarios; this investigation conducted no bank-withdrawal or conversion experiment.
