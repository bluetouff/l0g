An article can help an AI answer a question without bringing a reader to the website that published it. Cloudflare, a web infrastructure provider, wants a payment to follow the content into that other product. Its **Pay Per Use** beta, announced on 30 September 2026, sets up a commercial relationship: an AI company defines a use it will pay for and offers a price; a publisher accepts; reported uses become the basis for billing. [1](#source-1)

That gives publishers another way to sell their work. It also moves a crucial question downstream: **who can check how often the content was actually used?** In the system Cloudflare describes, the buyer supplies the events. Cloudflare checks whether they map to an enrolled publisher. Validating those records is different from establishing that every eligible use has been reported. The public announcement describes the former without setting out a universal, independent audit of the latter. [1](#source-1)

As of 2 November 2026, the documents reviewed for this investigation establish how the products are intended to work and the terms publicly described. They do not provide a verified aggregate of Pay Per Use payouts, median publisher earnings or a universal fee schedule. Understanding the conditions for a viable market comes before assigning it a financial return. [1](#source-1), [2](#source-2)

## When the answer replaces the visit

The traditional search bargain involved traffic moving in both directions. A search engine crawled a publisher’s pages and sent readers back. Answers delivered inside the search interface can change that exchange. The publisher’s economics then depend on the visits it retains, their value and any revenue it can earn somewhere else.

Pew Research Center provides a useful historical reference. Its study covered browsing by 900 US adults in March 2025. A click on a traditional search result followed 8% of search visits with an AI summary, compared with 15% without one. Links inside the summary received a click in 1% of visits featuring a summary. That last measure concerns a separate group of links. [10](#source-10)

<CloudflareContentFigure id="01" lang="en" />

The results illustrate the vulnerability of referral traffic. They do not establish a causal revenue loss. Queries with summaries can differ from queries without them, and the search results were collected on 7–17 April, after the browsing period. Applying the difference directly to a particular publisher’s revenue would create an unsupported estimate. [10](#source-10)

Charging for downstream use addresses a real economic possibility: a source may improve an AI product even when it receives no visit. How much of that value the buyer will share, and which event makes a payment due, are still commercial questions.

## Three products with different billing events

Cloudflare’s products operate at different points in this chain. **Pay Per Crawl** charges for successful access to content. Launched in private beta on 1 July 2025, it is still described as a closed beta in the documentation reviewed. An identified crawler can receive an [HTTP 402](/en/glossary/http-402/) response indicating that payment is required; a successful paid retrieval becomes billable. [3](#source-3), [4](#source-4), [18](#source-18), [20](#source-20)

A publisher can set a default price and use dynamic pricing by URL or content. The documented minimum is **$0.001 per crawl**. That floor belongs to Pay Per Crawl. It says nothing about the average price buyers accept or the price of Pay Per Use events. Security blocking rules continue to take precedence over charging rules. [4](#source-4), [5](#source-5), [6](#source-6)

The 402 status is a signal rather than a complete payment system. The HTTP specification reserves it for future use; the surrounding protocol and commercial agreement supply the terms and settlement mechanism. For Pay Per Crawl, Cloudflare acts as merchant of record and describes monthly payouts through a dedicated Stripe Connect account, subject to applicable settlement conditions. [4](#source-4), [7](#source-7), [8](#source-8)

**Pay Per Use** prices an event further downstream: a [billable use](/en/glossary/usage-facturable/) covered by a buyer’s program. **Monetization Gateway**, also announced in beta on 30 September 2026, targets interfaces through which software exchanges requests and responses (APIs), tools and data for which the request itself is the consumption. Its launch describes x402 payments in USDC on Base, with Coinbase as facilitator. The initial closed beta is for eligible US-based participants. Those arrangements should not be assumed to apply to Pay Per Use or to a publisher based in France. [1](#source-1), [16](#source-16)

<CloudflareContentFigure id="02" lang="en" />

The right charging point depends on the product. An API request can deliver the service immediately. A web page might be fetched, stored in an index, left unused or drawn on repeatedly. Requests and uses can therefore move in quite different directions.

## One copy, a hundred uses

Consider a deliberately normalized example. One service retrieves a page a hundred times but uses it only once in a covered experience. Another retrieves it once and uses it a hundred times. At a fictional price of one monetary unit per event, access-based and use-based billing produce opposite invoices. [l0g calculation]

<CloudflareContentFigure id="03" lang="en" />

These are invented counts, not observed traffic. They show why server requests and uses inside an answer measure different things. Refresh frequency, caching policy and the product’s design all affect the relationship between them.

Charging for every crawl makes the buyer pay for material it evaluates and rejects. Charging for use lets the buyer wait until content contributes to its product. Cloudflare argues that this can attract more buyers. Publishers, in turn, become exposed to the contractual definition of use and the way it is measured. [1](#source-1)

A source appearing in a results list, a passage reproduced in an answer and information influencing a recommendation are distinct events. A high price attached to a rare event may yield less than a small price attached to a broad one. Comparing offers requires understanding the denominator as well as the headline rate.

## The buyer keeps the underlying records

Under the Pay Per Use beta, an AI company submits events containing the time of use, the source URL and an identifier. Cloudflare aggregates those reports, bills the buyer and schedules monthly publisher payments. Cloudflare says the program terms require complete reporting. [1](#source-1)

Verifying a crawler’s identity helps a publisher decide who can enter. A URL connects a reported use with an enrolled site. Neither, by itself, describes everything that happens later inside the buyer’s systems. Content can move through an index, a cache (a stored copy for reuse) and an answer generator. Establishing the completeness of reported uses would require additional verification; the reviewed public documents leave that question open. [1](#source-1), [18](#source-18) ; analysis

On 16 October 2026, Cloudflare described internal monitoring of Verified bots’ behavior, including compliance with `robots.txt` preferences and responses to HTTP 429 rate-limit signals. Public trust scores and customer-selected thresholds are presented as future steps. These observations of access behavior leave a separate question: the completeness of Pay Per Use reporting inside the buyer’s systems. [21](#source-21)

<CloudflareContentFigure id="04" lang="en" />

A rigorous comparison of programs would examine their billing definitions, treatment of repeated uses, log retention and dispute procedures. Publishers could seek sample-based checks or independent assurance. These are proposed evaluation criteria, not features confirmed as universally available in the beta.

Verification also has privacy implications. Checking a source’s contribution does not automatically require handing a user’s entire conversation to the publisher. Cloudflare says it is working on additional context, such as topics or keywords, without exchanging personal data. The actual protections need to be assessed against the terms of the particular program. [1](#source-1)

Estimated earnings, invoiced amounts and cash received should remain separately visible. Validated events are one stage of the process; adjustments, potential disputes, fees and payment timing can change the eventual cash contribution. An effective dashboard should support that reconciliation rather than make an event total look like a bank balance.

## A crawler can walk away

Stack Overflow offers a particularly useful counterexample. In an interview published on 19 February 2026, engineer Josh Zhang said some crawlers stopped making requests after the site began returning 402 responses instead of the 403 refusals they had previously received. [9](#source-9)

This is a commercial partner’s account, recorded in a conversation with Cloudflare. It is neither a representative market study nor a financial assessment of the program. But it highlights a basic buyer option: declining to purchase access. A control can successfully reduce unwanted consumption without generating a sale. [9](#source-9) ; analysis

In July 2026, Cloudflare described experiments with Ceramic.ai and You.com involving content appearing in search results and access to premium sources. These examples document commercial approaches being tested. They do not establish the number of paying buyers in the September beta, settled volumes or individual publisher earnings. [2](#source-2), [11](#source-11)

Fresh, specific and hard-to-replace information should have greater bargaining value. This is an economic hypothesis: a buyer weighs the price against the cost of finding another source, the risk of a weaker answer or the option of abandoning the request. Original local reporting and a generic rewrite of a press release present different substitution problems. Public evidence reviewed here does not quantify that difference.

## From a quoted price to newsroom revenue

A small model makes the economics inspectable without pretending to forecast them. Imagine **one hypothetical month** with one million potential downstream uses. Just 20% ultimately fall within participating programs and become covered, billable, reported and validated events. At an assumed **$0.002 per event**, gross revenue is $400.

Assume aggregate deductions of 10% and $100 in additional monthly costs. The resulting contribution is **$260**. The deduction rate is an invented model input, not a published Cloudflare commission. The use price is also hypothetical and separate from the Pay Per Crawl minimum. [l0g calculation]

<CloudflareContentFigure id="05" lang="en" />

Now define a comparison explicitly: 50,000 fewer human visits, each contributing an average of $10 net per thousand visits, would put $500 at stake. Neither the visits nor their value are derived from Pew or from l0g’s traffic. They provide a comparison in the same currency and over the same fictional month.

To produce $500 after the model’s deductions and additional costs, the use price would need to be about **$0.00333 per billable event**, with coverage unchanged. At the initial $0.002 price, one third of potential uses would need to become billable. The accompanying data and calculator expose each assumption. [l0g calculation]

The 20% share combines several filters. Other uses might belong to buyers outside the program or fall outside the contractual definition. It is not an estimate of reporting accuracy or fraud. Higher prices may also reduce demand. The calculator holds volume constant to isolate a sensitivity; it does not estimate buyers’ response.

Scale matters at the publisher level. Shared infrastructure can make small payments viable when separately negotiated contracts would consume them in administrative costs. Yet an attractive rate on a few thousand events cannot fund expensive reporting. Covered volume, monitoring costs and the durability of the commercial relationship determine the outcome.

## Separating generative answers from conventional search

Bargaining power also depends on alternatives to joining a paid program. Here the picture changed in 2026. Google says that on **31 August** it completed a worldwide rollout of a Search Console control allowing publishers to exclude their content and links from AI Overviews, AI Mode and relevant generative features in Discover. Its documentation says the choice is not a ranking or inclusion signal for other parts of Search. [13](#source-13), [19](#source-19)

Opting out also removes impressions and visits from those AI features. Training is controlled separately. The setting broadens publishers’ choices without guaranteeing stable traffic or replacement revenue. Describing the choice as accepting all AI uses or disappearing from conventional search would now be outdated. [13](#source-13)

Cloudflare announced separate controls for search, training and agents on 15 September, alongside an *Accountable* designation based on requirements already met or commitments with specified timelines. The deployed functionality matters as much as the label. On 30 October, Google also announced plans to support a shared AI-training signal emerging from AIPREF standards work, targeting 2027. That remains a prospective commitment. [12](#source-12), [14](#source-14)

Preferences, access security and billing operate at separate layers. The `robots.txt` standard explicitly states that it is not a substitute for valid security controls. A preference must be read by its recipient; access enforcement acts on incoming requests; licensing defines authorized and payable uses. Keeping those layers separate avoids attributing to a technical setting the power to resolve every copyright question. [15](#source-15) ; analysis

## Cloudflare’s new position between publishers and AI products

The strongest case for the system is coordination. A small publisher cannot easily negotiate and maintain an integration with every prospective buyer. Shared infrastructure brings identity, enrollment, reporting and settlement together. Lower coordination costs could make previously uneconomic transactions possible. That is a credible proposition to test. [1](#source-1) ; analysis

It also gives Cloudflare another potential role between the producer and distributor of information. Buyer admission, offer presentation, billing definitions and the information returned to publishers all shape negotiation. A useful intermediary can become a dependency. This is an analysis of incentives, not a legal finding of dominance or abuse.

Other companies are pursuing related models. On 3 February 2026 Microsoft introduced its **Publisher Content Marketplace**, then a pilot involving content licensing, publisher-defined usage terms and reporting, with experiments in Copilot. Public descriptions allow a comparison of the architectures. They do not establish which offers a publisher the best return without concrete prices, fees and volumes. [17](#source-17)

In the September Pay Per Use beta description, buyers propose the usage definition and price. Cloudflare describes counteroffers and finer pricing by content and use as future ambitions. The distinction matters: being able to decline an offer and being able to change its terms provide different kinds of leverage. [1](#source-1)

## The evidence that would establish a market

A useful assessment would follow cohorts of publishers for several months: active paying buyers, usage definitions, gross receipts, deductions, cash received and continued participation. The distribution of payouts matters too. A large aggregate can coexist with negligible earnings for most publishers.

The next task is to connect the use count to its controls: identifiers, deduplication rules, reporting delays, corrections and dispute rights. That would allow an assessment of the records’ reliability. None of the documents reviewed here establishes a representative financial return for the Pay Per Use market. Missing public data leave an open question; they do not demonstrate that no payments exist. [1](#source-1), [2](#source-2), [11](#source-11)

Cloudflare has documented infrastructure and a way to organize an exchange. Its value depends on buyers’ willingness to pay, the scope of paid uses and confidence in reporting. For a publisher, the final test is tangible: how much verifiable net contribution does the relationship provide toward producing the next piece of original information?

## Method and limitations

This document-based investigation is current to **2 November 2026**. Product and control descriptions come from their suppliers and are attributed accordingly. Stack Overflow provides a partner’s account. Pew’s observational evidence concerns 2025. No interviews, commercial account access or production tests were undertaken.

The scenarios use nominal US dollars over one fictional month. Every input is hypothetical. No traffic statistic has been converted into a revenue estimate for l0g or any other publisher. The model excludes demand elasticity, tax and settlement timing. The evidence file distinguishes publication, update and consultation dates for evolving documentation; full original URLs appear below.

**Terms.** A [*crawl*](/en/glossary/crawl/) is an automated retrieval. A [*paid use*](/en/glossary/usage-facturable/) is an event defined by a commercial program. An *API* lets software exchange requests and responses. The model’s *net contribution* deducts assumed aggregate charges and additional costs before tax; it is not a company’s reported accounting profit.
