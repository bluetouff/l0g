In the first quarter of 2026, WhiteFiber sold a batch of servers fitted with Nvidia H200 [GPUs](/en/glossary/gpu/). The disposal note put their carrying amount at **$24.3 million** on the sale date. WhiteFiber fetched **$26.1 million** and had collected the full sum by the time it filed on May 14. Its November filing repeats the transaction. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

The previous year, WhiteFiber had changed its estimate. From January 1, 2025, it expected to use its cloud service equipment for **five years instead of three**. Slower [depreciation](/en/glossary/amortissement-comptable/) cut 2025 expense by **$10 million** and narrowed the net loss by **$7.9 million**. Spreading the charge over more years leaves more of the purchase price on the balance sheet. Earnings in use or the price at sale give an indication of whether the estimate holds up. [S20](#source-S20)

The disposal yielded a **$1.8 million** gain, about **7.4%** of carrying value on the rounded figures. Working out the return from purchase would require the original cost of this particular lot, the revenue it earned in service and its operating costs. The gain measures the difference on the day of the sale. [S01](#source-S01) [S02](#source-S02)

## Finding another job for the same chips

WhiteFiber kept another part of its fleet and sold time on it. In May 2026, Hyperbolic Labs signed up for two years of service using **512 H200s** that WhiteFiber already owned, according to its quarterly filings. The company reports recognising revenue from the contract in the second quarter. For those machines, the evidence of value is a customer agreeing to pay for their work. [S02](#source-S02) [S23](#source-S23)

CoreWeave describes a different job for an older chip. A scientific research company rents A100s to generate and refine datasets and fine-tune models. In September, the cloud operator disclosed a three-year renewal running **into 2029**. It said the terms were in line with a typical one-year deal and kept the rate private. Nvidia launched the A100 in **2020**. The installation dates of these particular machines remain unknown. If the contract runs its course, it will show how long the design can still attract paying customers. [S03](#source-S03) [S22](#source-S22)

The job can change the case for replacing a chip. In a trial by the Hasso Plattner Institute, a system equipped with a B200 delivered roughly **82% more throughput** in a matrix multiplication test than a system with an H100. Its lead fell to **19% on the combined score** for three TPCx-AI use cases. The second test also reflects work on the host processor. The machines hosting the chips differed. An operator weighing purchase price and power bills first needs to know which jobs the machines will run. [S21](#source-S21)

At the Oak Ridge National Laboratory, each compute node of the Summit supercomputer held six V100s. Summit entered full production on January 1, **2019**, and received an extra year of service after the five originally planned. Its extension awarded time to **108 projects** and delivered **21.4 million node-hours**, each representing one compute node in use for one hour. Summit ran its last jobs on **November 15, 2024**. That is observed service. A public research lab and a commercial cloud operator attach different economics to an hour of computing time. [S05](#source-S05) [S06](#source-S06)

## One year on Amazon’s books

Amazon’s filings offer a rare chance to see the earnings effect in both directions. On January 1, 2024, the company extended its estimate for servers from five years to six. For the full year the change took $3.2 billion out of depreciation expense and added $2.5 billion to net income. The earnings lift came from spreading the cost of servers over more years. [S07](#source-S07)

A year later Amazon shortened the estimate from six years to five for **a portion** of its servers and networking equipment. It cited faster technological change, particularly in AI and machine learning. On the 2025 accounts, that meant $1.4 billion of extra depreciation and $1 billion less net income. A separate decision to retire certain equipment early had already produced roughly $920 million in charges in the fourth quarter of 2024, including accelerated depreciation. The 2025 revision covered a narrower set of equipment. Each annual effect belongs to its own pool of assets and accounting period. [S07](#source-S07) [S08](#source-S08)

<GpuSecondLifeFigure lang="en" kind="amazon" />

Dividing each disclosed after-tax boost by reported net income for the same fiscal year gives **4.1%** for Microsoft in the year to June 2023, **4.1%** for Alphabet in calendar 2023 and **4.3%** for Meta in 2025. The revised schedules cover categories of servers or network equipment that extend well beyond AI chips. [S09](#source-S09) [S10](#source-S10) [S11](#source-S11) [S12](#source-S12)

Meta shows how a growing fleet changes the totals. Extending useful lives to five and a half years reduced 2025 depreciation by **$2.92 billion** for the affected equipment already in service at the end of 2024. Third-quarter server and network depreciation still rose to **$5.34 billion** in 2026 from **$3.60 billion** a year earlier. A new estimate changes the schedule for existing machines. The quarterly results also include equipment that entered service in the meantime. [S11](#source-S11) [S12](#source-S12) [S13](#source-S13)

## One sale, two answers on the books

Suppose an operator pays $120 million for a fleet when it enters service. It expects to use the equipment for six years and assumes zero residual value at the end. Straight-line depreciation allocates an equal $20 million to each year. After two full years, $40 million has gone through the income statement and $80 million remains on the balance sheet. Now suppose management cuts the **total** useful life to four years. The first two years stand. The remaining $80 million has to be written off over two years, at $40 million a year. That is what it means to change an estimate prospectively. [S14](#source-S14)

At the end of year four, a buyer offers $12 million, unexpected under either schedule. Under the original six-year estimate, the fleet still carries a value of $40 million. The sale produces a $28 million loss. Under the revised four-year schedule, the fleet is fully depreciated. The same $12 million wire transfer produces a $12 million gain. **Over the holding period, the purchase price less sale proceeds comes to $108 million either way.** Accumulated depreciation and the gain or loss on disposal reconcile to that amount before operating costs, financing and taxes. The path of reported earnings is different. [S14](#source-S14)

Sometimes an event also casts doubt on whether equipment can earn back the amount on the balance sheet. For long-lived assets held for use, US accounting rules call for a recoverability test when such indicators arise. The company compares the carrying amount of the relevant asset group with the undiscounted cash flows it expects the group to generate. If it fails that test, the loss is measured against fair value. The SEC staff illustrates the distinction with an older generation of computers, mainframes due for replacement. As the replacement plans take shape, management needs to reconsider the remaining useful life even while the asset group still passes the recoverability test. [S14](#source-S14) [S20](#source-S20)

## The next test comes at renewal

In April 2026, IMF staff compared property, plant and equipment with depreciation at ten large cloud operators. Their proxy suggested a useful life of roughly **seven years across those assets**. The authors discussed the possibility that advanced chips could become obsolete in **two years**. They separately modelled a **three-year capital life**, which reduced aggregate operating margin by more than **nine percentage points** in their simulation. The IMF subsequently highlighted the risk of debt outliving the equipment it finances. The ECB discussed obsolescence risk in its November review. [S15](#source-S15) [S16](#source-S16) [S24](#source-S24)

In June 2025, AWS cut the on-demand price for its H100-equipped P5 instances by **44%**. In the third quarter of 2026, CoreWeave said it obtained **30% to 50%** higher prices on its Ampere and Hopper generations than a quarter earlier. One is a posted price change. The other comes from a different operator fifteen months later. To decide whether to keep a machine, the operator needs the actual rate it collects, the hours it sells and the cost of keeping it running. At a site with no spare power, a newer machine might earn more in the same slot. [S04](#source-S04) [S17](#source-S17)

The most telling dates are in CoreWeave’s November 10 earnings call. The company said **more than 130,000 GPUs would come off contract in 2027, followed by about 170,000 in 2028**. It depreciates technology equipment over six years. Renewal rates, new contract prices and the time machines spend waiting for another customer would give readers a way to test that schedule against revenue. A quick return to paying work at prices that cover operating costs would support a longer economic life. Racks standing idle or repeated sales below book value would weigh against it. WhiteFiber’s sale gives us one firm observation. We know the carrying value of that batch of H200 equipment and can compare it with the cash that came in.
