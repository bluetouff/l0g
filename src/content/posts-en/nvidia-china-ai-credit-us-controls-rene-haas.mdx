**In Songyuan, northeastern China, an official investment pitch promises a computing center combining Nvidia chips with Chinese processors. Its developer proposes seeking bank loans for roughly 80% of the cost. Between that pitch and the revenue needed to repay the loans lies a step on which everything else depends: obtaining the equipment and being able to operate it.** [S01](#source-S01)

Nvidia’s crisis gives that question new weight. US export controls shape access to GPUs, the processors powering large AI systems. Jensen Huang’s succession raises a separate question about the commitments the next leader will maintain. On 13 November, the *Financial Times* reported that **Rene Haas, chief executive of Arm and SoftBank Group International, had been approached**. [S09](#source-S09) [S10](#source-S10)

This ninth instalment follows our investigation into [servers declared as collateral in Shanghai](/en/analysis/nvidia-shanghai-server-collateral-lutnick-bessent/). It examines a different set of Chinese projects identified by Reuters, then considers the authorizations and governance decisions that may shape their financing.

## Songyuan seeks financing before the revenue arrives

A project description published by the Jilin government portal on **27 April 2026** sets out planned investment of **2.2589309 billion yuan**, or approximately **2.26 billion**. Songyuan Guoxin’s proposed center combines H100s with Chinese processors and offers computing and rack-rental services. [S01](#source-S01)

Its funding section envisages approximately **80% bank borrowing** and **20% company or shareholder capital**. The developer intends to seek long-term loans on favorable terms from commercial or policy banks. The page still invites partners to negotiate. It presents a proposed financing structure, with lending agreements and disbursements yet to be identified. [S01](#source-S01)

<NvidiaChinaCredit2026 lang="en" figure="funding" />

Turning that proposal into credit requires examining the shareholders’ actual contribution, equipment orders, authorizations and customers expected to pay for the computing service. Cash remaining after operating expenses must cover debt payments. The [debt-service coverage ratio, or DSCR](/en/glossary/dscr/), addresses that relationship.

An equipment delay could push revenue further out while some construction or financing expenses continue. Replacing the specified processors would also require reviewing costs, software compatibility and the performance promised to customers. The risk comes from a mismatch between repayment dates and incoming revenue.

## Chinese project budgets cover different industrial stages

Reuters examined **more than 150 documents** and identified **over 23 billion yuan** in projects and equipment agreements involving Nvidia technology. Its 13 November investigation includes planned investment, contracts and installations described as completed. Buildings, electrical systems and other infrastructure are part of that scope. The figure indicates the scale of the identified projects across different stages of development. [S02](#source-S02)

Gansu provides an example beyond a search for partners. A tender dated **22 June 2026** envisages company funds and bank borrowing for a computing center specifying equipment described as “H100(200).” The final award notice of **20 July** names a construction and engineering consortium with a contract value of **1.1564 billion yuan**. An award is an identifiable commercial step. Server acceptance and financing terms require their own records. [S03](#source-S03)

Reuters expressly separates its identified projects from Megaspeed and the allegations involving Huang. Their relevance here is economic: infrastructure, orders and repayment plans are being designed around access to controlled processors. Nvidia and China’s commerce ministry had not immediately responded to the agency at publication. [S02](#source-S02)

## A computing contract can change operators before it expires

A regulatory response from Damingcheng illustrates a further stage. In a statement published on **27 June 2026**, the issuer describes an H800 cluster built using company funds and bank loans. Its associated computing-services contract originally amounted to **759.296 million yuan over 85 months**. That figure described a service agreement extending over time, with a different scope from the cost of a data center. [S04](#source-S04)

As part of an equity sale, the old agreements ended on **31 January 2026**. A three-party amendment transferred Mingcheng Digital’s rights and obligations toward Shanghai Unicom to Shida Group, effective **1 February**. It describes the transaction as a move toward a lighter operating model. This commercial restructuring predates the current crisis by several months. [S04](#source-S04)

A lender must keep following the service contract after equipment delivery. When operations change hands, it needs to know who provides the service, who collects customer payments and which company remains liable for the debt. Servers can stay where they are while the legal entity earning the revenue changes.

## US authorizations enter the financing timetable

The restrictions predate the present crisis. In a filing published on **31 August 2022**, Nvidia reported that it had been notified on **26 August** of an immediately effective licensing requirement for future A100 and H100 exports to China, including Hong Kong, and Russia. Systems incorporating those processors were covered as well. [S05](#source-S05)

The regime subsequently changed. A rule published and effective on **15 January 2026** provides case-by-case review for certain exports **from the United States** to China and Macau, including qualifying H200 products. It requires checks involving customers, security, US market availability and independent testing in the United States. Eligibility for review precedes an individual licensing decision. Other covered transactions, including certain reexports and in-country transfers, retain a presumption of denial. [S06](#source-S06)

<NvidiaChinaCredit2026 lang="en" figure="licensing" />

Chinese decisions matter too. In its 10-Q for the quarter ended **26 July 2026**, Nvidia reports H200 licenses obtained from February and a portion of equipment actually shipped. The company also describes Chinese restrictions that constrained purchases. The supplier’s account identifies several steps between US permission and a completed sale. [S08](#source-S08)

## Trump holds commercial leverage whose records are being sought

A separate customs decision sits alongside export licensing. Trump’s **14 January 2026** proclamation imposes a **25% duty on certain covered US chip imports**, effective the following day. It provides exemptions according to use, including US data centers, research, repairs and several industrial or public purposes. The duty therefore depends on the scope and conditions of the import. [S07](#source-S07)

The proclamation also confirms that Commerce transmitted its **Section 232** national-security report to the president on **22 December 2025**. The public text gives that date and the presidential conclusions adopted. The complete report remains an important record for reconstructing the decisions. [S07](#source-S07)

On **12 November**, Ron Wyden and Richard Neal, the ranking Democrats on the relevant Senate and House committees, requested preservation of records concerning that process, Nvidia licenses and communications. They challenge the arrangement and raise a constitutional objection to a levy they characterize as export-related. Their letter initiates an inquiry and requests records preservation. [S13](#source-S13)

**Our assessment: access to chips gives Washington influence that can reach project financing.** Greater uncertainty over authorizations can change schedules and the protections lenders seek. Trump could try to turn that dependence into a political or industrial advantage, a scenario explored in our [examination of state leverage](/en/analysis/nvidia-trump-tim-cook-lutnick-state-leverage/).

## The Haas report brings SoftBank’s interests into the succession

The *Financial Times* reported on **13 November** that Haas had been sounded out during the search for Huang’s successor. The reported approach moves Haas beyond the names floated by analysts. [S09](#source-S09)

His background explains the interest. Arm says Haas has been its chief executive and a director since February 2022, after a career that included seven years at Nvidia. He also runs SoftBank Group International and sits on SoftBank Group’s board. SoftBank’s April 2026 announcement describes the expanded role as coordinating its semiconductor and AI holdings. [S10](#source-S10)

Those roles intersect with significant existing financial relationships. SoftBank is an investor in SB Energy. For the SB Energy/OpenAI project in Ohio, Nvidia agreed guarantees covering defined lease and power payments, subject to a combined **$105 billion** cap. They generally become effective as each lease begins. This arrangement has its own contractual scope, explored in our [seventh instalment on AI guarantees](/en/analysis/nvidia-ai-financing-guarantees-asian-investigations/). [S08](#source-S08) [S12](#source-S12)

If Haas joined Nvidia, the board would need to clarify his other positions and how relationships with SoftBank-linked companies would be handled. The practical issue would be freedom to decide on industrial agreements and financial commitments.

Nvidia’s official succession process remains open. The board accepted Huang’s resignation on **11 November**, then announced the following day that Colette Kress would serve as interim chief executive while retaining finance responsibilities, with an internal and external search under chairman Harvey C. Jones. [S11](#source-S11)

## Nvidia’s next leader inherits decisions that need financing

An internal candidate could maintain commercial relationships while reviewing the most sensitive commitments. An outsider would need to establish a mandate with the board and partners. The Haas report adds a specific question: how to arrange the responsibilities of an executive already at the center of SoftBank’s industrial interests.

The Chinese projects illustrate a constraint any successor will encounter. An installation financed against future revenue needs equipment, commissioning and creditworthy customers. Governments can influence parts of that chain. Nvidia’s next leader will decide how much risk the company is willing to support, while lenders and investors look for enforceable contracts behind promises of computing power.
