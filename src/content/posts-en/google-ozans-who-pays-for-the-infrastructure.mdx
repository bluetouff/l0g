Google’s proposed campus at Ozans depends on power lines, roads and shared networks. Following the money reveals the commitments behind the project and their implications for the region.

Before Google can run servers at its proposed Ozans campus, the site needs a connection to France’s electricity grid. Google would fund 70% of the public substations and the first power links supplying its Ozans campus. France’s electricity transmission operator, RTE, would cover the remaining 30%. Google would pay the full cost of the second links, designed to provide a [redundant supply](/en/glossary/redondance/). The project summary shows the same allocation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-20" title="S20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

Those percentages provide a useful starting point for a wider question. When a global company reserves substantial grid capacity, who advances the money, who benefits from the infrastructure, and who carries the risk if development slows? Answering it means following several financial channels. An upfront connection charge, a regulated network tariff and the proceeds from selling land each do a different job.

The proposed campus occupies roughly 200 hectares at Étrechet, east of Châteauroux in the Indre department. Google envisages six to eight buildings developed over ten to twenty years, with the first targeted for around 2030. Public consultation opened on 2 November 2026 and is scheduled to run until 10 January 2027. As of 6 November, the project remains at a preparatory stage: the dossier places applications for authorizations after consultation. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-03" title="S03" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-04" title="S04" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

The investment estimate conveys the scale. The dossier puts the completed site at about US$10 billion, presented as approximately €8.6 billion excluding VAT. That figure excludes IT equipment and software, while including Google’s share of the electricity connection. Google describes the campus as financed from Alphabet’s own funds, without public subsidies, while exploring technical and financial partnerships with third parties at a European scale. The estimate covers a future development built in stages. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.114" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>


## The buildings depend on the power connections

A data center combines servers with electrical equipment and systems that remove the heat they produce. At Ozans, the construction schedule depends on the power the grid can make available. Connection power, measured in megawatts, defines the electrical capacity made available to the site. Annual electricity use will depend on the actual load and how long it runs.

The first connection, at 225 kilovolts, would supply 120 MW to the first building. It involves a public substation within the Ozans development zone and two underground links of roughly 300 meters each. Grid availability is envisaged ahead of the building’s opening, targeted for around 2030. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.43–44 and 113" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The later buildings require a larger connection. A second public substation would connect to the 400 kV network, with two overhead lines linking it to the campus. This branch would initially provide an additional 300 MW, with connection envisaged for early 2031. It could subsequently rise to 1,200 MW following reinforcement of the Éguzon–Marmagne transmission route. RTE gives 2032 as the earliest commissioning date for that reinforcement. Authorizations, engineering studies and the pace of industrial development all qualify these schedules. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.49, 57–59 and 113" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-09" title="S09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>


<GoogleOzansFigure lang="en" kind="capacity" />


The move from 300 MW to 1,200 MW concerns the same 400 kV branch. The original 120 MW connection remains alongside it. The dossier therefore gives total maximum contracted capacity of 1,320 MW. Estimating annual electricity consumption would require a separate set of assumptions: which buildings open, how quickly they fill, and their operating load. The dossier explicitly states that the maximum power would not be drawn continuously. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.74" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

That distinction matters to the public debate. Substations and cables have to be sized before the servers reach their operating load. Grid spending anticipates future use. The quality of that forecast, together with the commitments binding the applicant, determines part of the economic risk.

## The 70/30 split depends on the assets

France’s connection rules include a mechanism known as [réfaction tarifaire](/en/glossary/refaction-tarifaire/): part of eligible connection costs is covered through network tariffs, with the applicant paying the balance. For electricity consumers connected to the high-voltage HTB network, the ministerial order of 30 November 2017 sets that share at 30%. The Energy Code provides for tariffs to cover part of connection expenditure and all grid reinforcement costs. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-05" title="S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-06" title="S06" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-14" title="S14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-16" title="S16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

The Ozans dossier applies this framework to both connections. Google would meet 70% of the cost of the public substations and first links, with RTE carrying 30%. The second links and their connections inside the substations would be funded entirely by Google. These provide redundancy: a second connection helps secure the electricity supply. Google would also fund its own private 400 kV substation in full. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.114–115" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-20" title="S20, p.2" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>


<GoogleOzansFigure lang="en" kind="financing" />


The published cost estimates are approximately €25 million for the 225 kV connection and €100 million for the 400 kV connection. Both are provisional. The dossier does not break each estimate down between assets subject to the 70/30 split and assets paid for entirely by Google. That missing allocation prevents a reliable calculation of RTE’s total contribution in euros. Applying 30% to the whole amount would extend tariff support to items that the funding diagram assigns entirely to the applicant. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.114–115" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

There is also an overlap with the €8.6 billion campus estimate, which already includes Google’s share of the connection. Adding the full cost of both connections to that figure would count some spending twice. The published amounts describe overlapping scopes. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.114" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Following the money beyond RTE leads to the *TURPE*, France’s regulated charge for using public electricity networks. It remunerates the networks and covers costs accepted under the regulatory framework. RTE’s share of eligible investment enters this collective funding channel. The current transmission framework is TURPE 7 HTB, with rates revised from 1 August 2026. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-05" title="S05" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-08" title="S08" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

The future campus would itself generate network revenue through its use of the system. Assessing its net effect on other users would require comparing incremental costs, upfront contributions and expected tariff revenue over time. The public documents examined do not supply that calculation for Ozans. They establish part of the funding allocation, while leaving its eventual effect on bills unresolved.

## A regional line with several potential beneficiaries

The larger transmission project creates a further attribution problem. RTE describes the Éguzon–Marmagne reinforcement as approximately 100 kilometers of line, with €300 million of investment and 5,400 MW of additional transmission capacity. That figure describes additional transfer capacity across the shared network, potentially serving several users. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-09" title="S09" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

The campus would depend on this reinforcement to increase its 400 kV connection from 300 MW to 1,200 MW. RTE, however, presents the line as serving national electricity flows and regional industrial development. At a public meeting in Ambrault on 30 September 2026, the operator said the project would remain necessary if Google withdrew. This is RTE’s explanation, recorded in the meeting minutes; the minutes are not an independent appraisal of the line’s economic value. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.57–59" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-10" title="S10, pp.12–13" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>


Shared infrastructure can improve supply to several users, accommodate changing power flows and retain value beyond its first prominent prospective customer. A very large applicant can also influence the timing, size or location of an investment.

Sound cost attribution would compare two documented network plans: one with Ozans and another without it, using the same dates and other known demand. That comparison could identify the additional expenditure actually caused by the campus. The technical dependency alone cannot justify assigning the entire €300 million regional project to Google.

Transparency therefore also means explaining RTE’s assumptions: which needs justify each asset, when they arise, and how much of the asset’s usefulness depends on each stage of the campus proceeding.

## Land sales pay for roads, after public cash has been committed

For Châteauroux Métropole, the local intermunicipal authority, the funding channel runs through land development. The dossier says it would build peripheral roads serving Google’s plots and other sites in the development zone, with land sales financing the work. Google would build roads inside the campus. Water infrastructure provided by the authority would likewise serve the wider zone. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.38–39" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-19" title="S19" aria-label="Source 19">19</a></sup>

This model ties up cash. The developer acquires land and equips the zone, then receives proceeds as plots are sold. The executed accounts offer a concrete example: in 2025, the Ozans development-zone budget received an advance of approximately €3.266 million from the authority’s main budget. The figure covers the whole zone. It measures public financing carried by the development operation, without establishing a grant to Google or the campus’s net cost to the authority. The accounts were approved on 30 June 2026. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-13" title="S13, PDF p.2" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

Future land-sale receipts can repay earlier expenditure. Evaluating the outcome requires comparing cash actually received with acquisition costs, infrastructure spending, maintenance and the cost of committing public funds over time.

The zone’s general land-sale specifications, dated February 2023, place a plot’s connection to the public water network at the buyer’s expense. They also allocate certain additional connections and equipment for higher water pressure to the buyer. These general rules help distinguish zone-wide infrastructure from private facilities. Google’s specific obligations would need to be examined in the deeds and agreements governing its own project. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-12" title="S12, pp.15–16" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

In the public material reviewed, we found no confirmation of the complete 2026 land-receipt schedule or an updated account isolating the local infrastructure required by the campus. That gap prevents a conclusion about the authority’s eventual net gain or net cost.

## Water circulates; heat has to leave

Much of the electricity used by computing equipment becomes heat. Removing that heat is a continuous function of a data center. Ozans is designed around dry cooling, using a closed water loop and transferring heat to the outside air. Water circulates within the system; evaporative cooling is excluded from the proposed normal operating design. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.70–71" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

This explains the different water figures in the dossier. For one building, the initial cooling-loop fill is estimated at approximately 1,000 cubic meters, followed by occasional top-ups. Sanitary uses form a separate flow, averaging an estimated 10.4 cubic meters a day. Sanitary wastewater is also estimated at 10.4 cubic meters a day. The initial fill is a one-off input; the daily figures describe incoming water and outgoing wastewater. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.71" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>


<GoogleOzansFigure lang="en" kind="cooling" />


Treating these volumes as interchangeable would distort the demand picture. Initial filling follows the building-opening schedule. Sanitary demand depends on operation. Cooling-loop top-ups need separate monitoring. The project also includes other requirements, notably construction and fire protection. The daily sanitary estimate describes water supplied through a network, with an estimated discharge to wastewater treatment. It is not a measure of net water consumption across the wider catchment. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.70–72" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The dossier envisages supply from the authority’s water network, without direct groundwater abstraction to supply the campus’s uses. Hydrogeological studies will also assess whether construction requires temporary groundwater drawdown. Assessment must therefore follow that network back to its water source and account for seasonal constraints. The proposed dry-cooling system reduces recurring water demand associated with evaporation. Performance and volumes will still need to be established through further studies and eventual operation: the documents currently describe a proposed design. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.70–73" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Heat also offers a possible local benefit. The dossier considers recovery for uses including agricultural greenhouses and drying. Recoverable heat becomes useful when a customer can receive it at an appropriate temperature, within an economically viable distance and at the times it is needed. Transfer equipment and, where required, equipment to raise the temperature add costs of their own. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.90" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Studies of potential users are continuing. We found no contracted volume of delivered heat or supply agreement in the reviewed material from which to calculate local revenue or savings. Heat recovery remains an option to develop. Its value will depend on matching the campus’s operation with a sufficiently regular nearby demand. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.90" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## The employment estimates reach across France

The published employment ranges are substantial: an annual average of 10,600 to 14,200 jobs supported during construction, followed by 1,800 to 2,600 in the early operating years. The project summary specifies the geographical scope as the French economy. Both ranges combine direct, indirect and induced employment, and both are estimates from the project sponsors. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.95–96" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-20" title="S20, p.11" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

The calculation extends beyond the people entering the campus each morning. Construction spending supports contractors, which buy materials and services from suppliers. Income paid along that chain supports further household spending. Economic-impact models seek to capture this propagation. Their results depend on assumptions about procurement, imports, productivity and consumption.





For residents of Indre, the immediate question is how much of this activity will remain in the department. The published national totals give neither the campus’s permanent direct workforce nor a local allocation of supported employment. We found no methodological annex allowing the Ozans ranges to be reproduced. They should be read as sponsor estimates, within the geographical and employment categories those sponsors identify.

An annual average describes a level of employment supported over a period. Presenting it as that many additional hires every year would change the measure. Construction and operation also involve different activities; the gap between their ranges does not measure jobs destroyed. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, pp.95–96" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-20" title="S20, p.11" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

Local benefits will also depend on practical choices: access to contracts for nearby businesses, available skills, training and the retention of expertise. A national estimate may be economically relevant while only partly answering the community’s question. Bridging that gap requires location assumptions, expected occupations and a staffing timetable for people working directly at the campus or providing services on site.

## Tax receipts have their own geography

The dossier discusses property tax, the property-based business levy known as CFE, and the business levy on value added, CVAE, among potential fiscal benefits. It says definitive amounts are for the tax administration to determine. The reviewed documents provide no verifiable annual tax yield specific to Ozans. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.97" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

One detail changes how CVAE should be understood locally. Since 1 January 2023, receipts from this business levy on value added have been allocated to the central government budget. Affected local authorities receive compensation based on a share of VAT. The French tax administration’s local-government portal explains that, since 2025, the allocation of the dynamic component for municipalities and intermunicipal bodies takes property rental values and employee numbers into account. Local activity therefore retains a connection to the distribution formula, but through a different mechanism from an individual company’s CVAE being paid directly to its local authority. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-15" title="S15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

Current law also schedules CVAE’s abolition for 1 January 2030, around the first building’s target opening. Projections therefore need to use the regime expected at commissioning and be updated if legislation changes. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-15" title="S15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

Any local fiscal benefit has to be established tax by tax, using the relevant bases, rates, exemptions, recipients and commissioning dates. Expected revenue then needs to be compared with associated local spending. We found no public confirmation of a campus-specific net figure, or of a specific exemption that would allow that calculation to be completed.

## The contract matters when development slows

A campus built over ten to twenty years raises a central question: how do Google’s commitments develop alongside those of the grid? The dossier says the first building could operate on its own if later phases were abandoned. It also mentions the possibility of returning undeveloped land to the authority. Phased development makes the provisions governing expenditure already committed particularly relevant. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.105" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

RTE’s general rules govern entry into the connection queue, monitoring of project maturity and the circumstances in which capacity is released. The procedure approved by the energy regulator, CRE, in February 2026 includes annual progress monitoring for the relevant applications and transitional provisions for older applications. The rules applicable to a specific project depend on the signed documents and their dates. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" title="S07, pp.33–40 and 49" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-17" title="S17" aria-label="Source 17">17</a></sup>

For termination, the procedure first defers to the terms of the contract. Where those do not provide otherwise, it sets out rules including the treatment of payments made to enter or remain in the queue. Fixed payments permanently retained by RTE, together with their accrued remuneration, reduce the costs to be recovered through network tariffs. Conversely, potential [stranded expenditure](/en/glossary/actif-echoue/) on upstream reinforcement studies and works increases those costs if a project fails to proceed. Assessing those costs requires distinguishing expenditure made redundant by the project’s exit from assets that could still serve other users. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" title="S07, pp.39–40" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>





This general mechanism explains the risk. It does not quantify the amount the network would carry if Ozans were abandoned. The dossier says the maximum capacity has been contracted, but the reviewed public material did not contain the technical and financial connection offers, signed connection agreements or financial annexes. Guarantees, conditions for reducing reserved power and the precise allocation of consequences from delays therefore remain to be documented publicly. <sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-01" title="S01, p.74" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-07" title="S07" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

The central issue is the timing of commitments. Which works become irreversible, when does Google commit to paying for them, and which assets would remain useful if ultimate demand were lower? Staged construction can limit some premature spending. It also requires coordination between buildings, reserved capacity and the grid’s investment decisions.

## Financial commitments still open to public scrutiny

The dossier already allows part of the money to be followed. Google would fund its campus and much of the connection infrastructure. The grid would carry the share provided for under tariff rules. The local authority would use land-sale receipts to finance development. The eventual outcome depends on actual expenditure, use and the timing of revenues.

The most useful next documents would be an asset-by-asset breakdown of connection costs, a schedule of financial commitments, an updated account of land and local infrastructure, and a verifiable description of on-site employment. Water volumes and heat arrangements should become clearer as studies progress. For the grid, explicit scenarios with and without successive campus phases would help establish shared value and residual risk.

Ozans could bring a long-term user to shared infrastructure, business to suppliers and revenue to local authorities. The strength of that benefit will depend on connecting the promised returns to the spending and commitments required to achieve them. These details can still inform consultation before authorization applications and the main commitments to construction.

### Method and cutoff

Documentary analysis verified on 6 November 2026. Google and RTE materials describe a proposed project and sponsor estimates. Funding arrangements were checked against official rules, and the development zone’s financial carrying against executed public accounts. No site visit, interview or audit of confidential contracts was undertaken. Statements that public confirmation was not found refer to the material reviewed. Future dates remain conditional. The references identify the passages, definitions and documentary limits. The sharing image is a conceptual illustration, separate from the project’s actual layout or construction status.
