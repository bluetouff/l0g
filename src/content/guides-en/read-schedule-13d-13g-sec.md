Beneficial ownership above **5%** of a covered equity class generally triggers a disclosure obligation. Schedule 13D is the default detailed report. Schedule 13G is available only under specified eligibility categories, including qualified institutions, passive investors and exempt investors. An activist’s plans may appear in Item 4 of a 13D, but a 13D filing alone does not establish an activist campaign. Start with the [SEC’s final rules and guidance](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2023/33-11253.pdf).

## Beneficial ownership and reporting categories

Schedules 13D and 13G concern beneficial ownership of a covered class of equity securities, generally a class registered under Section 12 of the Exchange Act. The threshold is ownership of **more than 5%**, subject to the applicable rules and exemptions. Eligibility for 13G must be checked before choosing the shorter form. [SEC rules](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2023/33-11253.pdf).

For a hypothetical **5.2%** holding, the filing category, certification, acquisition circumstances and stated plans must be read together. The percentage alone does not establish control intent. A 13G can reflect an institutional or exempt category rather than the passive-investor category. [SEC guidance](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2023/33-11253.pdf).

## Where the forms come from

The legal framework is set out in Sections 13(d) and 13(g) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and Regulation 13D-G. [SEC interpretation guide](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/corporation-finance-interpretations/exchange-act-sections-13d-13g-regulation-13d-g-beneficial-ownership-reporting). The logic is control transparency: once a holder crosses 5%, the market and other shareholders should know who is there, how much they own, and what they intend to do with that ownership.

This is why these filings matter in takeover fights, activist campaigns and slow accumulations. They are not just position reports; they are early warnings about possible governance pressure.

## The dividing line: intent

Intent is one dividing line; category eligibility and ownership thresholds also matter. A Schedule 13D can disclose plans involving management, board seats, asset sales or control, but it is also the default form when a holder does not qualify for 13G. The passive-investor and qualified-institutional categories carry control-intent conditions; exempt investors are a separate category. [SEC rules](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2023/33-11253.pdf).

The important signal can be the switch. A passive holder that develops an intent to influence must move from 13G to 13D within **five business days**. Check the triggering event and the amended filing rather than inferring a campaign from the form alone. [SEC rules, including Rules 13d-1(e), (f) and (g)](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2023/33-11253.pdf).

## Deadlines changed in 2024

A lot of old pages are now stale. The SEC accelerated beneficial-ownership reporting. Since **February 5, 2024**, an initial 13D must be filed within **five business days** after crossing 5%, down from ten calendar days. Material 13D amendments are due within **two business days**, replacing the older, vaguer “promptly” standard.

Since **September 30, 2024**, 13G deadlines have also tightened: five business days for passive investors, forty-five days after quarter-end for qualified institutional investors, and quarterly rather than annual amendments when material changes occur. Since **December 18, 2024**, these reports use structured data. The filing cut-off has also been extended to 10 p.m. Eastern time. Additional accelerated deadlines apply to some larger holdings and changes. Check the filer category and triggering event in the [final-rule tables and compliance dates](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2023/33-11253.pdf); there is no single 13G deadline covering every situation.

## Item 4: the activist signal

In a 13D, one section carries most of the signal: **Item 4, Purpose of Transaction**. This is where the investor describes its plans: board representation, strategic alternatives, asset sales, capital allocation, buybacks, governance demands, or even control intentions.

A 13D with a vague Item 4 is not the same as one that states the investor has contacted management, requested board seats or prepared proposals. Reading a 13D means reading Item 4 first.

## The group problem

The subtle trap is the idea of a “group.” When several investors act together to acquire, hold or vote securities, their stakes are aggregated for the 5% threshold. The SEC has clarified that a group can arise when a future filer intentionally communicates its plan to file a 13D in order to induce another investor to buy, and that other investor buys.

Parallel purchases or a shared investment view do not by themselves establish a reporting group. The agreements and conduct must be assessed under the [SEC’s group guidance](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2023/33-11253.pdf).

## How to read the filings on EDGAR

Everything is public on [EDGAR](/glossaire/#edgar). Search either the company or the reporting person, then filter for `SC 13D`, `SC 13G` and amended versions marked `/A`.

The reading order is simple. First identify the filing type, eligibility category and stated intent. Then read Item 4 of the 13D to understand intent. Watch for conversions from 13G to 13D. Finally, reconstruct possible groups by looking at filings on the same issuer over a short window.

## Limits

These disclosures have blind spots. Positions below 5% stay invisible. Synthetic exposure through derivatives has long complicated the regime and is still not perfectly captured. A holder can remain passive in form while approaching a level that makes it influential in practice. The shortened deadlines still leave an accumulation window. Group doctrine remains fact-intensive. A filing is not an investment thesis; it is the start of the investigation.

## Confluence with 13F and Form 4

The value rises when 13D/13G data is crossed with other signals. [Form 13F](/en/guides/how-to-analyze-sec-13f-filings/) shows reportable Section 13(f) securities held by covered managers, quarterly and with delay; it is not the manager’s complete portfolio. [Form 4](/en/guides/how-to-read-sec-form-4/) shows insider transactions within two business days. A 13D adds the missing layer: intent.

These disclosures can document different aspects of ownership, but their dates, coverage and transaction types differ. Their combination does not establish future performance. This is the logic behind <a href="https://13flow.eu" rel="noopener">13FLOW</a>.
