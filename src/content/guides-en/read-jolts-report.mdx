A monthly employment balance can conceal very different patterns of hiring and departures. JOLTS helps identify them. This guide uses the [BLS release for May 2026, published June 30](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_06302026.htm), as a fixed reading example. Its figures are that release’s seasonally adjusted estimates, not the latest readings or a later revised vintage.

## The labour-market flows captured by JOLTS

The [Employment Situation](/en/guides/read-us-employment-report-nfp/) gives a snapshot: net jobs created or lost during the month. JOLTS gives the movie behind that snapshot. Hiring and separations can both be large even when net employment changes little. However, payrolls and JOLTS are different statistical programmes; their monthly estimates need not reconcile exactly. See the [BLS definitions and comparisons](https://www.bls.gov/jlt/jltdef.htm).

JOLTS is published after the reference month. Check the [BLS release calendar](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/2026/home.htm) rather than assuming a fixed interval from the payroll report. Its series describe vacancies, hiring and the nature of separations. A labor market can show a stable payroll balance while activity underneath freezes: fewer hires, fewer quits, fewer layoffs. Payrolls would miss the rotation; JOLTS shows it.

## The five key numbers

JOLTS is read through five series. **Job openings** measure unsatisfied labor demand: positions open, actively recruited for, and able to start within 30 days. The opening exists on the last business day of the month; hires and separations are flows over the whole month. [BLS data definitions](https://www.bls.gov/jlt/jltdef.htm). **Hires** count workers added during the month. **Total separations** include all exits, split into **quits**, **layoffs and discharges**, and other separations.

In May 2026 the U.S. economy had **7.6 million** openings, **5.2 million** hires, **3.1 million** quits, **1.7 million** layoffs and discharges, and **5.1 million** total separations. These rounded levels come from the [archived May release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_06302026.htm). Quits and layoffs are components of separations, so do not add them to the total.

## The quits rate: worker confidence

The quits rate is quits during the month divided by employment, expressed as a percentage. The BLS describes it as a measure of workers’ willingness or ability to leave jobs. That can inform an interpretation of confidence, but it is not a direct survey of expectations and cannot by itself establish wage pressure. [BLS release and definitions](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_06302026.htm).

In the fixed May example, the release reports a **3.3%** hiring rate, **3.2%** total-separations rate, **1.9%** quits rate and **1.1%** layoffs-and-discharges rate. Use the published rates: dividing rounded headline counts can produce a different result. [BLS, tables 2–5](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_06302026.htm).

## Job openings per unemployed worker

Divide job openings by unemployed people for the same reference month, using compatible seasonal adjustment. The denominator comes from the household survey, not JOLTS. The [BLS Employment Situation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_06052026.htm) explains the household and establishment measures.

The result is a ratio, not the probability that a particular unemployed person will receive an offer. Skills, location, recruitment time and mismatched survey populations matter. A ratio below one does not create an automatic recession threshold. Record both inputs and the release vintage when making the calculation.

## The Beveridge curve and the soft landing

The Beveridge curve links the job openings rate and the unemployment rate. In normal slowdowns, openings fall and unemployment rises. The post-2022 soft-landing bet was that the economy could slide down the steep part of the curve: fewer vacancies, little unemployment damage.

A movement along the curve and a shift of the curve have different interpretations. Compare several observations before describing a change in matching efficiency. The curve does not supply a fixed point at which vacancies must turn into unemployment. JOLTS is evidence for a labour-market assessment alongside payrolls, household employment and other indicators.

## Measurement traps

JOLTS has all the weaknesses of a survey, and then some. Its estimates are subject to sampling error, nonresponse and revisions; the [BLS Handbook of Methods](https://www.bls.gov/opub/hom/jlt/pdf/jlt.pdf) describes the survey design and estimation. One month should never be over-read; the three- to six-month trend carries the signal.

An online advertisement is not automatically a JOLTS opening. The survey has specific conditions for an existing job, a possible start within 30 days and active outside recruitment. Check revisions, confidence intervals and the release date before treating a one-month movement as a trend. [BLS definitions](https://www.bls.gov/jlt/jltdef.htm).

## Keep the reference month visible

The May example describes that release’s observations. It does not establish the state of the labour market when a reader opens this guide. Open the [BLS archive](https://www.bls.gov/bls/news-release/jolts.htm), select a dated release and compare like with like before drawing a contemporary conclusion. Pair the result with the [payroll guide](/en/guides/read-us-employment-report-nfp/) and the [dot plot guide](/en/guides/read-dot-plot-sep/) when examining monetary-policy assumptions.

## How to use it

Read JOLTS as the indispensable counterpoint to payrolls. Payrolls give the net balance; JOLTS gives the flows. The quits rate informs an interpretation of willingness or ability to leave. The openings-to-unemployed ratio tells labor-market tightness. The Beveridge curve helps organise the comparison between vacancies and unemployment. Separations, split between quits and layoffs, tell what kind of slowdown is underway.
