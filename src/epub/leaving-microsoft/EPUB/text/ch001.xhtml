Introduction

A public service must survive a change of hands

A member of staff opens a case, checks a document and sends a decision. To the person waiting for an answer, the software behind that work is usually a secondary concern. For the public body, keeping the task possible as its technology changes is a lasting responsibility. This investigation starts with ordinary administrative work and follows a political question: how can an institution choose its tools and pass the means of making that choice to the people who come after it?

Microsoft provides the thread connecting the chapters. The records examined reveal relationships between office software, email, accounts, applications and hosting. Chapter one follows a roads agency application in Schleswig-Holstein that still calls Excel during a move to another office suite. That small connection takes the investigation behind the desktop. A digital case depends on its contents, the people permitted to access it and the operations they need to perform.

Those relationships have a history. Chapter two goes back to purchasing and standardisation. Combining orders or aligning configurations can make an IT estate easier to run. The resulting environment then shapes how later services are built. A buyer preparing a renewal inherits that organisation of work. Understanding the original reasons for a choice helps distinguish continuing benefits from constraints that a new decision could reduce.

Chapter three follows usage rights into the cloud. Moving an application requires both a working destination and the licences needed to run it there. The conditions vary with products, versions and commercial programmes. Chapter four returns to the employee’s desk, where a budget template or a consultation automation can embed administrative rules in software. Recovering those rules is part of taking over the service, alongside retrieving its documents and data.

Funding introduces several timelines. An avoided renewal, an investment in transition and an expected annual saving measure different things. Chapter five examines those distinctions in German public records. It also follows periods of parallel operation and time spent helping colleagues. Assessing a programme requires knowing which services the money covers, over what period, and which resources will still be needed once the switch is complete.

Chapter six looks beyond the launch. A fix needs testing alongside neighbouring applications; a team must be able to install the next release and resolve incidents. The cases provide examples of that organisation, within the limits of the accounts available. The final chapter follows its handover through contracts, assigned responsibilities and exit exercises. An option can endure when the next team can understand and exercise the rights its predecessors secured.

This perspective gives digital sovereignty a practical meaning: capabilities that can be described for each service, alongside the dependencies accepted and the means of reviewing them. Hosting location, security, software rights and staff expertise address connected questions. Bringing them together requires judgement. The chapters retain suppliers’ arguments and the benefits of integrated services where these help explain the choices available to a public buyer.

This edition contains all seven investigations, their references and fifteen infographics, with an original introduction and conclusion. Its documentary cut-off is 9 October 2026. Historical cases retain their dates; announcements remain attributed to their authors and forecasts retain their status. The book offers a way to read public decisions and hold institutions to account for what follows, from the initial contract through to maintaining an alternative that another team can continue to use.

The mechanisms and references behind this overview are developed in the seven chapters.
