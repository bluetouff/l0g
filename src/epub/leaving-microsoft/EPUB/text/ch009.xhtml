Conclusion

Passing on the ability to decide

Across these chapters, an exit becomes a succession of responsibilities. Someone must identify an application’s dependencies, check the available rights, fund parallel operation and help staff facing difficulties. Another team will eventually inherit that work. Freedom of choice therefore has an institutional dimension: the knowledge, records and resources needed to exercise it must remain usable through changes of personnel. The handover matters as much as the initial decision.

The opening chapters show how early that responsibility begins. One procurement helps establish the environment for the next. A licence assignment rule shapes access for new users. A procedure built around a workbook or an email service can extend the dependency to other teams. Preserving options requires the public body to explain those choices and retrieve the materials needed to change them. That knowledge also supports an informed decision to retain an integration whose benefits justify its constraints.

Money requires the same continuity of scrutiny. The records examined in chapter five track some expenditure and expectations while leaving the full cost unresolved. A useful public assessment would place commitments, costs incurred and savings actually available on comparable scopes and periods. It would also make internal work visible. Time spent training or helping a colleague uses organisational capacity even when it produces no additional invoice. That resource has to be considered without counting the same cost twice.

Service continuity offers another way to assess progress. Chapter six follows an email transfer into justice applications, then examines upgrades and maintenance. These accounts direct attention to the tasks staff can complete, the incidents they encounter and the resources available to resolve them. The evidence describes how that work is organised. Comparable observations over time would allow a fuller assessment of the results and of the experience of people using the service.

The final chapter connects daily practice to future decisions. Contracts can arrange for documentation and takeover materials to be delivered throughout the relationship. Architecture can make particular components replaceable. Exercises can identify remaining adaptations and improve the estimate of the work ahead. Each has a distinct role: a right must be exercisable, a function must be tested in its intended environment and an assigned responsibility must come with adequate resources.

The destination deserves the same attention. A different technology or operator opens possibilities and creates new working relationships. A team may take on more operations; several public bodies may contribute to the same software. The book proposes examining these commitments as closely as those attached to the outgoing provider. The ability to change needs continued care after the first successful transfer, as staff, applications and requirements evolve.

Readers can follow the next stages through concrete records: a renewed contract, a retired service, an incident report, a maintained fix or a documented exit exercise. Dates and scope will allow those records to be compared with earlier ambitions. A public institution retains meaningful choice when it leaves its successors enough knowledge, rights and working capacity to make an informed decision of their own. That is a capability worth maintaining across successive contracts and political terms.

The sources for this synthesis remain attached to the corresponding chapters.
