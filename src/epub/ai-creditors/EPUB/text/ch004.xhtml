The loan examined in our second instalment also appears in the accounts of two US investment funds. Searching for its legal borrower, CoreWeave Financing DDTL V-V, brings it into view among hundreds of investments. At 30 September 2026, Apollo Debt Solutions BDC held about $13 million of funded principal, and Blue Owl Technology Finance almost $9 million. S01 S02 S03

Following those holdings leads to the funds’ own creditors. Blue Owl Technology Finance has financing subsidiaries that borrow from banks. One, Athena Funding III, has a facility to which Deutsche Bank committed $150 million. The published agreement provides individual lender commitments, including an annex naming the institutions. Funds sit between AI infrastructure and bank balance sheets. They hold loans, contribute capital and may borrow against investments. Their accounts reveal the financing relationships, while the contracts define the protections available to each creditor.

CoreWeave in two investment portfolios

CoreWeave calls the facility DDTL 5.5, a delayed-draw term loan allowing borrowing in stages. It finances servers equipped with graphics processing units, or GPUs, and related infrastructure. The facility provides $2.6 billion of capacity, with $1.555 billion of gross debt outstanding reported at 30 September. The borrower’s legal identity connects that liability to positions disclosed by the funds. S01 S06

Apollo Debt Solutions, or ADS, and Blue Owl Technology Finance, or OTF, are business development companies, known as BDCs: US investment companies that finance businesses and publish detailed investment schedules. Each has its own portfolio, investors and creditors. The accounts describe those investment vehicles, separately from the asset-management groups Apollo and Blue Owl. S02 S03 S07

Their schedules allow a consistent addition: $21.973 million of funded principal held, roughly $22 million, against the same borrower on the same reporting date. This is the principal represented by the portfolio holdings. Tracing how they were acquired, whether at origination or in secondary trades, would require the relevant purchase or subscription records. S02 S03

The funds separately disclose $14.775 million of unfunded commitments. They must provide the agreed amounts when contractual drawdown conditions are satisfied. Those obligations introduce a further demand on liquidity: cash the funds may need to keep financing the borrower. S02 S03

Athena borrows for the fund

Athena Funding III is an OTF financing subsidiary included in the fund’s consolidated accounts. This type of special-purpose vehicle, or SPV, holds assets and borrows under its own contractual arrangements. The subsidiary list identifies its relationship with the fund, and the financing agreement defines the banks’ rights. S03 S05

The amendment signed on 11 September 2026 specifies $150 million from Deutsche Bank AG’s New York branch and $100 million from First Citizens. Deutsche Bank signs as a committed lender as well as acting as the facility agent. At 30 September, OTF reported $75 million drawn under the $250 million facility. The table gives the syndicate’s total outstanding balance, leaving individual lenders’ drawings unidentified. S03 S04

OTF’s investment schedule has a general pledge provision covering its financing arrangements. The CoreWeave position falls under that rule, while other holdings carry an exclusion footnote. Assigning this particular claim to Athena III requires a current collateral schedule: the accounts list several facilities, and the public documents reviewed leave the allocation among them open. S03 S04

The documented relationship therefore connects Deutsche Bank to a subsidiary of a fund holding CoreWeave debt. Its $150 million commitment finances Athena III and its portfolio. Treating that amount as CoreWeave exposure would change the borrower and the purpose of the agreement.

Collateral determines available liquidity

After the $75 million of drawings, Athena III’s facility had $175 million of nominal unused capacity. In the next column, OTF reported $93.727 million available, reflecting limitations tied to the pledged assets. The contractual ceiling and usable borrowing capacity describe different constraints within the same arrangement. S03

Lenders set the criteria for eligible assets, the value they recognise and the concentrations they will accept. Those inputs feed into a borrowing base, the contractual calculation governing borrowing against the portfolio. An asset may count for a fraction of its value, and excessive concentration can reduce the amount recognised. S04

The $93.7 million is the availability reported at 30 September, subject to the facility’s other conditions. The gap from $175 million helps explain its structure. Assessing whether lending terms had recently tightened would require collateral values and usable capacity at more than one date.

The arrangement can become restrictive if the value recognised for the portfolio falls. When a borrowing-base deficiency arises under the agreement, remedies include adding eligible assets, contributing cash or selling investments, within the applicable deadlines and conditions. S04 A loan may keep paying interest while supporting less borrowing by its holder. Portfolio financing facilities make that connection between valuation and liquidity particularly clear.

The order and scope of protection

Equity contributed to a vehicle absorbs losses before senior claims are impaired. The ECB highlights this protection in its analysis of bank relationships with private-market funds, also noting that some funds may be less leveraged than banks. These are general mechanisms whose effectiveness depends on the portfolio and the agreements. S08

Athena III’s agreement limits recourse against the vehicle to its collateral, subject to the contractual qualifications. OTF retains the obligations attached to its own roles, including as services provider. Consolidated reporting combines the subsidiary’s accounts with the fund’s, while the contracts define the legal rights of recourse. S04

Position size also matters. OTF reports the CoreWeave loan examined here at $9.025 million of fair value, against $15.182 billion of consolidated assets, or about 0.06%. The calculation uses the estimated accounting value of this single holding. Measuring the fund’s entire AI portfolio would require a classification of its other investments. S03

How a borrower’s difficulties reach a bank depends on the holding’s weight in the collateral pool, the portfolio’s decline in value and the capital absorbing losses first. Trouble may reduce investors’ returns or available financing before impairing a more senior claim if those protections become insufficient.

A collateral exclusion at BCRED

At 30 September, Blackstone Private Credit Fund, or BCRED, held $104.379 million of principal in Firmus Supercloud. Firmus describes AI computing infrastructure in its financing announcement of 9 February. S09 S10

This position carries a footnote expressly excluding it from the assets pledged under the fund’s credit facilities. BCRED also reports borrowing by its subsidiaries in the same accounts. A map of the collateral must leave this holding outside the facilities covered by the exclusion. The fund’s broader sources of financing are a separate question. S09

The comparison with OTF sharpens the method. A general pledge provision opens an inquiry into how assets are assigned among vehicles. An explicit exclusion rules out that collateral link for the specified holding. A bank’s name and an AI asset appearing in the same accounts need contractual evidence to connect them.

Cash demands can build on both sides

A fund must meet its commitments to borrowers even when its portfolio might provide less financing capacity. The $14.8 million of unfunded CoreWeave commitments identified across ADS and OTF illustrates that conditional obligation. S02 S03

The IMF examines this risk across private credit in its October 2026 report. In a sample of ten large BDCs, four had borrower commitments exceeding the liquidity buffer calculated by the authors. That buffer adds cash and available undrawn secured facilities, then subtracts short-term unsecured debt. The exercise concerns the funding balance of the BDCs studied, beyond AI financing alone. S11

Shareholder liquidity follows a different route at exchange-listed OTF. A sale to another investor transfers shares in the market, without creating a claim for redemption by the fund at net asset value. OTF may choose to buy back its own stock under its repurchase programme. The fund must also finance borrower drawdowns and its debt maturities. S03

Counting debt at each level

Measuring the debt received by CoreWeave requires keeping the calculation at that borrower’s level. Loans obtained by funds and their subsidiaries are further financial relationships. Keeping them on the map helps identify bank exposures and shared risks, while avoiding counting the same money more than once as funding delivered to the project.

Ted Berg and Jung Hoon Lee explain the tracing problem in a study published by the US Office of Financial Research. Their work shows that searching for borrowing under a BDC’s name alone can miss loans taken out through its SPVs. Consolidated accounts help recover those obligations and identify the entities carrying them. S07

“Off balance sheet” then requires identifying whose balance sheet is meant. An SPV’s debt may be consolidated in the fund’s accounts, while the corresponding bank loan appears as an asset for its creditor. The fund’s non-bank status describes the intermediary. Its contracts and accounts locate the obligations, security and limits on recourse. S03 S04

The next step is to follow promises of protection. When an insurer covers a loan, the risk also depends on its obligations, exclusions and capacity to pay. That extends our investigation into insurance for AI lending.

Limitations

This investigation uses public documents reviewed through 17 November 2026. Fund accounts and agreements are filings made by the parties with the SEC, and the interim financial statements are unaudited. Institutional research provides a framework for interpreting the mechanisms.

The review covers two holdings in a single CoreWeave facility and a different collateral case at BCRED. The $21.973 million of funded principal and $14.775 million of unfunded commitments are calculated from disclosures rounded to thousands of dollars. Each figure retains its measure, date and currency. Bank lending to the funds and CoreWeave’s facility capacity remain separate amounts. The investigation calculates no European total or loss estimate.

Completing the chain requires collateral allocations among vehicles, current positions by bank, transfers and external credit protection. The diagrams identify documented relationships and unknown allocations. Risks described conditionally remain scenarios.
