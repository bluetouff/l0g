AI debt: mapping the banks, funds and insurers

Published: 2026-11-18T11:01:46+01:00

Original online edition

AI’s creditors · 6/6. Previous instalment: the conditions attached to the cash. Explore the investigation in our AI financing dossier. Documents reviewed and information current to 18 November 2026.

The name occupies a single line: CoreWeave Financing DDTL V-V, LLC. It appears in six funds’ investment schedules, among hundreds of other holdings. Each line leads back to the same delayed-draw term loan, DDTL 5.5. CoreWeave reported $1.555 billion of gross debt outstanding at 30 September. By matching the funds’ disclosures, we identified $57.778 million of funded principal, or roughly 3.7% of that balance. S01 S03 S04 S05 S06 S07 S08

That small slice opens a window onto a much larger network. Some funds holding these loans borrow from banks themselves. Insurers buy securities or take on conditional payment obligations. Banks can therefore encounter AI financing at several points on their balance sheets, while the same debt moves between holders or acquires new protection.

Starting with the Jupiter loan, this series has followed banks, funds, insurers and the contractual conditions governing cash. The final instalment brings those findings together. Public documents support precise holdings and identifiable connections. They also explain why adding “banks, private credit and off-balance-sheet exposure” would produce a distorted picture of the risk.

Six portfolios behind one facility

The third instalment identified two holders: Apollo Debt Solutions BDC and Blue Owl Technology Finance. Our wider search adds Blue Owl Capital Corporation, Ares Core Infrastructure Fund and two HPS funds. All six publish investment schedules dated 30 September 2026. The borrower’s name, the instrument and the loan’s terms allow the entries to be matched. S03 S04 S05 S06 S07 S08

The entries first need to be put on the same funded-principal basis. Both HPS funds include undrawn commitments in reported par, so we subtract those amounts. The Blue Owl funds report identical allocations in separate portfolios, whose consolidation boundaries we checked: each holding counts once. Calculations and page references appear in the source notes. S05 S07 S08

Together, the six funds also disclose $38.850 million of unfunded commitments. Those promises are tracked separately. Future funding remains subject to contractual conditions, so they stay outside the 3.7% calculation. Funded principal, the price paid for a holding and its reported fair value also answer different questions. S03 S04 S05 S06 S07 S08

Roughly 96.3% of the outstanding loan sits outside these six identified holdings. Its allocation among other funds, banks and investors remains unknown. Moving from portfolio holdings to each lender’s identity would require the loan register, alongside records of any participation interests and purchased credit protection. S02

The bank names on the agreement’s cover tell a different part of the story. Crédit Agricole CIB, Deutsche Bank New York and Société Générale appear among the bookrunners arranging the loan’s placement. The public filing omits the allocation schedule. It establishes their arranging role, while each bank’s retained principal remains unknown. S02

The lender has a lender

Consider one of the six funds, Blue Owl Technology Finance, or OTF. Its portfolio contains $8.969 million of funded principal in DDTL 5.5. Its accounts also lead to Athena Funding III, a consolidated subsidiary with a bank financing facility of its own. This is a special purpose vehicle, or SPV, used here to borrow against a pool of assets. S04

The agreement amended on 11 September assigns $150 million of commitments to Deutsche Bank New York and $100 million to First-Citizens. At 30 September, $75 million was drawn across the facility. The reviewed accounts do not allocate that drawn balance between the two banks. S09 S04

These records establish the banks’ connection to the fund. Extending the path to CoreWeave would require the actual portfolio pledged under Athena III’s facility. OTF describes a general pledge of its assets without assigning this holding to that particular facility. The diagram leaves the final link open. S04 S09

The distinction explains how to read the two layers. A bank has a claim on a fund financing subsidiary. The fund holds a position in CoreWeave’s debt. Where a bank loan refinances a fund’s underlying claim, adding both as separate contributions to the project counts part of the financing twice. The map of creditor relationships preserves both layers: each lender has its own rights, security and place in the repayment order.

Bank lenders generally benefit from the equity beneath their debt and restrictions on eligible collateral. In Athena III’s agreement, the usable facility amount depends on a borrowing base calculated from the assets securing it. A deterioration in that pool could therefore reduce available credit before a bank recognises a loss. The outcome would depend on the affected assets and the applicable terms. S09

Deutsche Bank itself acknowledged, in its 29 July presentation, that a small part of its data-centre exposure also appeared in its private-credit portfolio. It gave no amount. That disclosed overlap reinforces the point: categories used in investor presentations can cover some of the same exposures. S14

European banks report different exposure measures

The ECB’s Financial Stability Review, published on 18 November, distinguishes two ways of measuring AI-related bank lending. A sector-based approach covers data centres, semiconductors and software development. It reaches approximately €75 billion, equivalent to 1.1% of euro area banks’ total committed corporate exposures. These sectors include businesses extending beyond AI. S23

A company-based selection follows 165 important firms in the AI supply chain and their subsidiaries. In data compiled up to June 2026, commitments to this group totalled €24.3 billion, including €11 billion undrawn. The ECB notes incomplete coverage of some financing booked outside the euro area. The two selections overlap only partly. Reconciling them requires identifying the companies, their subsidiaries and the relevant loans. S23

Each approach gives the investigation a different boundary. The named-company selection covers infrastructure, frontier AI models and chips. The sector approach follows entire business categories. Adding the figures would count some claims more than once while retaining gaps in coverage. S23

Individual banks illuminate other parts of the picture. Société Générale reports around €12 billion of exposure at default, 60% of it in data centres at the end of September. Multiplication gives approximately €7.2 billion for that activity. Exposure at default, or EaD, is a risk measure incorporating the relevant treatment of commitments, rather than simply cash already advanced. Both inputs are rounded, and data centres also serve uses beyond AI. S11

On 10 November, Barclays reported £4.5 billion of data-centre commitments, expressly distinguishing commitments from drawings. On 28 October, Deutsche Bank described less than €10 billion of AI and data-centre exposure, with less detail on the measurement convention. The answers in question do not expressly give an observation date for those balances. Reconciling the figures requires aligned dates, instruments and reporting boundaries, as discussed in the instalment on European banks. S12 S13

Geography introduces another boundary. The euro area, European banking groups and their US subsidiaries are different populations. Debt contracted in New York can expose a European group. A European sector statistic can miss the legal vehicle that borrowed the money.

Beyond banks, several useful measures

Private credit has benchmarks of its own. In a bulletin published on 7 January 2026, BIS researchers estimated more than $200 billion of outstanding direct loans to AI-related sectors, using data extending through 2025. Their PitchBook selection combined “Artificial Intelligence”, “Big Data” and “Cloud Tech”. This historical benchmark covers a private-credit segment extending beyond data centres. Its geographic breakdown remains unspecified. S15

At the level of an individual insurer, Athene reported $12.9 billion of funded digital-infrastructure investments at 30 September 2026. The measure includes ACRA minority interests on a gross basis and encompasses chip-related financing. It describes an investment portfolio with its own conventions. Reconciling it with credit-fund data would require matching the underlying assets. S16

An insurer can also provide a promise to pay. In the Big Sky arrangement examined in the fourth instalment, Athene’s holding company, AHL, guarantees a share of WHCO’s remaining purchase obligation in the event of default. A third party must reimburse part of what AHL actually pays. The exposure therefore depends on a trigger, an outstanding obligation and a right of recovery. Such a guarantee belongs in a different part of the map from a security already purchased. S17

Loan insurance opens another route. A bank can retain its loan while transferring part of the risk to an insurer or investor. In its study of synthetic risk transfers, the Basel Committee also describes banks financing protection sellers. Some risk can consequently return to the banking sector. In funded structures, the seller delivers cash or collateral covering the protection at the outset, reducing the bank’s counterparty risk. The study describes the general synthetic risk-transfer market. It supplies a mechanism to investigate in AI financing without establishing a link to this inquiry’s loans. S22

Bond markets widen the picture further. The Federal Reserve estimated more than $1.1 trillion of outstanding public-market debt related to advanced computing in October 2026. It also identifies mutual funds, pension funds and insurers as the main holders of hyperscaler and graphics processing unit (GPU) firms’ on-balance-sheet debt. That sector-level observation applies to those issuers. A detailed allocation of the broader debt stock, including its European holders, remains to be established. S21

These are measures of private loans, insurer investments and outstanding securities. They refer to different dates and populations, with possible overlaps. Estimating Europe’s ultimate exposure requires identifying the holders and the institutions financing them. Currency conversion alone leaves that work undone.

Euro bonds extend the network

An ECB analysis published online on 17 November adds a European perspective. Its authors report that large US cloud providers, or hyperscalers, account for close to 8% of the market value of euro-denominated investment-grade corporate bonds with more than ten years remaining to maturity. The chart’s latest observation is 30 June 2026. The share refers to the index’s long-maturity segment and is concentrated in a few issuers. S18

Euro area insurers and pension funds show a modest overweight in those bonds relative to their benchmark. The ECB describes the allocation changes as marginal so far. It highlights a concentration that can persist because of the debt’s maturities, alongside the links between US markets and European portfolios. S18

The mechanism can operate before a default. If expected revenues disappoint and investors demand higher compensation for credit risk, existing bond prices can fall. A portfolio holding both shares and bonds of the same companies can experience both declines. Diversification across instruments can still leave substantial exposure to the same borrowers.

Observations on government financing offer a counterpoint. In a letter dated 6 November, Christine Lagarde described smooth absorption of new issuance by euro area investors, whose sovereign bond holdings had also risen. Effects on comparable corporate issuance were small and temporary. The ECB reported no significant observed impact on government bond yields. That assessment reflects the evidence available when the letter was written. S20

Following the rights of recourse

An effective map preserves two views. The first measures financing received by the ultimate borrowers, counting each loan or security once and keeping future commitments separate. The second follows claims between financial institutions: who finances a fund, who holds a junior tranche, and who guarantees payment on which terms. That second network explains how a shock might travel.

“Off balance sheet” cuts across both views. It describes how an obligation is treated by a particular entity. A credit fund carries loans as assets; a bank may retain undrawn commitments; an SPV may be consolidated in its parent’s accounts. The consolidation perimeter therefore needs checking before a claim is classified. Assets managed for clients must also be distinguished from proprietary investments.

The bank connection can be measured beyond our individual examples. An ECB study published in May identified €62.5 billion of drawn exposures to private-credit funds, across a sample of twelve euro area banks, using 2024 data. This covers all sectors. It measures financing of the intermediaries themselves, a different layer from lending to companies in the AI supply chain. S19

The protections that slow transmission deserve equal attention. Equity absorbs the first losses, while lenders can benefit from collateral and priority in repayment. Long-term funding also reduces the risk of forced sales. The ECB’s private-credit exercise found that bank losses were broadly absorbable in the scenarios examined, subject to sample and methodological limitations. The supervisor described banks’ capital and liquidity as robust on 9 November, and the 18 November report still identifies liquidity and capital buffers as supporting their ability to absorb shocks. S19 S10 S23

A shared deterioration across several layers would be the vulnerable point. Weaker revenues could depress loan values, reduce eligible collateral and tighten funds’ financing. Contractual clauses could then retain cash or call on a guarantor. The agreements examined in this series describe potential transmission paths. Their impact would depend on revenues, collateral values and the clauses actually triggered.

The decisive records for future monitoring will be updated lender allocations, the actual pledged portfolios and the conditions attached to credit protection. CoreWeave provides a measure of the progress made: six dated portfolio positions can now be linked to one facility, and one holder leads to identifiable bank financing. The next question is the precise recourse available to each creditor when expected revenues meet payment deadlines.

Limits

This documentary investigation draws on public records rechecked on 18 November 2026. The six funds form an identified subset. The 3.7% measures this collection’s coverage of a particular loan. The matching exercise relies on issuer and fund disclosures, without interviews or private counterparty confirmation.

Fund positions are dated 30 September, while bank disclosures and institutional statistics use the periods specified in the article. Guarantees, indirect participations and risk transfers remain partly documented. Those reporting boundaries prevent a consolidated estimate of European bank and non-bank exposure, or expected losses, from being derived from these records.
