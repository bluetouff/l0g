Introduction

A building site with creditors

A data centre can owe money long before its first operating revenues arrive. Land must be prepared, equipment ordered, power arranged and construction financed. Several companies take on those tasks. Their promises meet in contracts whose timetables may differ. A building can rise while permission for its power supply remains under review. Interest can accrue before a tenant begins paying full rent. Credit gives that gap between spending and income a financial structure, allocating the cost of waiting among the parties involved.

The investigation collected here begins with Jupiter, a New Mexico campus intended for Oracle. Bloomberg’s report of a loan claim sold by HSBC and proceedings over an air permit open two separate lines of inquiry. One follows a right to future payments. The other concerns the operating conditions of infrastructure. Bringing the documents together poses a question: how do the contracts divide the consequences of delay among the owner, its customer and the institutions that lent the money? The opening chapter preserves the reporting, attribution and uncertainty surrounding each event. [B13] [B14]

A project’s commercial name takes the search only so far. Its buildings, power system and equipment may belong to different entities. A special-purpose vehicle, or SPV, carries a particular transaction. A bank may arrange a loan without retaining the entire claim. A fund may own part of it, while one of that fund’s subsidiaries borrows from banks to finance a portfolio. Tracing the links requires legal names, instruments and dates. The agreement establishes rights, while the accounts reveal holdings and funding at a particular point in time. [B01] [B04]

The six chapters follow that trail. Jupiter leads to European banks and the loan positions they disclose or leave unquantified. The investigation then moves to private lenders and insurers. It examines the contractual control of cash before bringing identified creditors together on a map. The evidence comes in different forms: fund investment schedules, credit agreement exhibits, quarterly accounts and supervisory publications. Each offers a different degree of resolution. An organiser named on a contract cover and an investor reporting funded principal supply different kinds of evidence.

The distinction between funding and protection becomes essential. A bank lending to a project company owns a claim. A guarantee adds recourse to another party under specified conditions. An insurer can buy a security, cover a loan’s non-payment or guarantee an equipment purchase obligation. Those activities can call on its resources through different routes. Athene’s accounts describe both investments and a conditional undertaking by its holding company. Understanding their place in the network requires preserving the function of each contract and the party that stands behind it. [B05] [B11]

Risk also has a timetable. An approved facility remains subject to draw conditions. A reserve directs receipts towards specified payments. A creditor may therefore gain protection at the same time as the borrower has less freely available cash. Chapter V explores these arrangements at CoreWeave and Applied Digital. The clauses describe possible sequences. Establishing a project’s actual position requires evidence of execution, together with the disclosures available at each relevant date. A reserve allocation and its current balance are separate pieces of information. [B01] [B08] [B09]

The numbers in this book consequently resist simple addition. A loan limit, outstanding principal and a fund’s holding sit at different levels. The price in a secondary sale follows another payment route, to the seller of the claim. A guarantee may protect debt already counted. When a bank finances the fund holding that debt, both relationships belong on the map, with an overlap to resolve before measuring the money reaching the project. The bank’s own claim still matters because its debtor, collateral and priority are different. [B03] [B10]

This documentary investigation aims to make those rights legible. Each chapter retains its date, evidence and limits. The final instalment incorporates the ECB’s Financial Stability Review published on November 18, 2026. Figures and an offline glossary provide a way back to the mechanisms, while reference notices point to the underlying documents. Together, the chapters offer a framework for following AI’s creditors as funds are advanced, agreements change and protections may eventually be called upon. The path runs from the promise to the party responsible for paying it.
