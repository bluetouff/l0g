Introduction

The cost of time

A model is waiting for an evaluation. A factory is trying to deliver chips at scale. A customer is comparing two services. A lender is waiting to be repaid. Each faces a different deadline. Bringing those schedules together makes it possible to ask a practical question about calls to slow artificial intelligence: who can afford to wait, on what terms, and at whose expense?

The investigation starts with the organisations that evaluate models. A safety procedure gives power to whoever chooses the tests, interprets the results or authorises the next step. Independence deserves scrutiny when commercial relationships connect the participants. The documents examined here raise questions about whether an assessment can be challenged, whether a disagreement can be published and whether competitors face comparable requirements.

Chapter two returns to incidents. What did the agent do, which permissions did it have, what environment was it operating in, and for how long? Those details shape what can be learned from an alarming account. A response in an exercise, an escape from a restricted environment and an action affecting a real system can carry different consequences. Public disclosures provide only a partial view of practices whose frequency is difficult to measure.

Competition introduces another timetable. A Chinese model may become useful to a developer before China has an autonomous industrial supply chain. Chapters three and four follow the gap between a usable service and manufacturing capacity. They examine prices, licences and migration work, then chips, memory and production constraints. Performance claims remain attributed to the organisations that publish them, with the limits of the comparisons kept visible.

Waiting also has a financial cost. Chapter five starts with Japan and the financing terms described in the cited documents to explain the value of time. A payment made today and a receipt pushed into the future occupy different positions in an investment calculation. Currency, maturity and borrower risk need to be preserved when interpreting an interest rate. Comparing different instruments without those distinctions can obscure the mechanism.

The final chapter turns to contracts. A customer commitment may expire before the loan that financed the equipment. Some repayments follow a schedule; others depend on an event specified in the agreement. A guarantee has its own conditions and scope. Reading these clauses helps identify who would bear a difficulty and when. Hypothetical examples explain the mechanics, while amounts drawn from filings retain their accounting meaning.

This book brings together the six articles published on 27 September 2026, their sources and fourteen diagrams. It examines the debate through public documents, without assigning a single motive to the actors or predicting a crisis. Reported facts, interpretation and scenarios remain distinct within the chapters. The thread connecting them is how power and costs are allocated when the technical timetable meets the economic one.

The investigations and their references are included in the six chapters of the book.
