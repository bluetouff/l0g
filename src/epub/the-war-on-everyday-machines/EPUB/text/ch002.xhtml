Chapter 1

The day supermarket freezers became a target

Published 3 November 2026

The war on ordinary machines · Part 1 of 7

Remote commands were changed, food was pulled from shelves, and another set of incidents followed three weeks later. The attacks on commercial refrigeration exposed a very tangible form of power: control over the conditions that keep goods fit for sale. Technical documentation and testimony from France’s cybersecurity agency help reconstruct the mechanism and correct the early explanations.

In Gradignan, near Bordeaux, an Intermarché operator put the food lost after the night of 11–12 October 2026 at €21,000, valued at purchase cost. He told Le Monde that a judicial officer had documented the loss. This was one shop’s assessment, reported by its operator. 1

Before the incident, the goods were inventory awaiting sale. Once their storage conditions had been compromised, their value depended on a food-safety decision and, potentially, disposal. A change made through a computer interface had reached into the assets of a business.

Refrigeration makes that transition unusually visible. An indicator can turn green again, a remote connection can be closed and a technician can complete a repair, while the fate of the affected food remains unresolved. Restoring a machine and rebuilding saleable inventory are separate jobs. This opening instalment follows the established events and the physical mechanism linking them.

Three weeks, and an explanation that changed

On 12 October, CERT-FR, the government incident-response centre within France’s national cybersecurity agency, ANSSI, issued an alert. An update on 13 October expanded the equipment covered; another on 16 October revised the recommendations. The alert names Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC families, alongside Copeland XWEB and Dixell XWEB. It records compromises of remote refrigeration controllers. 2

Le Monde also reported incidents involving Dixell equipment on the night of 12–13 October. That second night should be distinguished from the initial French incidents. 10

A controller is a specialised computer that monitors or operates equipment. A refrigeration installation can have several layers: a device controlling an individual cabinet, a supervisor collecting information across the shop, and an interface through which a maintenance contractor works. Their precise capabilities depend on the installation. The alert identifies product families; establishing the route taken at each site requires investigation. 2 5

A significant clarification arrived on 21 October. Testifying before the French Senate, ANSSI director-general Vincent Strubel said the agency’s analysis pointed to equipment directly exposed to the internet, inadequately protected administration and weak passwords. At that stage, he rejected previously published vulnerabilities from the preceding two years as the explanation for the compromises under examination. 3

His intervention changes the order of the questions. Documented software flaws certainly exist. Finding one on a machine is useful information; establishing that an attacker used it requires further evidence. Investigators need to determine how access was obtained, which permissions were available and what commands followed. The testimony records the agency’s assessment on 21 October. It leaves room for differences between individual sites and for later incidents to involve other methods.

On 2 November, refrigeration incidents were reported across several European countries. Reuters obtained confirmations in Belgium, Luxembourg, Sweden and Ireland. It said the perpetrators and any connection to the French incidents remained undetermined. 4

There are twenty-one days between 12 October and 2 November. That is an interval between reporting dates, not a measured period of attacker activity. The public chronology establishes a recurrence of a type of damage. Describing it as one operation would require links between access records, infrastructure, commands and their origins. A convenient news label such as “wave” can suggest more coherence than the evidence supports.

Method and scope

Reports and document revisions carry different dates. The 21 days from 12 October to 2 November are calendar days. Visual spacing is editorial, not a time scale. The 21 October testimony concerns the compromises examined at that point; a connection to the November incidents remains unproven.

A freezer also has a way to generate heat

To understand the empty shelves, follow the heat. Refrigeration removes energy from an enclosed volume and the goods it contains. Frost can accumulate on the evaporator, the heat exchanger that absorbs that energy. The ice obstructs heat transfer and has to be removed. Defrosting is therefore part of the normal operation of many installations. 6

Depending on the design, defrosting can use electric heaters or hot gas. Temperature and time limits ordinarily govern when it ends. A function essential to preservation thus introduces heat into part of the equipment at particular moments. Controlling that function is crucial. 6

At shop level, a supervisor can coordinate multiple controllers, collect temperatures and handle alarms. Danfoss describes these capabilities in its product documentation. We use those documents to explain one possible architecture and its legitimate functions. They do not reveal how any particular victim’s installation was built. 5

The risk emerges when an intruder obtains control permissions. A legitimate function can then be used outside its intended operating conditions. The controller executes an instruction; the consequences continue through a heat exchanger, the surrounding air and eventually the goods. That sequence explains how access to a narrowly defined technical interface can produce direct material losses.

The illustration below uses an electric-defrost example. Other installations use different methods, and local protections vary. It identifies the boundaries that an investigation should examine: who authorises the command, which device acts on it, which sensor observes the result and how an alert reaches someone able to respond. It does not reconstruct the attackers’ settings.

Remote maintenance has a clear operational purpose: detecting a fault, interpreting an alarm and preparing a repair before a technician travels to the site. The economic question concerns how much authority that connection carries. Reading a temperature, changing the target temperature and altering a configuration have different consequences. A contract describing “remote monitoring” may bundle those functions together. An investigation needs to separate them.

The tempting shortcut of a known vulnerability

In August 2025, Danfoss published a security advisory for vulnerabilities in the AK-SM 800A, remedied in version 4.3.1. The advisory describes flaws that require prior authentication. It establishes a product risk, rather than evidence that these flaws were used in October. 8

The distinction matters when reading technical alerts. A CVE identifier names a catalogued vulnerability. Connecting it to a particular incident still requires checking the model, software version, configuration and available records. Similar product names can also be misleading: the manufacturer’s documentation distinguishes the AK-SM 800 from the AK-SM 800A generation. 5 8

A separate Danfoss page describes security options on the AK-SM 800A. Software branch 4.0.x and later defaults to strict mode after installation, while retaining a compatibility option. The company warns that an upgrade may affect communications with third-party software and recommends having personnel on site where the configuration is uncertain. 9

This is where a recommendation to update becomes an operational project. The refrigeration contractor needs visibility over the equipment, the shop needs continued monitoring of its food, and the IT team needs to control access. A security change can affect all three relationships. That calls for preparation and testing. It also produces a specific documentary question: who was responsible for verifying the complete service after the change, and what evidence of that verification exists?

Supplier documentation explains product capabilities and precautions. It says much less about how individual installations were configured, maintained or covered by contracts. Responsibility for an incident depends on those local facts. Assigning all the blame to a manufacturer, contractor or shop at the outset would settle the issue before examining the records that matter.

A dramatic temperature needs a precise definition

Reports of these attacks include very high temperatures. Before repeating a number, establish what it describes: a target entered into a controller, an air-temperature measurement, or the temperature of the food itself. The first is an instruction. The second is an observation at a particular sensor location. The third concerns the goods whose safety is at issue. 6 12 13

European food law makes the distinction useful for managing the cold chain. For products within the quick-frozen category, Directive 89/108/EEC sets a reference of −18°C or below throughout the product, subject to specified tolerances. Regulation 37/2005 separately addresses air-temperature monitoring in transport and storage, with derogations including those for retail display cabinets. The rules themselves distinguish food temperature from the temperature around it. 12 13

The evidence should be read accordingly. Sensor location, calibration, exposure time and the history of a batch all matter. A screenshot stripped of that context illuminates one part of an incident while leaving much to reconstruct. Chilled products also have requirements that vary by category: the quick-frozen reference has a defined scope.

Method and scope

The diagram separates three objects of evidence, without depicting readings from an attack. The −18°C reference concerns quick-frozen foods under Directive 89/108/EEC, subject to its specified tolerances. Regulation 37/2005 addresses air monitoring in defined settings, with derogations. No storage time or decision to release goods for sale is calculated.

The distinction also prevents a misleading assumption about recovery. Bringing the air back to its normal operating temperature does not erase what happened earlier. A decision to release food for sale concerns its history and the relevant safety requirements. The EU’s food-hygiene regulation places primary responsibility for food safety on the operator and requires procedures for controlling hazards. 11

For accurate reporting, a properly contextualised reading is more useful than an isolated maximum. For customer protection, shop staff need to make decisions about batches even when the information is incomplete. They may withdraw goods as a precaution. Such a withdrawal, by itself, is not a count of items whose spoilage has been individually demonstrated.

The hourly report may smooth away the event

The documentary research exposes another issue: how temperature histories are presented. The June 2026 revision of the Danfoss AK-SM 800A manual describes a food-safety reporting function that samples at fifteen-minute intervals and displays an hourly value based on the average of four samples. It distinguishes this function from other service logs. 7

This describes a feature in that manual. Whether the affected shops had it, or had enabled it, is unknown. Its value to this investigation is methodological: an hourly average and the maximum within that hour answer different questions. Several sets of four readings can have the same average. The summary therefore loses some of the information in the underlying observations.

That arithmetic has a practical consequence for evidence gathering. “Send the temperature records” is an incomplete request. Does the file contain original readings, averages, maxima or a reformatted display? Which periods have missing data? Were the clocks synchronised? A well-presented table may still be an incomplete account of what happened.

Method and scope

Danfoss manual BC337629891709en-001101, dated June 2026, describes this feature in section 5.10, printed page 52. T1 to T4 are symbols, not invented temperatures. Their arithmetic mean is their sum divided by four. The intervals separate successive samples; no exact position within the hour is attributed to the software. A mean alone cannot recover the maximum or time above a threshold. Use of this feature at victim sites is unknown.

There is a second question: does the equipment issuing commands also retain and transmit the measurements? Supervisory documentation shows that several of these functions can be combined. Investigators should therefore consider the independence of the available evidence. This is a reason to compare records from different sources; it establishes no falsification of logs in the incidents discussed here. 5 7

A robust chronology brings together configuration changes, alarms, readings and human interventions. Each timestamp describes a different event: a command arriving, a sensor detecting a change, a message being sent, or a person acting. Treating them as interchangeable can turn a transmission delay into an apparent failure to respond, or conceal a period in which nobody had the information needed to intervene.

Bringing refrigeration back, then refilling the shelves

Ireland provides a useful comparison. On 2 November, Musgrave said that thirteen SuperValu stores had been affected and that chilled and frozen products had been withdrawn as a precaution. The company credited its monitoring systems with detecting the incident, according to its statements to the Irish Farmers Journal. 14

A second report dated the same day said Musgrave considered the incident resolved and stores operating normally, while replenishment of the affected ranges was still under way. These remain company statements reported by the newspaper. The pages reviewed do not establish the precise timing of each operational milestone. 15

The sequence separates two jobs: making the equipment usable again and restoring product availability. The latter depends on logistics and stock decisions. A declared end to an IT incident can coexist with shelves still being refilled. Measuring economic damage therefore requires a definition of recovery: control of the machine, availability of goods, restored sales or payment of the costs incurred.

The Gradignan estimate was expressed at purchase cost. Other estimates might use retail value, disposal charges, lost margin or the cost of damaged equipment. Combining these measures could count the same consequence twice. A credible aggregate would require supporting records by category, a common period and a distinction between losses, additional expenditure and insurance recoveries.

This instalment therefore keeps local amounts within their stated scope. The documents reviewed provide no consolidated European economic-loss total. The lack of a reliable aggregate leaves the reality of the incidents intact and avoids attaching artificial precision to it.

The gap between professions

Commercial refrigeration sits between building services, maintenance, IT and food hygiene. ANSSI describes industrial systems as operational technology (OT), systems that act directly on physical processes. It also notes that security measures used in office IT may require adaptation in these environments. 16

A simple organisational question follows: who has a complete view from access permission to the decision about a batch of food? The shop understands its products. The refrigeration contractor understands the controls. The IT team understands the connections it manages. The investigation needs to establish where that knowledge meets, especially when equipment has been installed or maintained under a contract separate from the rest of the IT estate.

CERT-FR’s alert calls for removing direct internet exposure and restoring protected access. It also asks operators to check the insurance terms before interrupting reporting to the refrigeration contractor. That detail is a reminder to preserve effective temperature monitoring while securing the connection. 2

The missing evidence can now be named: before-and-after configurations, original logs, batch inventories, intervention reports and maintenance contracts. These records would help establish responsibilities and explain why losses differed between sites. A common retail brand identifies a commercial network. It says much less about how uniform the technical installations are.

Attribution requires another level of evidence. Destructive acts can serve several motives. Naming a group or a state demands sufficiently strong technical and contextual indicators beyond the damage and its timing. The record available on 3 November supports an account of a loss mechanism and an organisational vulnerability. It leaves the perpetrators and the connections between episodes unresolved. 4

The freezers made a familiar dependency visible. Food inventory retains its value only while physical conditions are continuously maintained. Remote control provides influence over those conditions. Protecting access, retaining usable measurements and arranging an on-site response are consequently parts of the same task: keeping control of the goods until they are sold.

Method and limitations

Documentary investigation, evidence cutoff 3 November 2026. No third-party systems were accessed or tested, and no interviews were conducted for this article. Testimony and company statements are attributed to the outlets that obtained them. The illustrations explain mechanisms; no victim’s temperature trace was obtained. Command settings, air readings and product temperatures are treated separately. The next instalment will examine the allocation of control between supervisors, local controllers and remote maintenance.
