Introduction

The power to change a temperature

A command sent across a network can end with goods in a waste container. On the way, it passes through a controller, a piece of equipment, the surrounding air and time. Refrigeration makes that journey tangible: food retains its value only while its physical conditions are maintained. When those conditions change, a green status light still leaves people with decisions about safety, replacement stock and the return of normal trade.

In autumn 2026, France’s government incident-response centre, CERT-FR, confirmed compromises affecting several Danfoss, Copeland and Dixell refrigeration-controller families. Its alert, issued on 12 October and revised on 16 October, also asks operators to check insurance conditions before interrupting certain data feeds to their refrigeration contractor. That detail brings the contract onto the factory floor. A connection can carry maintenance access, alarms and information used to document storage conditions.

The owner then needs to establish who can act, which equipment they can reach and how long their authority lasts. Documentation from Danfoss, Siemens, HMS and IXON separates the functions involved: reading a measurement, writing a setting, transferring a configuration and administering access. These are descriptions of different products. Bringing them together helps identify decisions and dependencies; it does not establish the architecture at any affected shop.

The investigation also works backwards, from damage towards an actor. A temperature reading, a login record and a public claim provide different kinds of evidence. The public cases examined here show why identifying an operator, defining a group and establishing state involvement each require their own support. The American and Danish precedents remain separate from the European refrigeration incidents, whose connection to a named perpetrator remains unresolved in the reviewed record.

Spoiled goods then enter the accounts and the cash forecast. Purchase cost, abandoned sales, avoided expenses and replacement inventory describe different movements. Chapter five uses a wholly fictional example to make those differences visible. French insurance law helps explain deadlines, coverage questions and recovery rights; the victims’ individual policies and payments remain outside the evidence. The distinction matters to a business that needs to buy fresh stock before it knows how much money will arrive.

European rules seek to organise part of this responsibility over time. The Cyber Resilience Act addresses covered digital products and their manufacturers; NIS2 addresses risk management at organisations within its scope. An available security update still needs to reach the right machine, work with the installation and pass the checks required for operation. Application dates and support periods therefore become practical information for purchasing and maintenance.

Norsk Hydro’s experience in aluminium brings the focus to recovery work. After its 2019 attack, the company described operations maintained with more manual procedures alongside sharply different conditions across business units. That account does not quantify the losses those workarounds prevented. It directs attention to people, configurations and resources that can actually be mobilised. The final chapter develops the question through six fictional sites, where the capacity of recovery teams also determines how long each site waits.

These seven investigations follow decisions through to the service people receive. The diagrams retain their mechanisms, sources and limitations. Bibliographies lead back to the documents, while the glossary provides help when a technical term becomes useful. The subject belongs to people who make, maintain, sell and finance as much as to those who secure networks. An everyday machine can become a very practical source of power. Understanding that power makes the commitments around it open to scrutiny.
