Nvidia in Taiwan: political outreach around an employee’s trial

Part 16

Published 2026-11-16T16:56:13+01:00 · Original article

Nvidia approached Taiwanese political officials over the prosecution of one of its employees, according to a Bloomberg investigation published on 15 November. People familiar with the matter describe the company disputing the case and asking TSMC for advice. Nvidia says it provided information requested by the authorities and sought to protect its employee’s welfare. S01

Court records give this account a procedural setting. A September ruling upheld bail for four defendants, including the Nvidia employee. In October, another decision recorded the continuing trial and kept a different defendant’s car under seizure. These earlier acts help assess the new reporting without assigning the company’s undated contacts a place in the court’s reasoning. S03 S04

This sixteenth installment extends our examination of Nvidia’s board inquiry and the financial interests surrounding export controls. The Taiwanese case brings the scrutiny inside the commercial checks governing access to advanced servers.

Reported approaches and the company’s response

Bloomberg’s sources say Nvidia raised concerns with political officials after its employee’s arrest in July, objected to the charges and believed he should not have been investigated. Those officials reportedly answered that they had no role in the proceedings. The same investigation says Nvidia sought TSMC’s advice and the chipmaker declined to become involved. The officials and the precise dates of these conversations remain unidentified. S01

Asked about its engagement with TSMC and political officials, Nvidia described providing information requested by Taiwanese authorities and protecting its employee’s well-being. It expressed hope for a quick resolution. That statement supplies the company’s explanation of its engagement, while the requests and private exchanges remain described by Bloomberg’s anonymous sources.

TSMC declined to comment to the news agency. Taiwan’s Ministry of Economic Affairs did not respond. Keelung prosecutor’s office spokesperson Chen Po-wen said the office was unaware of the reported communications and that the case was proceeding in the district court. The dates of these individual responses are unknown. S01

Supporting an employee during prosecution can involve legal assistance, supplying records and contact over their welfare. Establishing the purpose and effect of a particular approach requires the exchange itself. Here, the absence of dated correspondence leaves the reported conversations separate from the documented judicial decisions.

The court record identifies the employee and the dispute

The Taiwan High Court’s 10 September 2026 ruling identifies the employee known as Chang in Bloomberg’s reporting. In its account of the prosecution’s appeal, the court identifies his workplace as the Taiwan branch of a Hong Kong Nvidia entity. Prosecutors describe him as a key person in deciding whether B300 equipment passed an on-site inspection and could be released. S03

The allegation concerns a commercial checkpoint. According to the prosecutors’ argument reproduced in the ruling, Chang concealed the buyer’s shortage of funds and the Chinese origin of its financing. The prosecution also questions the information supplied about delivery quantities and his communications with another participant. Chang denied wrongdoing. These are contested allegations within a criminal proceeding.

Nvidia tells Bloomberg that its own investigation found no indication its employee knew about the alleged diversion by people at other companies. The company’s internal conclusion and the prosecution’s account therefore address a central disputed issue: the employee’s knowledge when commercial approval was given. S01 S03

The Keelung prosecutors’ catalogue dates the announcement of charges against nine people to 24 August. An October court decision records the case reaching the district court on 26 August, under docket 115年度金重訴字第3號. Announcing the prosecution and bringing the case before the court are separate dated steps. S02 S04

Bail addressed detention while the case continued

On 26 August, Keelung District Court authorized the suspension of detention for four defendants, including Chang, against NT$4 million in bail each. It imposed an eight-month ban on leaving Taiwan by air or sea and regular reporting to local police. The prosecutors appealed, arguing that these measures were insufficient. S03

The High Court dismissed that appeal on 10 September. It recognized serious suspicions and risks of flight or collusion, then examined whether detention remained necessary. Prosecutors had completed their investigation, relevant evidence had been secured and digital material recovered. The court considered the bail and restrictions sufficient to protect the subsequent proceedings. S03

This was a decision about custody during the case. The presumption of innocence continues to apply: article 154 of Taiwan’s Code of Criminal Procedure requires evidence to establish criminal facts and presumes innocence until a final conviction. S07

The chronology supplies no demonstrated connection between Nvidia’s reported outreach and the bail decision. Its contacts have no precise dates in the reporting, while the ruling sets out the judges’ reasons for accepting the restrictions as adequate. Attribution of an additional political cause would require evidence beyond those two accounts.

A later seizure decision confirms an active proceeding

On 13 October, Keelung District Court rejected another defendant’s request for the return of a seized car in the same main case. The decision says the proceedings were continuing and keeps the vehicle available to secure a possible future recovery of criminal proceeds. S04

In recalling the indictment, the court refers to the alleged resale and export of 74 B300 servers to China. It also states that the defendant’s actual proceeds still require investigation and determination. The seizure protects a possible later financial recovery while that assessment proceeds.

That October record provides a useful limit to any account of political protection: the criminal case remained before the court, with a disputed asset still seized. Bloomberg’s November report adds the prosecutor’s statement that the proceedings were continuing. S01 S04

Commercial approval and public export controls

The High Court lists charges of breach of trust and use of knowingly false business documents among the offences alleged against the four defendants. These provisions appear in articles 342, 215 and 216 of Taiwan’s Criminal Code. The case places the accuracy of statements and the performance of professional duties at the center of the prosecution. S03 S06

A commercial inspection and a government export authorization serve different purposes. A supplier’s checks can establish a buyer’s facilities or eligibility under its sales arrangements. Public authorities decide the legal conditions for exporting goods. Approval at one level leaves the requirements at the other to be examined under the applicable rules.

In material prepared for a 2 November legislative hearing, the Ministry of Economic Affairs discusses criminal cases under existing law alongside work on further chip controls. That record describes continuing regulatory work in a setting where prosecutions are already proceeding. S05

For Nvidia’s governance, the substantive question is how its customer checks operate and how it tests the conclusions of its internal investigations. Court records, inspection documents and the company’s responses can be compared on those specific functions. The Taiwanese proceedings add a distinct case to the AI financing dossier, with its own defendants, evidence and judicial decisions.
