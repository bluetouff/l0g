Chapter 9

Nvidia: Chinese AI credit meets US export controls

Published: 2026-11-13T19:40:00+01:00 · Editorial revision: 2026-11-15T11:39:19+01:00

Original online edition

In Songyuan, northeastern China, an official investment pitch promises a computing center combining Nvidia chips with Chinese processors. Its developer proposes seeking bank loans for roughly 80% of the cost. Between that pitch and the revenue needed to repay the loans lies a step on which everything else depends: obtaining the equipment and being able to operate it. S01

Nvidia’s crisis gives that question new weight. US export controls shape access to GPUs, the processors powering large AI systems. Jensen Huang’s succession raises a separate question about the commitments the next leader will maintain. On 13 November, the Financial Times reported that Rene Haas, chief executive of Arm and SoftBank Group International, had been approached. S09 S10

This ninth instalment follows our investigation into servers declared as collateral in Shanghai. It examines a different set of Chinese projects identified by Reuters, then considers the authorizations and governance decisions that may shape their financing.

Songyuan seeks financing before the revenue arrives

A project description published by the Jilin government portal on 27 April 2026 sets out planned investment of 2.2589309 billion yuan, or approximately 2.26 billion. Songyuan Guoxin’s proposed center combines H100s with Chinese processors and offers computing and rack-rental services. S01

Its funding section envisages approximately 80% bank borrowing and 20% company or shareholder capital. The developer intends to seek long-term loans on favorable terms from commercial or policy banks. Addressed to prospective partners still invited to negotiate, the page presents the funding structure being sought. Lending agreements and disbursements are among the next records to look for. S01

A lender will have to test the plan against the shareholders’ actual contribution, equipment orders, authorizations and the strength of customers expected to pay for computing services. Cash remaining after operating expenses must cover debt payments. The debt-service coverage ratio, or DSCR, measures that repayment capacity.

An equipment delay could push revenue further out while some construction or financing expenses continue. Replacing the specified processors would also require reviewing costs, software compatibility and the performance promised to customers. The risk comes from a mismatch between repayment dates and incoming revenue.

Chinese project budgets cover different industrial stages

Reuters examined more than 150 documents and identified over 23 billion yuan in projects and equipment agreements involving Nvidia technology. Its 13 November investigation brings together planned investment, contracts and installations described as completed. The amounts include buildings, electrical systems and other infrastructure. Their total gives the scale of a set of projects at different stages of development. S02

In Gansu, the project has reached the contract-award stage. A tender dated 22 June 2026 envisages company funds and bank borrowing for a computing center specifying equipment described as “H100(200).” The final award notice of 20 July names a construction and engineering consortium with a contract value of 1.1564 billion yuan. That document identifies the contractor and award value. Examining server acceptance and financing terms will require their own records. S03

Reuters expressly separates its identified projects from Megaspeed and the allegations involving Huang. Their relevance here is economic: infrastructure, orders and repayment plans are being designed around access to controlled processors. Nvidia and China’s commerce ministry had not immediately responded to the agency at publication. S02

A computing contract can change operators before it expires

Damingcheng’s regulatory response, published on 27 June 2026, describes an H800 cluster built using company funds and bank loans. Its associated computing-services contract originally amounted to 759.296 million yuan over 85 months. Here, the amount is the value of a service agreement extending over time, with a different scope from the cost of a data center. S04

As part of an equity sale, the old agreements ended on 31 January 2026. A three-party amendment transferred Mingcheng Digital’s rights and obligations toward Shanghai Unicom to Shida Group, effective 1 February. It describes the transaction as a move toward a lighter operating model. This commercial restructuring predates the current crisis by several months. S04

Once the equipment is delivered, a lender must keep following the service contract. A transfer of operations changes the questions it needs answered: who provides the service, who collects customer payments and which company still owes the debt? Servers can stay where they are while the legal entity earning the revenue changes.

US authorizations enter the financing timetable

The restrictions predate the present crisis. In a filing published on 31 August 2022, Nvidia reported that it had been notified on 26 August of an immediately effective licensing requirement for future A100 and H100 exports to China, including Hong Kong, and Russia. Systems incorporating those processors were covered as well. S05

The regime subsequently changed. A rule published and effective on 15 January 2026 provides case-by-case review for certain exports from the United States to China and Macau, including qualifying H200 products. It requires checks involving customers, security, US market availability and independent testing in the United States. Eligibility for review precedes an individual licensing decision. Other covered transactions, including certain reexports and in-country transfers, retain a presumption of denial. S06

Chinese decisions matter too. In its 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 July 2026, Nvidia reports H200 licenses obtained from February and a portion of equipment actually shipped. The company also describes Chinese restrictions that constrained purchases. The supplier’s account identifies several steps between US permission and a completed sale. S08

Trump holds commercial leverage whose records are being sought

A separate customs decision sits alongside export licensing. Trump’s 14 January 2026 proclamation imposes a 25% duty on certain covered US chip imports, effective the following day. It provides exemptions according to use, including US data centers, research, repairs and several industrial or public purposes. The duty therefore depends on the scope and conditions of the import. S07

The proclamation also confirms that Commerce transmitted its Section 232 national-security report to the president on 22 December 2025. It makes public that date and the presidential conclusions adopted. The complete report would provide important context for reconstructing those decisions. S07

On 12 November, Ron Wyden and Richard Neal, the ranking Democrats on the relevant Senate and House committees, requested preservation of records concerning that process, Nvidia licenses and communications. They challenge the arrangement and raise a constitutional objection to a levy they characterize as export-related. Their letter initiates an inquiry and requests records preservation. S13

Our assessment: access to chips gives Washington influence that can reach project financing. Greater uncertainty over authorizations can change schedules and the protections lenders seek. Trump could try to turn that dependence into a political or industrial advantage, a scenario explored in our examination of state leverage.

The Haas report brings SoftBank’s interests into the succession

On 13 November, the Financial Times reported an approach to Haas during the search for Huang’s successor. Its account describes contact with an executive previously named as a possibility by analysts. S09

His background explains the interest. Arm says Haas has been its chief executive and a director since February 2022, after a career that included seven years at Nvidia. He also runs SoftBank Group International and sits on SoftBank Group’s board. SoftBank’s April 2026 announcement describes the expanded role as coordinating its semiconductor and AI holdings. S10

Existing financial ties to Nvidia give those roles particular weight. SoftBank is an investor in SB Energy. For the SB Energy/OpenAI project in Ohio, Nvidia agreed guarantees covering defined lease and power payments, subject to a combined $105 billion cap. They generally become effective as each lease begins. Our seventh instalment on AI guarantees examines the contractual scope of that agreement. S08 S12

A move by Haas to Nvidia would require the board to clarify his other positions and the handling of relationships with SoftBank-linked companies. Those arrangements would shape his freedom to decide on industrial agreements and financial commitments.

Nvidia’s official succession process remains open. The board accepted Huang’s resignation on 11 November, then announced the following day that Colette Kress would serve as interim chief executive while retaining finance responsibilities, with an internal and external search under chairman Harvey C. Jones. S11

Nvidia’s next leader inherits decisions that need financing

An internal candidate could maintain commercial relationships while reviewing the most sensitive commitments. An outsider would need to establish a mandate with the board and partners. The Haas report adds a specific question: how to arrange the responsibilities of an executive already at the center of SoftBank’s industrial interests.

The Chinese projects bring the succession back to its economic consequences. An installation financed against future revenue needs equipment, commissioning and creditworthy customers. Governments can influence parts of that chain. Nvidia’s next leader will have to decide how much risk the company is willing to support. Lenders and investors will examine the contracts that turn promises of computing power into enforceable obligations.
