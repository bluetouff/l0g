Introduction

A chip at the centre of the commitments

Buying a chip is only the beginning. It needs power, a building and customers willing to pay for its work. At the scale of a large data centre, those requirements set off a chain of decisions: secure an electricity connection, finance servers, reserve capacity and sell compute. Nvidia occupies several positions in that chain. It supplies the hardware, invests in selected partners and guarantees obligations that help some projects raise money. Its influence reaches far beyond processor performance. [B06] [B16]

The crisis surrounding Jensen Huang brings those connections into sharper focus. A boardroom decision can alter a lender’s confidence. An investigation into the destination of machines can raise the stakes of export licensing. A delayed power connection can push back a campus’s expected revenue and debt repayments. These links give the affair its industrial, financial and political reach. Understanding them requires attention to each party’s obligations, dates and conditions.

This book collects the twelve instalments published by l0g between November 9 and November 15, 2026. The investigation develops through reported warnings, company responses, congressional requests, board decisions, prospectuses and contracts. The opening chapters examine Nvidia’s relations with Washington and the questions surrounding Megaspeed and chip controls. The account then moves into the boardroom and out to lenders, customers and the guarantees supporting AI expansion.

Huang’s departure is a visible break in the story. Nvidia’s November 12 filing records decisions taken the previous day and places Colette Kress in interim charge. Contracts run on a longer timetable. Orders, leases and lender protections continue to have consequences after a change of leadership. Their execution will help establish the crisis’s economic scale. [B01]

Large numbers attract attention. Making sense of them takes a closer look. An announced project may still need capital. A guarantee has a beneficiary, a cap and a trigger. Equipment purchases can precede delivery, acceptance and the first receipts from customers. The chapters work through these stages, giving the reader a way to distinguish a commercial ambition from a signed obligation and an actual payment.

The final instalment offers a useful example. Korean filings confirm payment for Nvidia’s investment in Naver on November 6, before Huang’s departure. Nscale’s prospectus describes a different, additional billion dollars expected around November 16. The same group is involved in both transactions, at different dates and stages. Treating them as a single story about “Nvidia’s billions” would lose the crucial distinction between money received and money still expected. [B04] [B17] [B03]

Politics runs through these arrangements. The United States controls licences, conducts investigations and buys computing services. Chinese demand involves customers, intermediaries and infrastructure in several jurisdictions. Commercial interests meet national-security objectives at these points. Decisions can protect technology, open markets or increase partners’ dependence on Washington. Each effect needs to be traced to a specific act and its scope. [B14] [B15]

The investigations and civil actions are ongoing. Allegations, responses and accessible evidence have distinct roles in the account. Where a witness is anonymous or a contract unavailable, the text identifies the limit where it matters. References let the reader return to the underlying document and judge the reach of the argument.

The chapters retain their publication order. Questions raised early in the reporting meet later confirmation, rebuttal or further uncertainty. A glossary explains the language of credit, figures make the connections easier to follow and references accompany every chapter. The conclusion returns to the decisions still ahead on November 15. The aim is to make these interdependencies legible, so readers can follow an affair whose next stages extend well beyond the choice of a chief executive.
