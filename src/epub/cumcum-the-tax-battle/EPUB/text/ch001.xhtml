Introduction

One dividend, several claims on its value

A company pays a dividend. The income has an amount, a date and a recipient. Around that payment, however, shares can move between holders while contracts pass their economic value to someone else. Certain cum-cum arrangements use a temporary change of holder to obtain more favourable tax treatment. The investor and intermediary then share an advantage linked to French withholding tax. This investigation begins by making that transaction intelligible: following the securities, identifying the commitments and establishing who benefits from the income.

Crédit Agricole CIB and HSBC Bank plc provide specific public records against which to test that account. Their judicial agreements describe acknowledged facts and financial obligations, with a legal classification and scope particular to each case. The HSBC record requires a further distinction between companies: HSBC Bank plc entered into the agreement, while the court order records the closure of the investigation into HSBC Continental Europe. A banking brand is too broad a unit for assigning responsibility. The entity identifies where a fact, decision or payment belongs.

The documentary history also begins before the international revelations of 2018. ESMA recounts French information exchanges concerning tax matters in 2009; a Senate report documents proposed cum-cum tax adjustments from 2017. Administrations and journalists worked with different powers, evidence and forms of publication. Reading their records together helps explain how trading activity became a public issue and then a case. Some of the internal passage from information to decision remains to be established.

Moving from a trade to billions changes the scale of the evidence. France’s €33.27 billion estimate for 2000–2020 comes from the model published in 2021 by Christoph Spengel with CORRECTIV. In October 2026, the French government separately reported €4.5 billion in additional tax assessments notified after earlier adjustments. The first amount estimates historical losses; the second describes tax claims. Their periods, assumptions and collection status govern the comparisons a reader can make. Reconstructing each number gives substance to a debate otherwise driven by headline totals.

The contest continues through the wording of tax law. A statute defines a value transfer, guidance explains its treatment, and a legal guarantee limits the administration’s ability to change its interpretation of earlier transactions. A disputed paragraph published for 98 days in 2025 shows the consequences a drafting choice can carry. Its withdrawal left a question about past transactions, examined by the Conseil d’État through the principle of legal certainty. Assessing the effects requires both the relevant version of the text and the facts of the transaction.

Reform also makes a promise about results. A French procedure operating since January 2026 collects withholding first in specified cases, then allows relief when treaty entitlement is established. The EU’s FASTER framework has a principal application date in 2030. The performance of these rules will have to be judged through both the unwarranted benefits prevented and the treatment of valid claims. Initial receipts, subsequent refunds and the time taken to reach a decision belong in the same assessment.

This book brings the six investigations together around the people and institutions behind those records. Investors receive income, intermediaries arrange transactions and the public expects the tax legally due. The figures explain the mechanisms, and the source notes open a route back to the documents. Their limits remain part of the account. Understanding those boundaries gives readers a way to examine a subject usually left to specialists, challenge public choices and ask for a statement of results whose parts can be checked.
