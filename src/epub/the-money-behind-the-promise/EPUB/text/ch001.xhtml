Introduction

Following the money through to payment

An annuity turns a contract into money that someone will depend on for years. Behind each payment sit an insurance company, investments, agreements between businesses and decisions about how the money is managed. An acquisition can change that financial organisation while the recipient continues to receive the expected amount. This book follows those changes from a practical starting point: identifying the company that owes the benefit and the resources available to meet it.

Apollo, KKR, Brookfield and Blackstone give the investigation its cast. The documents around Athene, Global Atlantic, Just, American Equity and Corebridge reveal different combinations of ownership and investment management. These are groups of asset managers, holding companies and investment vehicles. Following them requires precision about the part each legal entity plays. The insurer owes the benefit, the manager invests within its mandate, and the shareholder receives the earnings attached to its ownership rights.

The investigation opens with pensions transferred by AT&T to Athene insurers. It then turns to acquisition agreements. Buying shares gives an owner influence over decisions and a claim on insurance earnings, together with exposure to losses and capital needs. Purchase prices, assets under management and management fees describe different parts of that relationship. The period for which savings remain invested becomes valuable to the businesses arranging their deployment.

The treaty between Jackson and Athene Life Re takes the reader inside reinsurance. Jackson keeps the investments in the funds-withheld account; the agreement allocates their economic return to the reinsurer, while Apollo manages them. Later chapters follow investments in related businesses and the division of investment income. A loan, an interest in a fund and a management fee confer different rights. Their prices and terms help determine how much value remains available to the insurance business.

Prudential regulation supplies another way to measure the promise. Its rules determine how obligations are valued and the resources required to withstand risk. The book follows those calculations into a British exercise modelling the return of reinsured liabilities. It traces collateral through to its use: ownership of an asset, regulatory eligibility and the ability to generate cash all contribute to meeting payments when they fall due.

The final chapter returns to the policyholder. The French Linxea Spirit 2 example shows how investment options and charges enter the definition of a capital guarantee. Reading it alongside a British lifetime annuity and American savings annuities brings the purpose of each product into focus. Policyholder protection schemes are examined through their eligibility requirements, measurement bases and procedures, keeping the contractual benefit in view.

There are potential gains to examine throughout this account: access to investments, additional capital, specialist expertise and closer matching of incoming and outgoing payments. Assessing those gains requires carrying costs and risks through the same analysis. The eight investigations collected here offer the documents, calculations and distinctions needed for that task, alongside the evidence still required to take it further. The aim is a more exact account of the financial relationships supporting a payment that matters to someone.
