Chapter 6

The regulatory price of a promise

Published: 2026-10-23T19:50:41+02:00 · Updated: 2026-10-23T19:50:41+02:00

When investment firms buy insurers · Article 6/8 · Documentary research current to 23 October 2026

An annuity still has to fund the same years of retirement, yet the resources required to support it depend on investment ratings, the reinsurance contract and the rules of the country where the liabilities are held. From the British proposal to new US risk factors, public documents trace how that treatment changes and the information needed to measure a capital saving.

Following the division of returns between policyholders, distributors and asset managers, this sixth investigation follows the prudential treatment of insurance promises.

On an insurer’s balance sheet, twenty years of payments to a retiree begin with an immediate question: what value of assets must the company hold today to meet those payments? The answer depends on when benefits fall due, the income expected from investments and the losses the business must be able to withstand. Some assets cover the value assigned to its obligations; own funds absorb adverse deviations. Together, they provide the financial backing for the promise. 1

On 29 April 2026, the UK’s prudential regulator put striking figures on the importance of the calculation. It estimated that an average funded reinsurance transaction currently involves valuation and capital charges equivalent to 2–4% of the underlying annuity liabilities, compared with 11–15% for economically similar investments. Its proposal would bring the first measure to around 10%. Those figures combine a valuation adjustment, solvency capital and the risk margin. Treating them as three directly interchangeable capital rates would obscure the very mechanism the PRA wants to change. 2, 3

That mechanism matters to groups combining asset management and insurance. Their organisation gives them influence over investment selection, financing and, where they also own a reinsurer, the entity carrying part of the risk. The BIS describes how these choices can combine better investment management with the effects of different reserving and capital rules. Establishing how much each contributes means following the calculation all the way through. 1

An investment becomes a claim on a reinsurer

Funded reinsurance links a portfolio of assets to the payments the reinsurer undertakes to make while bearing the risks transferred to it. Depending on the arrangement, those assets may be transferred or remain with the insurer ceding the risks, as in funds-withheld structures. The insurer that contracted with the retiree retains its responsibility to that person. On its UK prudential balance sheet, it records a claim on the reinsurer representing the amounts it expects to recover. The investments still matter, but the exposure also takes the form of another company’s promise. 1, 2, 4

The current treatment already includes a safeguard. The value of that claim is reduced to allow for expected losses if the reinsurer defaults. This is the counterparty default adjustment, or CDA. The PRA nevertheless considers its calibration too favourable compared with the treatment of similar direct investments. It proposes anchoring the adjustment to an existing reference: the fundamental spread for financial corporate bonds, selected according to credit quality and the timing of expected payments. 2

The immediate effect works through the asset’s value. When a claim is worth less on the balance sheet while liabilities remain unchanged, the excess of assets available as own funds shrinks. The solvency capital requirement (SCR) measures the capital needed to absorb a shock at a separate stage of the calculation. A rule can therefore make a transaction less attractive by reducing recognised own funds even while leaving the SCR rules in place. The British proposal follows this route; it would also affect how the CDA changes under stress. 2, 5

The timetable also matters. The consultation closed on 31 July 2026. It envisages applying the changes to calculations from 1 July 2027, while excluding treaties under which all the covered risks had been fully transferred by 30 September 2026. Our checks as of 23 October identified no final decision on the proposal. The official SS5/24 register still lists the October 2025 edition as current. September therefore defines the proposed boundary between transactions; July 2027 remains a proposed implementation date. 2, 6

Protection begins in the contract

To assign credit quality to the claim, the proposal starts with the reinsurer’s insurer financial strength rating, or IFS. This assesses its ability to meet insurance obligations, taking account of its balance sheet, diversification and regulatory environment, among other factors. It serves a different purpose from the rating of a loan held in the investment portfolio. The country of establishment therefore enters the assessment of the counterparty before the assets pledged as collateral are examined. 2

The PRA proposes up to three independent one-notch upgrades, depending on protections written into the treaty. To qualify for any of them, the insurer must first be able to obtain timely control of the collateral after a default, with identifiable, segregated assets protected from the competing claims specified in the rules. Securities issued by the reinsurer or its affiliates fail this eligibility condition: collateral whose value depends on the same business as the claim provides weak protection against that business failing. 2, 7

The three tests make the value of a contract tangible. One concerns required collateral at least equal to the premium, with subsequent changes constrained. Another concerns matching payments in the same currency and asset eligibility under the cedant’s UK matching adjustment permissions. The third rewards collateral whose weighted average credit quality exceeds that of the reinsurer. These are conditional improvements in the credit quality used for the prudential calculation. 7

The decisive detail is the portfolio to which the latter two tests apply. Under the proposal, firms would consider the worst portfolio permitted by the contract, in terms of credit quality and cash-flow mismatches. The reinsurer may have rights to substitute assets. A snapshot of collateral deposited at inception can then tell less than the terms governing what may replace it. A right to recover collateral becomes much more useful when the treaty constrains its composition throughout the arrangement. 7, 5

Existing supervisory expectations already address this exposure. SS5/24 asks firms to account for deterioration in the counterparty’s credit quality in the stressed CDA and to assess the consequences of recapturing liabilities. The 2026 debate concerns consistent valuation and incentives within a framework that already requires firms to model downgrades and recapture. 8

The rating meets the structure

In the United States, a document adopted during the summer of 2026 reveals another connection between ratings and capital. For year-end 2026, the NAIC introduced specific factors for the CLOs and related instruments covered by its table. CLOs pool loans and allocate their income and losses among tranches with different seniority. The protection afforded to a tranche depends on its position and the losses absorbed ahead of it. The published minutes record the Executive Committee and Plenary vote on 14 August 2026; the tracking page lists the change as adopted. 9, 10, 11

In that table, designation 1.A, associated with AAA, carries a pre-tax factor of 0.158% for bonds other than CLOs/CBOs/CDOs, compared with 0.040% in the CLO/CBO/CDO column. At designation 3.B, associated with BB, the corresponding figures are 4.537% and 25.150%. Applied to a hypothetical book or adjusted carrying value of $100 million, these produce $158,000 and $40,000 in the first case, and $4.537 million and $25.150 million in the second. The arithmetic isolates an asset risk factor before the other stages of the US risk-based capital (RBC) calculation. 9, 12

The revealing feature is that the differences point in opposite directions. The rule recognises the protection available to some senior tranches while requiring more for other parts of the structure. A claim that securitisation always reduces capital would miss this distinction. Understanding the factor requires the rating to be read alongside the claim’s seniority and the way losses reach it. 9

A second parameter makes the financial contract more tangible still: tranche thickness. For a BBB− tranche, designated 2.C, of a broadly syndicated loan CLO that meets the surcharge conditions, current thickness of no more than 4% of the underlying portfolio adds 11.77 percentage points to the 3.28% base factor. The result is 15.05%, or $15.05 million on our hypothetical $100 million holding. Thickness measures the slice of portfolio losses between the point where the tranche begins absorbing losses and the point where it is exhausted. It is distinct from the share of that tranche purchased by the insurer. 9

Several steps remain before arriving at the company’s own-funds requirement: portfolio adjustments, tax treatment, aggregation across risks and the regulatory intervention level. Multiplication by $100 million makes one factor legible. Measuring a capital raise, distributable funds or a realised saving requires the insurer’s full calculation. The adopted document sets the CLO portfolio adjustment factor at 1 for 2026, while providing for a review of the method for 2027 and subsequent years. 9

The quality of the rating remains crucial. In a working paper published on 1 September 2026, Alejandro Drexler and Andy Polacek examine private letter ratings, communicated privately, and their use following the US reform of capital categories. At comparable ratings, their sample contains more securities classified as NAIC6 among privately rated bonds. This category, covering ratings from CC to D, serves as their default proxy and also includes financial distress. They also find that some of the yield difference reflects lower liquidity. Their results call for a distinction between repayment capacity and the cost of selling an asset: yields compensate investors for both risks, which remain separate. The finding is a statistical relationship, rather than a determination that every privately rated security has been misrated. 13

In Bermuda, follow the payments through time

The country of establishment can change another part of the equation: the value assigned today to future benefits. In Bermuda, the scenario-based approach, or SBA, allows the asset requirement to be calculated, subject to conditions, by projecting how assets meet payments as they fall due. The rules examine matching, contractual options and currency risks; the method and material changes to it are subject to prudential oversight. Portfolio returns enter a detailed projection of cash flows. 14, 15

The consolidated rules use nine interest-rate scenarios: a base path and eight shocks. Within each scenario, asset cash flows, including repayments, are set against benefit payments through time. A cash-flow shortfall leads to simulated asset sales at the conditions prevailing in that scenario; a surplus is reinvested under the applicable rules. The best estimate liability is the highest initial asset requirement across the scenarios. The risk margin and capital requirement remain further parts of the calculation. 15

Asset-liability management (ALM) seeks this alignment of payments. Better-matched cash flows can reduce the need to sell in unfavourable conditions. That is an economic benefit a method attentive to timing can recognise. Its measurement nevertheless depends on credit and reinvestment assumptions. The Bermudian handbook requires projected asset cash flows to be reduced for default and downgrade costs. For private or structured assets without a directly published calibration, the insurer’s assumptions are subject to credit floors; for a securitisation, the relevant floor applies to the tranche. 14

The regulatory transition illustrates how much the scope of a percentage matters. For business already in force on 31 December 2023, the credit downgrade cost included in these projections rises in stages: 20% of the ultimate cost in 2024, 40% in 2025, 60% in 2026, then 80% and 100% in 2027 and 2028. The insurer may also choose to apply the full cost immediately. Default costs apply in full, as do downgrade costs for new business after 2023. The 60% figure therefore concerns a loss assumption in the cash flows of an older stock of business. Translating it into a reduction in total capital would change the measure midway through the calculation. 14

A notice issued shortly before this article’s research cutoff provides a useful date check. In the notice dated 21 October 2026, the BMA states that its new consolidation of the rules restates existing standards without changing them. The download path carries a 20 October date; that publication difference does not turn the consolidation into a reform. 16

Nine valuations, and the comparisons still to build

Can the advantage of a jurisdiction be measured at a glance? The IAIS attempted an exercise more carefully controlled than a comparison of headline solvency ratios. Nine volunteer insurers and reinsurers in several jurisdictions were given hypothetical US dollar annuity cash flows to value as of 31 December 2023. They chose portfolios and certain assumptions within their own frameworks. In the report published in November 2025, technical provisions ranged from $52 million to $60 million, prescribed required assets from $53 million to $68 million, and target assets, including the additional buffer selected, from $54 million to $74 million. 17

Method and scope

IAIS exercise published in November 2025, valuations at 31 December 2023, nine volunteer participants and a shared hypothetical dollar cash-flow profile. Participants choose portfolios and some assumptions. Endpoints are not paired across measures; neither individual capital buffers nor jurisdictional rankings are inferred. The $15m range width is calculated as 68 − 53.

The dispersion shows the financial importance of the calculation. It also combines several causes: regulation, the returns on the assets selected, portfolio construction and modelling. The IAIS highlights differences between participants within the same jurisdiction, anonymises the results and rules out using them to rank countries by prudence. Nor can the published endpoints be used to subtract the lowest technical provisions from the lowest required assets and recover a company’s capital requirement. Those observations may come from different participants. 17

The same reasoning applies to the public reports of groups examined in this series. Athene’s 2025 Bermuda reports and Global Atlantic Re’s report describe their methods and disclose measures of provisions, eligible resources and required capital. They allow each entity’s balance sheet to be read on its own terms. The documents reviewed do not provide a counterfactual valuation of the same portfolio under another regime that would isolate the effect of domicile. A higher ratio, or a change in own funds, still leaves the underlying contributions unresolved. 18, 19

The BMA itself provides a method for establishing them. Its notice of 2 April 2025 asks for a reconciliation, for the relevant reinsurance blocks, between assets needed by the cedant and by the Bermudian reinsurer. This must distinguish the effects of book and market values, liability assumptions, discounting, the SBA and capital, with explicit target ratios. A change in asset allocation must appear separately. The reconciliation sets out the information needed to attribute relief to its cause; the public reports reviewed do not provide it transaction by transaction. 20

Capital savings can support a real service

The counterargument deserves to be followed through. An insurer expecting regular payments far into the future can make productive use of assets that are difficult to sell, provided their cash flows arrive at the right time. A reinsurer can also bring diversification, additional capital and investment selection capabilities. The PRA recognises these potential benefits; the contractual protections it proposes to reward give them a place in its reform. 8, 21

Research by Ralf Meisenzahl, Jackson Overpeck and Andy Polacek, revised on 27 April 2026 and published by the Chicago Fed, provides empirical support for that possibility. The authors link certain private investments to closer maturity and cash-flow matching for US indexed annuities, and to the growing market share of fund-owned insurers in that business. The finding applies to the products and data studied. It supports the existence of an economic service without measuring how the benefit is shared or establishing the strength of each reinsurance treaty. 22

For an investigation into a particular group, the decisive document would therefore reconcile the same block before and after cession, identifying the investments, rights over collateral and valuation assumptions. It would help separate the contribution of better-matched payments, actual risk transfer and a change in prudential treatment. Public rules and tables show where those effects enter the calculation; attributing financial amounts to them requires transaction data. 20, 17

For the retiree, the promise remains a stream of payments to be received. Its protection depends on the companies’ ability to fund them through adverse scenarios, and on the insurer’s rights when its partner stops fulfilling its part of the contract. The next stage of this investigation will follow those liabilities back: the assets recovered must then find their place in a balance sheet and a schedule of benefit payments. 8
