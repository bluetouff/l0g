Chapter 1

Encryption: France’s government moves to scrap the safeguard against backdoors

Published 7 November 2026

The economic cost of a backdoor · Part 1 of 7

Documentary investigation current to 7 November 2026. The parliamentary debate remains open as of that date.

France’s cybersecurity bill contains a proposed safeguard against compulsory measures that weaken encryption. The government wants to remove it, arguing that it could hamper criminal investigations and intelligence work. Behind the dispute over wording lies a practical economic question: what security will a business be able to buy, and who will bear the risk created by an additional means of access? S01S02

A boundary inside a security bill

The debate is scheduled to begin at 4 p.m. on Monday, 9 November 2026. France’s National Assembly is due to consider the bill on critical infrastructure resilience and stronger cybersecurity. The government has requested priority consideration of Article 16 bis on Tuesday the 10th, after questions to the government. At this investigation’s cut-off date, those are announced arrangements for a debate still to come. S04S08

This short article occupies an unusual place in the bill. The surrounding provisions organise incident prevention, supervision and institutional responsibilities. Article 16 bis draws a boundary: how far may public authorities require a supplier to alter the protections it provides to users? S01

Its wording addresses technical devices intended to deliberately weaken the security of systems and communications. It names master decryption keys, then extends the prohibition to any other mechanism or process enabling access to protected data without consent. It applies to encryption service providers, including qualified trust service providers. S01

In this debate, a master key means a means of decryption with a reach beyond that of an ordinary user. Yet the term is convenient shorthand for very different designs. Additional access could also involve a recovery function, an extra recipient or a software change. Its consequences would depend on how it worked and which data it could actually reach. S17S21

The government proposes deleting the entire article. The operative text of amendment 358 is just three words in French: “Supprimer cet article.” Its explanatory statement defends the capabilities of criminal investigators and intelligence services. It argues that a prohibition framed around all access without consent could undermine duties to cooperate that already exist in French law. S02

The amendment removes a proposed protection from a bill. Existing access powers arise under other provisions; deleting this safeguard specifies no additional decryption architecture. Assessing any future order would still require identifying its legal basis, the parties bound by it and the limits on its use. S01S02S10

The Senate vote and the committee’s position

The safeguard originated in the Senate on 12 March 2025, when senators adopted Olivier Cadic’s amendment. Official recorded division 227 lists 181 votes in favour and 134 against: 315 votes for or against, out of 334 senators taking part. The date appears in the official proceedings. S05

On 9 September 2025, the Assembly committee adopted Philippe Latombe’s amendment CS358, adding the words meaning “or process”. The text filed the following day incorporated that wording. The small expansion helps explain the disagreement: does the protection cover only a weakness introduced into a system, or could it also be invoked against an investigative procedure? This 2025 committee amendment is distinct from the government’s amendment 358 of 2026. S01S05S07

On 14 October 2026, the special committee endorsed six amendments proposing deletion, including those from the government and the lead rapporteur, Éric Bothorel. It acted under Article 88 of the Assembly’s Rules of Procedure, which governs this stage of preparing amendments for consideration in the chamber. S03S06

The decision clearly indicates the committee’s political position. The legislative decision still belongs to the full chamber. As of 7 November, the reference text remains the committee bill containing Article 16 bis; amendment 358 is marked as awaiting debate. Reporting that MPs had already removed the protection would be premature. Reporting that they had already imposed backdoors would go further still. S01S02S03

The bar proportionally divides the 334 voters in the Senate division of 12 March 2025: 181 for, 134 against and 19 abstentions. Votes for or against total 315. The timeline brings together separate procedural stages; spacing is schematic. The committee’s opinion under procedural Article 88 precedes a floor decision. An earlier dispute over the drug-trafficking bill explains some of the tension. In 2025, a separate provision, Article 8 ter, addressed obligations to provide access to messaging content. It was deleted in committee on 6 March; an attempt to restore it was rejected in the chamber on 20 March. That legislative history belongs to another bill. The comparison shows the continuity of the concerns, provided the proposed investigative power remains distinct from the safeguard now under discussion. S30

The government’s argument and the wording

Investigations often proceed without the subject’s consent. Wording that made consent the dividing line for all access to data could therefore conflict with legally authorised investigative acts. This is the executive’s strongest argument: the end of the sentence may reach further than its stated security objective. S02

The whole sentence nevertheless matters. Its opening addresses the incorporation of technical devices intended to weaken security deliberately. That condition supports another reading: the prohibition could concern changes that make a system vulnerable, while investigative powers continue to apply to data and capabilities already available. The disagreement turns on how these parts of the sentence interact. No judicial ruling resolving that question for this provision was found in the material examined. S01S02

The government also raises the risk of a cavalier législatif: a provision insufficiently connected to the original bill under France’s constitutional rules for parliamentary procedure. That is an admissibility and constitutional objection advanced by the amendment’s author. Any eventual review by the Constitutional Council would be separate from the technical assessment of a backdoor. S02

To clarify the disagreement, the debate needs to specify what a provider would be compelled to do. Requiring an operator to disclose data it holds, assist with an interception it can perform, or rebuild its service to make content accessible involves different acts. Useful drafting should make clear which act is required. S10S12S13

Deleting the article resolves the drafting dispute by removing the rule. Clarification would instead seek to define the safeguard and its relationship with existing powers. Parliament must choose between those approaches. To assess the choice, it needs verifiable examples of lawful acts that would actually be obstructed, and of technical changes that would be permitted in practice, each with its legal basis. That demand for specificity guides this investigation. S01S02

A legal order meets a technical design

Consider a business conversation protected by end-to-end encryption. The sender’s phone transforms the message into unreadable data for transmission. The recipient’s phone makes it readable on arrival. In this model, the means of reading the content remain at the endpoints; the provider carries the messages between them. Security also depends on authenticating the participants, protecting their devices and implementing the service correctly. S16S19S21

Several investigative avenues may remain. A provider might hold account data, timestamps or some routing information, depending on the service. These metadata describe aspects of the exchange. Their availability and detail vary. The label “end-to-end encrypted” alone does not tell an investigator which metadata exist or how much they reveal. S21S27

Content may also be obtained from a device, where its user reads or creates it. Subject to its own conditions and oversight, France’s Code of Criminal Procedure allows the capture of computer data, including data displayed on a screen or entered by a user. Such an operation acts at the endpoint. Recovering readable content there concerns the security of the endpoint; the encryption protecting the message between endpoints may still be functioning as designed. The operation’s technical reach must be assessed by examining the tool actually used. S12

A third situation arises when a person or provider has a means of decryption. Within its scope, Article L. 871-1 of the Internal Security Code requires cryptology service providers to supply the decryption information for data transformed using their services. It also allows them to be required to perform the decryption themselves, unless they demonstrate that they cannot comply with that request. Article 434-15-2 of the Criminal Code separately concerns refusal to disclose or apply secret decryption information known to the person concerned. The inability exception in the first provision relates to the provider being required to perform the decryption. S10S11

An authority might also seek a capability that the provider does not have. Something would then have to change: key distribution, the software, the list of recipients or another part of the architecture. That transformation raises the issue of exceptional access. The new capability would become a feature that has to be operated, secured and maintained. An authorisation defines when it may lawfully be used; the design determines how it can fail. This requires a separate assessment from requests for material that is already accessible. S17

Backups and linked devices complicate the picture further. A conversation may be well protected in transit yet accessible through a copy or an associated device. CISA’s mobile communications guidance, updated on 24 November 2025, accordingly recommends securing private messaging settings and practices. An assessment must follow the data through its actual journey. S19

Method and scope

A simplified functional model of end-to-end encryption. Content keys remain at the endpoints in the model shown. Copies, backups, linked devices and authentication require separate assessment. A key already available to a holder belongs to a different situation from the end-to-end model shown. Modifying an architecture to create additional access would require its own assessment. The three access routes below are alternatives, subject to their own legal and technical conditions.

One target can rely on a component shared by many

The word targeted first describes the authorisation: a person, an account, a period and a purpose. That is an essential legal safeguard. The extent of the technical risk depends on another question: what access would become possible if the mechanism were misused or compromised? S17S21

Imagine, for illustration, a service retaining an additional means of recovering keys for several accounts. An authorisation might concern only one account. The component carrying out the request must still protect all the others. It has to verify the requesting authority, authenticate its operator, restrict permissions, keep records, resist insider abuse and handle its own errors. Sharing that capability creates a common dependency. S17S23

The 2015 research report Keys Under Doormats examines this difficulty. Exceptional-access requirements add complexity, can undermine certain security properties and create targets concentrating sensitive information. The report is a technical risk analysis published before the French bill. It estimates neither the probability of a future intrusion in France nor its cost. S17

Capturing data from a device carries risks of its own. A tool used against a particular target may exploit a weakness present in many other devices. Its development, custody and deployment also require controls. Lawful Hacking, published in 2014, considers the implications of purchasing vulnerabilities and the possible misuse of the tools. The relevant comparison concerns the function added to a service, its potential for reuse and the dependencies it creates. S22

The European Court of Human Rights addressed the issue in Podchasov v. Russia, decided on 13 February 2024. In the Russian framework before it, the Court considered the consequences for all users of weakening end-to-end encryption and found a violation of Article 8. The judgment concerned that system for retaining and accessing communications, with its inadequate safeguards. Applying its reasoning to a French measure would require examining the particular measure and the controls surrounding it. S24

Method and scope

Conceptual model of an additional access capability relying on a shared component. The four account symbols aid explanation only: they measure neither victims, exposed accounts nor attack frequency. Authorisation targets one account, while the reach of a possible compromise depends on architecture and controls. No design is attributed to the French bill. Endpoint capture can also rely on a weakness present on multiple devices.

The American warning concerns infrastructure

Espionage against US telecommunications provides a concrete example of exposure. On 13 November 2024, the FBI and CISA announced that a campaign linked to the People’s Republic of China had compromised several telecommunications companies. Their statement described stolen call records, compromised private communications involving a limited number of targeted individuals, and copies of information subject to US law enforcement requests under court orders. Attribution to China is that of the US authorities. S18

These findings show the strategic value of networks and the surveillance-related information they handle. They establish that infrastructure and data were compromised. The statement does not demonstrate that attackers broke an end-to-end encryption protocol, or that the entire operation depended on a single lawful-access backdoor. S18

The security response is instructive. CISA recommends consistent use of end-to-end encrypted communications, with particular attention to people who face intensive targeting. Its initial mobile guidance appeared on 18 December 2024; the version dated 24 November 2025 strengthens precautions around private messaging. A public authority responsible for protecting infrastructure thus treats encryption as a way to reduce exposure. S19

The US case warrants examining concentrated access and the consequences of intrusion. It leaves the assessment of any future French mechanism open: that mechanism’s architecture would need to be described before a comparison could be made. The economic lesson concerns a dependency. An organisation can bear the consequences of a weakness at the intermediary carrying or processing its information. S18S21

The bill starts at the design stage

The first expense arises when an additional function must be built and maintained. Permissions have to be defined, procedures developed, failure conditions tested and secrets protected. The ability to change keys must be preserved. NIST’s key-management guidance covers this entire life cycle. Any added access capability has to be assessed within that wider system. S23

People and procedures are needed alongside the technology. Who receives a request? Who checks its legal basis and scope? Who has the necessary permissions? How is an account error handled, access revoked, or compliance with an authorisation demonstrated? These questions connect engineering with governance. As early as 2000, the IETF’s RFC 2804 highlighted the extra complexity of interception functions and the need to protect access to those functions. S21

Some costs might be reimbursed under the applicable legal framework. Article L. 871-1 leaves the conditions for state reimbursement of implementing its obligation to a decree. For the assistance systems covered by its own framework, Article D. 98-7 of the Postal and Electronic Communications Code provides for fair remuneration covering design, deployment, maintenance and the processing of requests, subject to its approval and funding arrangements. Reimbursement allocates the bill between the provider and the public budget. Commercial losses or damage associated with a different system would require a separate assessment. S10S13

The customer relationship follows. A business buys a service commitment, assurances and an allocation of responsibilities. If its supplier’s architecture or legal constraints change, it may need to revisit contracts, audits and the service’s suitability for the data entrusted to it. This is a possible economic mechanism; its significance depends on the imposed change and existing commitments. European requirements for managing supplier risk and securing data give that review a concrete basis. S14S25

Imagine an engineering consultancy exchanging an industrial design with a client. Its choice of messaging service depends partly on who can read the plans. If a new function lets a third party obtain the content, the team needs to reassess that assurance, identify the exchanges affected and decide whether to keep using the service. Audit time, a possible migration and staff training all require work, even without an intrusion. The review might conclude that the service remains suitable. It might also identify a gap between the new capability and the assurances on which the original choice depended. This hypothetical example illustrates how a technical change can feed through to a purchasing decision. The actual expense would depend on the added function and the alternatives available.

An incident would shift the spending again: investigation, containment, withdrawal of access, restoration of protections and compliance with applicable notification duties. Customers might have work of their own to carry out. The NIS2 Directive specifically includes incident handling, business continuity and supply-chain security among its risk-management measures. S14

The most lasting consequence could involve information already copied. Revoking a key or closing an access route may protect future exchanges. An intelligible copy obtained earlier remains usable by whoever holds it. For a business, the harm then depends on the content. A manufacturing secret, a negotiation, a customer file or material enabling fraud has different uses and a different period of value. These are loss scenarios to investigate case by case. S23S27

No defensible aggregate figure follows automatically from this chain. It would require a specified mechanism, implementation costs, the number of affected systems, the reach of a compromise and the losses avoided through investigations. A cost comparison must therefore begin with a consistent unit of analysis: a particular obligation applied to a defined set of services. Otherwise, apparently comparable figures may describe fundamentally different changes. A published average cost of cyberattacks would combine incidents with causes and scopes too different to measure the marginal effect of a backdoor. This investigation therefore retains the documented mechanisms and leaves the total unresolved.

Economic protection belongs within the security mandate

The NIS2 Directive requires covered entities to have policies and procedures on the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption. Recital 98 encourages end-to-end encryption and calls for it to be reconciled with national security and investigative powers, in accordance with EU law. It also sets an explicit limit: this should not weaken end-to-end encryption. A recital helps interpret legislation; the operative duties must be read in the articles themselves. S14

That relationship calls for an assessment of specific measures. The European provisions preserve national competences and exclude certain activities. Describing NIS2 as a blanket ban on interception would go beyond the text. Treating cryptographic security as outside its purpose would overlook one of its express requirements. S14

For a business using a service, these demands can meet in a single procurement decision. The business must reduce exposure, understand its suppliers and protect its data, while its chosen provider may face duties to assist the authorities. Whether the obligations fit together depends on the precise architecture, assurances and controls. A service marketed as “sovereign” reveals little about those properties through that label alone.

An access capability could also provide a public benefit if it enabled investigators to obtain useful evidence in serious cases. Assessing that benefit would require knowing when it actually changes an investigation’s outcome, what alternatives are available and whether targets can shift their activities elsewhere. Europol and ENISA’s joint statement in 2016 acknowledged both the difficulty for investigations and the importance of strong encryption. It called for solutions that preserve collective security. S16

Counts of requests or interceptions describe activity. On their own, they do not measure the benefit of a new obligation imposed on an encrypted service. Nor does an additional technical capability guarantee effective use against every target. The analysis needs to compare defined mechanisms, each with its own benefits and risks. S21

Parliament needs to identify the capability being sought

The debate announced for November should show whether the Assembly keeps, changes or removes the protection in Article 16 bis. The bill’s subsequent passage between the two chambers and the final enacted text will then need to be followed. As of 7 November 2026, the outcome remains open. S01S02S04S08

The available documents support a more precise conclusion about the underlying question. A duty to assist must be tied to particular data, a capability actually held or an explicitly described technical change. That is the starting point for examining the reach of access, the dependencies it creates and how its economic consequences would be distributed. S10S17S21

The useful question for the chamber is therefore straightforward: what additional capability would have to be built, and which users would depend on it? A documented answer would allow the expected investigative gain to be weighed against the work and risk imposed on others. That is where the economic cost of a backdoor begins to become observable.

Reading guide

End-to-end encryption. Protection of content between the endpoints of a communication. Device security, copies and the identity of the participants remain critical. S16S19

Decryption information. The French legal expression convention de déchiffrement refers to the information needed to make encrypted data readable. Its availability and the ability to use it must be considered under the specific conditions of each legal provision. S10S11

Exceptional access and backdoors. A capability designed to let an authorised third party access otherwise protected data. Its architecture and risks vary; “backdoor” here refers to mechanisms that would weaken that protection. S17

Externality. A consequence of one party’s decision borne by others. In this analysis, the hypothesis to examine is that some technical risk is transferred to customers and to the people whose data pass through the service.

Method and limitations

This investigation brings together French parliamentary documents, legislation and primary technical publications consulted on 7 November 2026. Older sources are used for the mechanisms they describe, with their dates stated. Parliamentary information reflects the position documented at that cut-off. The graphics distinguish recorded votes, scheduled stages and conceptual models.

The material establishes public wording and positions. It provides neither access to secret operational systems nor a causal estimate of the investigative benefit or economic cost of exceptional access in France. The proposed cost chains are explicit inferences. US officials’ statements about the origin of the espionage campaign are attributed to those authorities. No interviews, penetration tests or encryption experiments are claimed.

One documentary discrepancy warrants a reservation. The page for amendment 358 is dated 3 October 2026, while its explanatory statement refers to activity during October 2026. The reviewed sources contain no public clarification of that mismatch. The volumes given in that passage are therefore excluded from the analysis. S02
