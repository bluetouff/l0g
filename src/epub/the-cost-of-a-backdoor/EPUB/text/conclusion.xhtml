Decisions backed by evidence and resources

The power to read a message is built through decisions reaching beyond the message itself. It depends on a device, an account, a key, a procedure and the people administering them. The journey through this book therefore leads back to a precise description of the proposed capability. The categories of accessible data, activation conditions and versions concerned make a proposal open to examination. The word “access” on its own leaves much of that decision out of view.

Protection first needs following through to the copies. Content can be protected during transmission and then retained on several devices. Backups have their own keys and recovery conditions. Replacing a secret may protect some future exchanges; a readable copy already held elsewhere requires separate assessment. For an organisation, mapping these routes helps with choosing services, preparing recovery and understanding an incident. That map connects a commercial promise to the actual possibilities for reading information.

An authorisation needs a traceable path too. Its purpose, recipients, duration and the operations performed all require records. Audit logs and administrative permissions help determine whether use can be examined afterwards. Supervisors need suitable access, expertise and time. The laws and reports reviewed document those tasks while leaving their performance in particular settings to further evidence. The budget for a measure should therefore include the resources needed for supervision, maintenance and possible correction.

Borders add another set of choices. The same service name can refer to different offerings and contracts. Storage location, control of keys, the operator and applicable law provide complementary information. Buyers need to understand the safeguards when entering a contract and as the service changes. The ability to leave also needs preparation: formats, volumes, expertise and recovery tests. A regulatory cap on particular charges answers a bounded question within that larger operation.

After an intrusion, damage follows the functions actually reached. Hijacked access may expose information or enable actions whose full scope still needs establishing. The cases in this book retain their differences and unresolved questions. Restoring service can consume resources while the fate of copied information remains open. Insurance terms and liability rules subsequently allocate some consequences. Compensation changes who pays; resources already consumed and information already exposed remain part of the underlying harm.

Investigative needs deserve the same serious attention. Another route may deliver useful evidence, require extensive work or arrive too late. Documented operations provide specific observations; extending their results to other systems requires further investigation. Teams need validated tools, procedures and expertise to build a case that can be examined. Assessing alternatives therefore means considering both their usefulness and their capacity for deployment, while retaining the particular value that the content being sought may have.

Numbers must stay attached to the questions they answer. An interception authorisation, an inaccessible device and a solved case are different units. A survey describes views; an economic estimate depends on a comparison scenario. The AES programme study helps explain how such an estimate is constructed, including its assumptions and uncertainty. These documents can inform decisions when their dates and limitations travel with them. Establishing the full cost of an unspecified architecture would require additional evidence, rather than applying an arbitrary calculation to an unrelated total.

The requirement proposed at the end of this book is for a decision that can be reconsidered. Defining the capability, examining its contribution against existing means and funding safeguards prepare a useful evaluation. Results, failures and incidents should then be able to change the initial decision. Correction, suspension and withdrawal are possibilities to organise in advance. Advocates of additional access need to establish its benefits and safeguards; critics need to examine lost information and the real reach of alternatives. The discussion concerns civil liberties, business protection and the resources of justice. It calls for evidence people can understand and institutions capable of examining it.
