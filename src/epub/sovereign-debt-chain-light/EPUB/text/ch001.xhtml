Introduction

One bond, several lives

A government can borrow for decades while a holder of its bonds needs cash before the day is over. Much of public finance takes place between those horizons. The state promises future payments; its creditor arranges the means to wait for them. This book follows those arrangements from the initial sale to the public interventions examined in the final chapter. It is written for readers who want to understand the market behind the announcements of government borrowing.

Who holds sovereign debt? At first the question seems to call for a list of institutions. The public records make their roles just as important as their names. A bank submitting a bid may represent a client. The successful buyer may sell the bonds on. A fund may retain them while borrowing part of the purchase price, and its lender may raise that cash elsewhere. Contracts combine these relationships in different ways. There is no single itinerary shared by every holder. The chapters use documented cases to make particular arrangements intelligible.

Chapter one starts at the auction, where buyers’ bids meet the securities offered by the Treasury. French and US transactions help separate requested quantities, awards and actual settlement. They also introduce a distinction that runs through bond markets: face value describes a repayment commitment, while the price is the consideration accepted today. The rules and dates allow the reader to follow a financing transaction beyond its headline amount, without treating each stage as an interchangeable measure of demand.

Once issued, the bond must be able to circulate. Chapter two examines dealers’ balance sheets, where a bank may agree to buy before finding the next customer. Holding that inventory exposes it to price changes and requires funding. The bank can also sell the securities while continuing to lend to their new holder. An inventory measure captures one part of its presence; financing activity captures another. Regulatory constraints and internal decisions likewise need their own evidence. The chapter asks how much intermediation is available, under which conditions, and to whom.

Chapter three turns to buyers who borrow. It explains the basis trade, linking a bond purchase, a short futures position and funding for the security. The strategy seeks a return on relative prices. A hedge can reduce interest-rate exposure while leaving a need for cash as margin requirements change. The investigated strategies retain their differences: hedge funds do not all follow the same trade, and aggregate futures positions cannot reconstruct every contract in their portfolios. Measuring the cash leg and its financing is part of understanding the position.

Chapter four follows the lenders. A repo provides cash against securities with an agreement to repurchase them. Collateral may be reused where the applicable rights permit it, and the related contracts may mature at different times. One portfolio can therefore connect several private obligations without multiplying the coupons owed by the government. The chapter also examines central clearing, which organises accepted commitments and default management. Securing those transactions does not in itself commit a lender to the next financing agreement.

In chapter five, a contract turns into an immediate deadline: post additional cash or collateral. An asset in a portfolio may take time to sell, transfer or make acceptable to the recipient. The British LDI crisis provides a historical case involving pension strategies designed around future liabilities. Stress exercises then explore possible reactions elsewhere in the system. The evidence retains its status throughout. A scenario can reveal an interaction worth examining without becoming a record of trades that actually occurred.

Chapter six follows the sale into its execution price. Market depth describes how much can be traded on particular terms, and it can matter greatly to a seller who must act quickly. The chapter then connects market conditions to new government issues and the schedule of interest payments. An existing fixed-rate bond keeps the coupon specified by its contract. The effect on the government’s interest bill depends on subsequent borrowing, repayments and the terms of its other debt. Numerical examples make that passage readable while preserving the limits of comparisons between observed prices.

The final chapter examines central banks through their operations and access rules. A secured loan supplies temporary funds to an admitted counterparty; an outright purchase changes who holds the security. The American and British cases, followed by the euro-area framework, show different institutional arrangements. Amounts deployed, risks covered and realised outcomes retain their distinct scope. Whether more recent facilities can prevent particular failures in a future crisis remains an open question. Their existence is documented; their performance in a shock that has yet to happen cannot be.

This edition brings together the seven investigations and their forty figures, with an original introduction and conclusion, reading guidance and an offline glossary. Its documentary cut-off is 12 October 2026. Each chapter retains the date of its investigation and the periods covered by its data. Illustrative transactions remain explicitly hypothetical. The purpose is to give readers practical ways to follow a commitment, find its counterparty and understand the limits of the available records.

The mechanisms and sources behind this synthesis are developed in the seven chapters.
