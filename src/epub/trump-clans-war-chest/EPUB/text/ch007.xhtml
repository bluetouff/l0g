Chapter 6

Trump and the funding that outlasts a shutdown

Published 2026-11-26T20:50:01+01:00

Original online investigation

On February 14, 2026, the Department of Homeland Security lost its regular funding for some operations. Immigration and Customs Enforcement could still draw on money Congress had provided the previous summer for detention. Congressional auditors confirmed that ICE used it. Some employees were furloughed and some bills still went unpaid on time. In April, a law restored annual funding for the department while leaving out ICE and the Border Patrol. The two received separate funding in June. S06 S07 S51

On December 11, stopgap funding for parts of the federal government expires. Without a deal, some services could close or slow down again the next day. Democrats are set to take control of both chambers of Congress in January. They will be able to withhold new funding. To halt spending their predecessors authorized for several years, they may have to win a different vote. S03 S08 S10 S51 S52

The money Congress approved before December

Congress gave ICE $45 billion to expand detention capacity, available through September 30, 2029. That is more than eight months after Trump’s term ends. The department also has other appropriations with different purposes. The June law, for example, funds federal investigations and places limits on its use for immigration and customs enforcement. S04 S05 S06

The law sets an end date for ICE to commit the money. How fast the agency signs contracts determines how long it can keep making new commitments. As of September 30, 2026, ICE reported about $10 billion in obligations against that appropriation. Its plan calls for committing the rest by the end of fiscal 2027. The department says it updated the plan. The Government Accountability Office still wants more realistic assumptions and records that support them. Buying a building is only the start. Someone will have to run it, carry out repairs and keep it in service. S07

In Tacoma, the government bought the detention center that GEO still operates. The property changed hands. The operating contract can generate bills through 2036 under the schedule GEO announced. A program can have most of its money committed while services and payments are still years away. S33 S53

The White House budget office has already moved that timetable. Between October 20 and November 13, it raised the amount ICE can commit for detention facilities on one budget line in fiscal 2027 from $3.2 billion to $7.4 billion. Congress had already authorized the money. The budget office changed its distribution, a step called apportionment. The contracts and payments follow. S56 S57

The Treasury’s September accounts offer a different measure. They show how much remains unpaid on ICE’s operating account for the 2026–2029 funding period. Some of that amount may already be committed to contracts. Judging how much the agency can still commit takes a look at the contracts as well as the outlays. S55

Before the order and after the invoice

Two columns in the White House budget annexes show the difference. Before signing a contract, an agency has budget authority it can use for the purposes set by law. Once it signs, it has an obligation. Months can pass before an invoice comes due. A shipyard, for instance, needs the government to commit to a ship years before delivery. S01 S43

At the end of September 2025, the administration reported about $2.4 trillion in carryover funding that agencies had yet to commit. That historical total includes insurance reserves, dedicated receipts and long-running programs. The mix is where the change shows up. Defense and Homeland Security accounted for nine dollars of every ten in the net increase over September 2024. Each appropriation remains tied to its legal purpose. Moving it to another account requires legal authority. S01 S12 S13

Collecting money and getting to spend it

Nearly every part of this series ran into the same distinction. Customs duties enter federal revenues. Spending from the Treasury requires an appropriation. The debt ceiling sets a limit on federal borrowing, while appropriations determine the spending agencies can commit to. Raising the ceiling can help pay obligations the government has already incurred without opening a new program. S02 S03 S05 S14

The Venezuelan funds illustrate a different kind of power. A January order puts certain Venezuelan funds in US custody and gives the secretary of state authority over distributions. Venezuela retains ownership. Washington controls releases of another government’s money under a regime separate from the federal budget. S20

The Hadrian case shows why government financing can matter to a shareholder. A loan can help a company build a factory before its initial orders bring in cash. The Pentagon confirms early talks and disputes the loan amount reported by ProPublica. The size of Trump Jr.’s fund’s stake and the terms of any loan would determine how large the private benefit could be. S35 S36

The fight over when funding is released

The White House can seek to delay when funds become usable. In September, the administration sent Congress a set of proposed cancellations just days before the funding was due to expire. The GAO concluded that the administration could not withhold the funds through expiration using the statutory window for considering the proposal. The practical question behind the funding freezes is whether an agency can sign in time or loses its authority before lawmakers have had a chance to vote. S15 S16

The documents that govern when money is released matter too. In March 2025, the White House budget office removed its public database showing how it made funds available over time or by activity. It resumed publication after a court ruling. Those records help show whether a restriction comes from law or arises in execution. The audit of DOGE’s access to Treasury payment systems shows why it also pays to check each permission. A staffer was mistakenly given temporary write access to one system. Auditors found no changes made with it. S18 S40 S49 S50 S54

Congress can cut funding it already approved by passing a new law. If Trump vetoes it, an override takes two-thirds in each chamber. Lawmakers can also attach terms to new funding, demand a record of commitments and challenge some spending freezes. If the December talks fail, agencies will have to fall back on the funding and exceptions already available. In January, the new Congress inherits some of the last one’s choices. The measure of Trump’s war chest is how much of his agenda can keep moving while lawmakers negotiate. S03 S05 S06 S07 S08
