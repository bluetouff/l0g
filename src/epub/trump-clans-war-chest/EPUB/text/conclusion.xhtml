The funding the next Congress inherits

In the autumn of 2026, the White House budget office issued two versions of the apportionment for an ICE operating account. Between October 20 and November 13, the amount the agency could commit on one line for detention facilities rose from about $3.2 billion to $7.4 billion. The law that made the money available had passed in 2025. The two apportionments in the final chapter show how the timetable can change after the vote.

That head start matters when regular funding runs out. It can keep contracts moving, with work and payments falling due beyond the current fiscal year. In Tacoma, the government acquired the building and the seller kept the operating contract. The next Congress can examine the cost and demand the records. Reversing spending authority already in law takes new legislation. A presidential veto would make the size of the majority in each chamber decisive.

The private interests call for a different set of records. A sale agreement gives the price the government paid. A company’s books or its share register can show the size of an investor’s stake. In the CoreCivic case, Trump’s disclosure gives the date of the April 13 trade. The order record and the acquisition team’s communications could show who decided to buy and what they knew when the order went through.

The transfer from the Navy account opened this book. The ICE accounts show where to take it next. Look for when an agency can commit the money, then find out whom it owes once the contract is signed. Read together and in time, those records can tell lawmakers which decisions are still theirs and which have already passed to the executive.
