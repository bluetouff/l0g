Chapter 1

Trump and the controls on federal spending

Published 2026-11-26T05:59:21+01:00

Original online investigation

Ten days after Donald Trump took office, a member of the Treasury’s DOGE team sent a spreadsheet to two members of DOGE at the General Services Administration. It listed the names of more than 350 people alongside amounts paid by USAID. The January 30, 2025 email went out without encryption or the Treasury bureau’s prior approval, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) found. The employee had access to systems at the bureau that processes payments for most federal agencies. S01

Less than two months later, the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) took down the public register that shows when federal agencies can put money appropriated by Congress to work. One episode concerns access to payment information. The other concerns public scrutiny of the decisions that shape when money is available to spend. Both sit on the path from a vote in Congress to a payment to a contractor, a grant recipient or a household. S01 S02

The Trump Clan’s War Chest, part 1. Could the White House gain enough discretion over selected spending to ride out a budget fight with Congress? We start by asking who can sign a contract, clear an invoice or delay either one, and under what authority.

From a vote to a bank transfer

The Constitution requires an appropriation made by law before money can be drawn from the Treasury. Once Congress provides budget authority, OMB generally parcels it out by period or program, a process known as apportionment. Agencies may then incur obligations, such as signing a contract, subject to the terms of the appropriation and the apportionment. When payment comes due, the agency certifies it. The Treasury’s Bureau of the Fiscal Service (BFS) processes the certified instructions and sends payment files to the Federal Reserve, which makes the payments. S03 S02 S01

A delay at the beginning of that chain can keep a supplier from getting a contract. A delay at the end can leave the same supplier covering payroll after the work is done. That difference also matters in budget fights between Congress and the White House: money already committed can remain tied up while a decision is pending.

DOGE inside the Treasury

Trump’s January 20, 2025 executive order renamed the United States Digital Service as the United States DOGE Service. It instructed agency heads to set up DOGE teams and give the service access to unclassified records and systems to the extent permitted by law. One project approved at the Treasury was to flag foreign aid payments for State Department review. S04 S01

The GAO reviewed the period from January 20 to April 11, 2025. Its preliminary access findings distinguish between an employee who could ask bureau staff to show them data and another with direct access to three payment systems. The second employee was mistakenly given write access to one system from January 31 to February 1. The auditors found no evidence that the employee changed the data. The file sent to the General Services Administration had already gone out the day before those permissions were granted. S01

The GAO completed its review of security controls and found that BFS had fully implemented only five of the fourteen it selected. The bureau agreed with three recommendations and gave no explicit position on the other three. The GAO reported in September that all six were still awaiting implementation. Its September audit covered six other agencies, four of which documented access to more than 23 systems between them. Three supplied partial information on controls. The records left the scope of individual permissions unresolved. S01 S09

The power to pause a payment

The Treasury team’s assignment to flag foreign aid payments also came up in a lawsuit brought by nineteen states. District Judge Jeannette Vargas distinguished referring a payment for review from blocking it. The complaint identified no specific payment to the states that the process had halted. On September 23, 2026, Vargas dismissed the access claims as moot after the Treasury DOGE team was dissolved. She dismissed the remaining claims because the complaint did not state a legally sufficient claim. S01 S08

On February 26, 2025, Trump ordered agencies to set up centralized systems that record payments under covered contracts, grants, loans and related instruments along with a written explanation. Once the systems were running, agency heads were to require a brief justification before approving covered payments, subject to exceptions. The order also calls for a way to pause and promptly review payments that lack a justification. It excludes direct assistance to individuals and spending in areas including immigration enforcement, the military and intelligence. The exclusions define which spending faces this particular review. Testing their impact requires evidence on actual obligations and payments. S05

OMB and the public record

A binding footnote in an OMB apportionment can make an agency’s access to funds contingent on a condition. In 2019, it used nine apportionment schedules to hold back about $214 million in Defense Department security assistance for Ukraine. The GAO concluded that the policy-driven withholding violated the Impoundment Control Act, the law that governs when an administration can withhold funds Congress has provided. S02 S06

Congress required public disclosure of apportionments for fiscal year 2022 and later made the requirement permanent. OMB took down the public site on March 24, 2025. District Judge Emmet Sullivan ruled on July 21 that the removal violated the statutory disclosure requirement. Publication resumed on August 15. S02 S07

On January 28, 2026, Sullivan ordered OMB to publish spending plans whose terms it had made legally binding through apportionments. The D.C. Circuit heard oral argument in the consolidated appeals on October 13. As of November 26, the court’s published opinions contained no ruling on the merits of that appeal. S11 S12 S13

From claimed savings to money available to spend

On July 7, 2026, DOGE’s Wall of Receipts claimed $110 billion in savings across contracts, grants and leases. The GAO’s August audit found that some estimates were wrong or lacked supporting evidence. For 96 percent of the amount claimed as grant savings, DOGE gave auditors too little information to verify the calculation method. A canceled contract can have a large ceiling and little work left to pay for. The savings depend on the payments actually avoided, taking into account any termination costs or replacement services. S10

Even when ending an obligation frees up budget authority, the agency must still follow the appropriation’s purpose and time limits. An expired fixed-period account can still be used to adjust and pay obligations already incurred and properly chargeable to it. Expiration ordinarily ends the authority to incur new ones. S14

The records show unusual access to Treasury systems, a targeted payment review and a stretch when the public lost sight of OMB’s restrictions. They tell us where the executive can act on the timing of federal spending. Whether the presidency could draw on a reserve of its own remains open. The second story examines what happens to frozen and canceled funding and how transfers have supported detention.
