Introduction

Recovery commits resources to the next step

A car is waiting for its buyer, a supplier for payment, a reader for a document. Completing each transaction involves operations that a cyberattack can interrupt. Opening a building or switching machinery back on leaves further work to do: restoring permissions, reconstructing an order, checking an amount or resuming exchanges with a partner. Wages and payment deadlines continue through that work. This book follows the money and the capabilities needed to make an activity usable again, from the first restored function to the resources required for the next stage.

JLR provides the starting point. On 25 September 2025, the manufacturer announced that its financial wholesale system was back online and that its capacity to invoice had increased. A phased restart of the first manufacturing facilities was subsequently announced for 8 October. Chapter one follows these separate functions within the same business. A vehicle being built, a sale being recorded and cash arriving all contribute to the operation. Their different dates also create different demands on liquidity. A manufacturing announcement therefore leads to further questions about the transactions surrounding the factory.

Chapter two moves into the supplier network. A decline in invoicing can reduce the credit available to a business that borrows against its receivables. Fewer orders can therefore affect both revenue and access to finance. Autins’ disclosures show an instance of this mechanism alongside other pressures on its liquidity. The purchase-order advance programme announced by JLR creates a different route, providing finance before an invoice exists. Its terms describe an option made available to suppliers. Establishing who used it, and how much they received, requires evidence about the programme’s operation.

Insurance introduces another connection between damage and funding. Coverage has to address the relevant event and the direction of the dependency. A customer’s outage can harm a supplier while falling outside a provision designed for disruption at a service provider. Claims then have to be assessed. Vertu’s published accounts allow the recognition of an insurance settlement to be distinguished from its later cash receipt. Chapter three follows that distinction while keeping JLR’s own coverage unresolved. A specimen policy explains contractual questions; it supplies no missing terms of an individual company’s agreement.

The fourth chapter widens the view to the national accounts. Lost revenue, expenditure on repairs and a reduction in value added measure different things. Adding them can count a consequence twice or combine a financial transfer with a resource consumed. The chapter places production statistics, company accounts and the Cyber Monitoring Centre’s estimate alongside one another, retaining their respective periods and methods. A return to a normal production rate describes a flow at a point in time. Measuring accumulated lost output requires the path taken through the interruption and recovery.

The British Library brings the rebuilding work into focus. Its March 2024 incident review describes recoverable copies of collections and metadata alongside infrastructure that needed rebuilding and ageing applications that were difficult to restore. A preserved copy still requires services, validated datasets and reconciled temporary records before it can support a reader. Chapter five examines the funding of that work through accounts, procurement records and response-service terms. Each document illuminates a defined commitment or expense. Together they leave the full price of reconstruction unresolved, including the allocation of internal resources.

Chapter six examines a shared dependency from two distinct settings. CDK’s outage disrupts car dealerships; Synnovis’ disruption affects laboratory testing and its use in hospitals. The activities and consequences remain specific to each case. They raise a related question about contingency arrangements: how much replacement capacity will remain available when several customers seek it at once? Resuming exchanges can also depend on the assurances a user obtains from its partners. A provider’s technical progress and the completion of every customer’s outstanding work need separate observation.

The final chapters connect crisis finance to decisions made before an incident. A British public guarantee shares part of the lending banks’ risk while JLR remains responsible for its debt. Following that commitment requires distinguishing a guarantee, a loan drawdown and money received further down the supply chain. Chapter eight then turns to preparation. TSB’s failed IT migration provides evidence about the limits of tests and exercises. Research on banks and public impact assessments offers ways of examining expenditure, retaining the assumptions and uncertainty attached to their results.

This edition contains all eight investigations, their references, thirty-two figures and a standalone glossary. Its documentary cut-off is 19 October 2026; individual chapters retain the dates of their observations and research. The new opening and closing essays connect the mechanisms without constructing a common cost total for these events. The book follows recovery to a usable operation, then asks which people and organisations advance the resources needed to sustain it.
