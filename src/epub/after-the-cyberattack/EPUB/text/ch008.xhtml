Chapter 7

The public guarantee behind JLR’s recovery

Published: 2026-10-19T12:49:32+02:00 · Updated: 2026-10-19T12:49:32+02:00

After the cyberattack, who pays? · Part 7/8

Britain backed a bank loan to help JLR recover and support its suppliers. Ministerial letters, company accounts and parliamentary scrutiny reveal how risk was shared, which borrowing was used and why the guarantee’s effect on suppliers remains difficult to measure.

On 25 September 2025, Tim Reid wrote to Britain’s business secretary to request approval for support that exceeded his agency’s normal framework. The chief executive of UK Export Finance, UKEF, considered the risk associated with Jaguar Land Rover acceptable and the proposed premium adequate compensation. His concern was the accumulation of commitments: further support for JLR would leave the agency heavily exposed to a single company at a time of considerable uncertainty. He requested a written direction. 1

Peter Kyle signed it the next day. His reply cited the national interest, the manufacturer’s 34,000 direct UK jobs and around 120,000 jobs in its supply chain. He accepted that the transaction could create a contingent public liability of up to £1.5 billion. Final terms, full due diligence and confirmation of Treasury consent were still to follow. Both letters were made public on 27 October. 2 19

The exchange records the point at which the consequences of a cyberattack become a decision about public financial risk. The manufacturer needs liquidity to get through a production stoppage; the minister wants to preserve companies dependent on its orders. Connecting those objectives is a loan guarantee: a commitment by the state to lenders, whose benefits and costs will become visible on different timescales.

The previous instalment on shared providers extends a series that has followed supplier invoices, insurance contracts and the work required to restart operations. Part seven examines the public commitment: the conditions that made it possible, the borrowing actually used and the information the government chose to require when following the beneficiaries.

A company already in the public portfolio

UKEF is Britain’s export credit agency. Its Export Development Guarantee, or EDG, supports bank lending for an exporter’s working capital or investment, covering part of the lenders’ risk. Financing can serve the company’s general needs without being tied to a particular export contract. That flexibility explains how an export finance instrument can be used during an industrial interruption. 3

JLR was familiar with this route. Its annual report records a separate £1 billion UKEF-backed loan, obtained and fully drawn in August 2025. The facility arranged after the cyberattack therefore extended an existing relationship. Further difficulties at the manufacturer could affect several of the agency’s commitments simultaneously. 7

Reid’s letter describes the constraint. The framework agreed with the Treasury governs the risk of an individual transaction and its place in the wider portfolio. A transaction can carry an acceptable level of individual risk while concentrating more exposure on one borrower than the normal rules allow. The test Reid identifies is regularity: compliance with the authorisations governing his actions. The published documents leave the numerical concentration threshold undisclosed. 1

The ministerial direction places responsibility for proceeding in those circumstances with the minister. Kyle adopts a broader objective: preventing the problems of a major exporter from spreading to firms whose survival depends on JLR’s orders. His letter retains financial due diligence and the requirement for final Treasury consent. It combines a political decision about risk with the work needed to establish the financing terms. 2

Method and scope

An acceptable risk on one company can create concentration outside a public agency’s normal criteria. The 25 and 26 September 2025 letters show how that change of scale leads to a ministerial direction and a requirement to document the decision. Parliamentary scrutiny in November accepts the rationale in principle while retaining oversight of the exposure. Fourteen working days is the wording of the committee chair’s letter of 13 November; the official statement of 29 October gives the normal period in sitting days and separates urgency from the notification error (note 23).

The guarantee works through the banks

The government announced the support on 28 September 2025. JLR’s accounts place the agreement of a £1.5 billion facility in October, available to draw in several tranches over two years. Final maturity is October 2030. The banks supply funds when JLR borrows, and the manufacturer incurs a debt it must repay. The government’s commitment changes the risk the banks agree to carry. 4 6 7

The minister’s letter of 13 October describes the allocation: banks retain £300 million of uncovered risk on the £1.5 billion facility. On the loan principal, that means a 20% retained share and 80% covered, equivalent to £1.2 billion of guaranteed principal. Kyle says the commercial rate set by the lenders applies across the whole facility, with UKEF receiving a premium for its risk. Exact pricing and detailed terms remain commercially confidential. 5

For a bank, the guarantee changes the consequences of a repayment default by giving it a claim against the public agency under the contract’s terms. JLR remains responsible for servicing its debt. UKEF’s EDG guidance describes a premium normally taken from loan interest and paid by the lenders, along with a commitment fee on undrawn balances during the availability period. Quantifying those payments for this transaction would require the JLR contract. 3

Another disclosure adds an essential qualification to the £1.2 billion figure. In its register of business supported in 2025–2026, published on 21 July 2026, UKEF lists a maximum liability of £1,467,550,596.94 for JLR’s general corporate purposes. A second entry, also mentioning investment related to electric vehicle manufacturing, records £1,063,189,088. The register provides these limits and descriptions of purpose; matching each entry conclusively to a contract requires further documentation because the rows provide neither a loan identifier nor an individual signing date. 20

UKEF’s glossary defines maximum liability as the maximum claims payable, including interest. An 80% calculation on principal therefore answers a narrower question. The eventual fiscal outcome would depend on guarantee claims, subsequent recoveries and the remuneration received by the agency. Treating those measures as interchangeable would turn a contractual limit into a financial result before the events needed to establish that result had occurred. 21

Method and scope

Banks fund the loan and retain £300m of uncovered risk. The bar divides only the £1.5bn principal: £1.2bn covered and £0.3bn retained by lenders. Maximum liability, including interest, is a different measure. The lower circuit describes premiums and fees in EDG guidance; pricing specific to JLR remains confidential. Arrows represent contractual relationships without claiming actual payments. Calculation on principal: £1,500m − £300m = £1,200m; 1,200 ÷ 1,500 = 80% and 300 ÷ 1,500 = 20%. Bar lengths are proportional to these shares.

Other borrowing is used while the public facility stays available

The accounts then show how financing was used. At the end of December 2025, the new £1.5 billion UKEF facility remained wholly undrawn. It was still undrawn on 31 March and 30 June 2026. These three dated observations show a credit reserve retained while JLR reorganised other borrowing. 10 7 11

A separate £2 billion bridge facility did provide cash during this period. JLR had drawn £500 million by the end of December, followed by another £500 million on 12 January 2026. It then owed £1 billion on the bridge. During April–June, the company entered into a new £2 billion term loan and used the first half to repay the bridge, which was terminated. That £1 billion moved from one borrowing arrangement to another to replace an existing debt. 10 11

The subsequent-events note reports two further £500 million drawdowns on the term loan, in July and August. According to the version consulted for this investigation, the term loan was therefore fully drawn, with maturity in May 2031. These transactions concern a facility separate from the UKEF reserve. Detailed uses beyond the initial refinancing would require additional disclosure. 11

The publication history needs care. The PDF accompanies results announced on 13 August and records authorisation of the accounts on 10 August, while another passage in the same note refers to a balance at 17 August. The precise date of this version remains unresolved. We therefore attribute the July and August events to the document consulted on 19 October, retaining 30 June as the latest verified accounting observation of the UKEF facility. A more recent trading announcement on 3 October concerns sales and provides no updated borrowing position. 11 12

On 9 September 2026, Blair McDougall tells the Commons that the guarantee remains in place and JLR has yet to draw on the facility. He credits it with reassuring the market. This ministerial statement supplies a later indication; the accounts at 30 June remain the latest verified accounting observation. 22

Method and scope

Each block represents £500m of principal. The £1.5bn UKEF-backed reserve remains fully undrawn at the three verified reporting dates. Excluding interest and fees, the £1bn drawn bridge balance is then replaced by a new term loan: this refinancing creates no additional net principal for that £1bn. The consulted accounts separately report two £500m drawdowns in July and August 2026. Their precise use remains to be established. The illustrated accounts stop at 30 June. The ministerial statement of 9 September on the undrawn facility is treated separately in the text (note 22); no balance at 19 October is inferred.

A facility can be useful before it is drawn. In his reply of 7 November 2025, Kyle argues that its availability gives JLR more confidence to spend its existing cash. The mechanism concerns the ability to meet future obligations: access to backup funding can make a company less reluctant to make payments today. The minister leaves drawdown decisions to JLR and its banks, based on commercial need and the extra servicing cost when borrowing begins. 8

That explanation is consistent with the function of a liquidity reserve. Measuring its effect requires identifying payments or decisions that would have been different without it. Drawdowns on the other loans provide an observable financing history; isolating the public guarantee’s contribution takes further evidence on treasury decisions and their consequences for the businesses receiving payments.

Leaving payment decisions with JLR

Why intervene through the manufacturer? On 13 October, Kyle makes the case for speed. The banks and UKEF already know JLR, its credit profile and the previous financing arrangements. Guaranteeing suppliers’ borrowing individually would require examining each business, working with different lenders and checking eligibility for the agency’s products. Some suppliers might fail the credit or export criteria. This is the minister’s rationale; a quantified comparison of timing and alternative options remains to be obtained. 5

The choice also relies on the manufacturer’s knowledge of its supply chain. In his November letter, Kyle acknowledges UKEF’s limited visibility and describes JLR as best placed to identify suppliers’ needs. The agency backs financing upstream; the manufacturer decides how to use its liquidity and which commercial relationships require support. 8

In parallel, JLR introduces a separate programme of up to £500 million, financing as much as 90% of selected purchase orders before invoicing. Part two on supplier credit examined how it works. Its existence explains how a supplier can receive an advance while the £1.5 billion publicly backed facility remains undrawn. JLR’s annual report says the programme has seen limited use and is being wound down, which the manufacturer attributes to the speed of recovery. 7

The parliamentary hearing on 11 November 2025 turns to suppliers further down the chain. The minister expects liquidity received by firms dealing directly with JLR to reach subsequent tiers. Asked how many second- and third-tier suppliers have benefited, he supplies no count. A new order, payment of an old invoice and an advance each affect a small business differently. Following the intervention to that business would require the nature and timing of payments it received. 9

Monitoring exposes a design choice

Kyle’s reply of 19 December, published on 6 January 2026, makes the trade-off explicit. The government says it relies on industry and regional organisations, including the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders and the Welsh Automotive Forum, for information on suppliers further removed from JLR. It describes the chain’s size, complexity and commercial sensitivity as obstacles to monitoring each tier-two or tier-three company directly. Those organisations provide business intelligence, which serves a different purpose from a record of disbursements. 13

The minister also addresses the suggestion of stronger commitments on allocating funds. UKEF can consider conditions suited to individual transactions, he writes, while expressing reservations about routinely imposing firm restrictions, ringfencing or successive disbursement approvals. His reply argues that such requirements would delay execution, complicate use of the credit and increase its cost. This general position leaves the terms specific to JLR open. 13

The difficulty of measuring beneficiaries is therefore part of an acknowledged choice. Leaving liquidity decisions with a company can speed up action because it knows its purchasing needs and payment obligations. For public evaluation, the consequence is having to reconstruct effects through commercial relationships that the agency follows less directly. The usefulness of flexibility and the ability to account for its results become connected questions about the same instrument’s design.

On 17 March 2026, the government answers a written parliamentary question asking what amount and proportion of the guarantee-backed financing have been used in the supply chain. The ministerial response restates the objective of the support and refers to JLR for decisions about use. It leaves the requested value unanswered. That documentary gap prevents the response from supplying a quantified account of how the financing reached suppliers. 14

Method and scope

The financial route runs through JLR, which decides whether to draw credit and how to allocate resources. Government expects support to reach further tiers. Public monitoring described in the letter of 19 December 2025 uses intelligence from industry bodies, distinct from a register of payments by recipient. Arrows show relationships and an expectation, without encoding disbursement amounts.

Parliament examines the consequences of acting quickly

In his letter of 13 November 2025, the Public Accounts Committee chair, Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, accepts the rationale for intervention and considers Managing Public Money requirements met in the circumstances of urgency and a parliamentary recess. He asks for closer adherence to the timetable, which he describes as fourteen working days. 18

The ministerial statement of 29 October specifies fourteen sitting days. It distinguishes urgency preventing that wait from an administrative error delaying the contingent-liability notification document, the Departmental Minute. In his reply of 17 November, Reid dates publication of the letters to 27 October and the laying of the Minute in the Commons to the 29th, then the Lords to the 30th. The Commons record independently confirms its laying there; the Lords date remains attributed here to Reid. His reply corrects the committee chair’s letter, which still described those documents as outstanding. 23 19

The committee chair also reports an expected-loss estimate significantly below the guaranteed amount, without publishing a number. He says the Comptroller and Auditor General will monitor the matter through the audit of UKEF’s accounts. That assessment recognises a financial exposure requiring continued examination even where the guarantee earns a premium and the industrial objective is accepted. 18

The debate later extends beyond JLR. In a report published on 4 February 2026 with the government’s response, the Business and Trade Committee treats the British Steel and JLR directions as a reason to adapt the decision framework to economic resilience. The government defends the existing rules’ ability to accommodate those objectives when proposals are assessed. The disagreement concerns how public intervention is organised; the documents present both institutions’ arguments without measuring the JLR loan’s economic performance. 16

Connecting the risk accepted to the time gained

The available publications define the limits of the assessment. UKEF’s 2025–2026 Impact Report, issued in July, highlights its activity and promises a full Annual Report and Accounts later in the year. The official portal checked on 19 October lists this Impact Report and a Financed Emissions Report among its documents; we did not find the promised full Annual Report and Accounts there. The material examined therefore leaves an audited reconciliation of remuneration, risk, any claims and recoveries for this transaction unresolved. The guarantee register records another dimension: the maximum liabilities declared for the transactions supported. 17 20

Support after the next cyberattack will also require a decision. On 20 April 2026, asked about a formal financing framework for affected companies, the government says it favours a case-by-case approach using existing schemes. The JLR decision is therefore a documented precedent whose criteria and results can be examined when another company seeks support. 15

This investigation follows a reasonably clear chain of responsibility: the agency flags concentrated exposure, the minister adopts the industrial objective, banks make credit available and JLR chooses its payments. It also identifies where measuring outcomes becomes harder, as liquidity moves through relationships between suppliers.

The decisive next evidence would connect decisions enabled by the reserve with payments actually received and production time preserved. For a workshop, being able to retain its workforce and buy materials ahead of renewed orders has a concrete industrial value. Relating that benefit to the guarantee, then comparing it with the risk and the compensation charged, would make it possible to assess the value of the recovery time the government’s commitment was intended to preserve.

The final part on the cost of faster recovery examines TSB’s tests, European bank assessments and the limits of the economic case for preparedness.
